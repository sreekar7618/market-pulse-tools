Sec 2.8

Commodities Markets

Where gold, crude oil, and agricultural produce get traded as standardized contracts, not sacks and drums.

The big picture, first

Think of it like this

A commodity exchange is what you'd get if a mandi (a wholesale produce market) merged with a stock exchange. Instead of a jeweller physically hauling gold bars or a farmer carting wheat sacks to strike every deal, everyone trades standardized paper contracts — a promise to deliver (or receive) a fixed quantity and quality, at a fixed price, on a fixed date.

That standardization is what lets a farmer lock in today's price for a crop that's still in the ground, or a jeweller lock in gold's price six months before a wedding season — without anyone needing to trust a random stranger on the other side of the trade. The exchange and its clearing corporation stand in the middle and guarantee everyone gets paid.

How we got here: a short regulatory history

1952
Forward Contracts (Regulation) Act sets up the Forward Markets Commission (FMC) — a regulator separate from SEBI, just for commodities.
2013
The National Spot Exchange (NSEL) payment crisis exposes serious gaps in commodity market oversight.
2015
FMC is merged into SEBI. One regulator now watches both stocks and commodities, with much tighter surveillance.
2018
SEBI allows stock exchanges to also trade commodity derivatives. BSE and NSE enter a space earlier reserved for dedicated exchanges like MCX and NCDEX.
Memory hook: "2015 — commodities came home to SEBI."

Once inside SEBI's fold, commodity derivative exchanges had to start looking a lot more like stock exchanges, governance-wise. Here's what that means in practice:

How the "spot price" is even discovered

Futures contracts settle against a reference spot price, so getting that spot price right matters enormously. Exchanges use a Spot Price Polling Mechanism — they poll a panel of market participants for the prevailing spot price (some exchanges do this in-house, some outsource it). To keep this honest, exchanges must publish their polling policy and participant details, grow their polling sample in a contract's final 15 days, and review monthly for anyone who habitually polls unrealistic prices.

Which commodities even qualify for derivatives trading?

Not everything can be turned into a futures contract. A commodity needs to clear four tests:

  • Market size — its total annual supply value should be meaningful.
  • Standardizable — it should be homogeneous, or at least gradeable into standard categories (gold can be graded by purity; a random vegetable often can't be).
  • Durable & storable — you need to be able to hold it without it rotting or degrading before delivery. Something like fresh tomatoes fails this test badly; gold or wheat passes easily.
  • Wide distribution — either produced or consumed broadly across the country, not concentrated in one pocket.

Two more things push a commodity towards or away from being derivatives-friendly:

  • Price controls work against it. If the government caps or floors the price (common for food grains and essentials, under laws like the Essential Commodities Act or APMC Act), there's little point discovering a "market price" through futures.
  • Global correlation, seasonality, and volatility work for it. A commodity whose price swings with the global market, moves with the harvest season, or is simply volatile has a genuine hedging need — and that's exactly what a derivatives market is for.

Position limits: Agri vs. Non-Agri (they're opposites)

How big a bet one client or member is allowed to hold differs sharply depending on whether the commodity is agricultural or not — and the two approaches don't just differ in number, they differ in method.

Agricultural commodities — classified first

Every agri commodity gets bucketed using a rolling 5-year average of production and import data:

CategoryWhat puts it there
SensitiveProne to frequent government/external intervention (stock limits, import/export restrictions, other trade barriers) — or has a history of price manipulation in the past 5 years.
BroadNot Sensitive, and average deliverable supply over the past 5 years is at least 10 lakh MT and at least Rs. 5,000 crore in value.
NarrowEverything that's neither Sensitive nor Broad.

Position math for Agri: a client's long positions and short positions across all contracts are not netted against each other — the higher of the two totals is treated as the client's overall open position.

Non-agricultural commodities — a different, netted approach

LevelLimit
ClientLongs and shorts are netted first. Limit = higher of a specified numerical value, or 5% of market-wide open interest.
MemberLimit = higher of 10× the client-level limit, or 20% of market-wide open interest.

A trading member's own proprietary positions are computed and treated exactly like a client's position for this purpose.

Why does Agri get such different treatment from Non-Agri?

Agricultural commodities are far more exposed to government intervention (export bans, stock limits during a bad harvest) and manipulation risk, since supply is seasonal and geographically concentrated. Taking the higher of long/short (instead of netting) is a more conservative way of capping a client's real exposure to that volatility. Non-Agri commodities like metals and energy are typically more globally arbitraged and liquid, so netting is considered safe enough.

Margins: how much collateral is enough?

TermWhat it is
MPOR (Margin Period of Risk)The time it could take to close out a defaulting participant's position. Minimum 2 days for all commodity derivatives, more if the product is less liquid.
Initial Margin (IM) floorA minimum IM applies regardless of what the volatility model says. 5% for Nickel, 4% for everything else.
Extreme Loss Margin (ELM)A flat 1% of gross open position value, collected in real time from the clearing member's liquid assets — on top of IM, as a buffer against tail-risk moves.
Additional MarginExchanges may levy more, at their discretion, in specific situations.

Margin computation happens in two stages: first, an initial margin is computed for each individual contract; then those contract-level margins are applied to the client's actual portfolio of positions to arrive at the final portfolio initial margin. Volatility inputs feeding this (EWMA estimates) are refreshed at least every 2 hours through the trading day, and again at the close.

Worked example — IM floor and ELM
Client's Nickel position value = Rs. 10,00,000
  Min. IM (5% floor) = Rs. 50,000

Same client's Crude Oil position value = Rs. 10,00,000
  Min. IM (4% floor) = Rs. 40,000

Gross open position (all contracts) = Rs. 50,00,000
  ELM (1%) = Rs. 50,000 — collected separately, in real time

Daily settlement: it's all cash, every single day

Open futures positions are marked-to-market and cash-settled daily against the Daily Settlement Price (DSP) — essentially the contract's closing price, usually the last half-hour's weighted average traded price. Gains and losses must be settled in cash before the next day's trading (T+1) begins. If that collection is late, the exchange scales up the initial margin by a factor of √2 to cover the extra risk window.

Theoretical DSP — for contracts not traded in the last half hour
F = S · ert
F = theoretical futures price  ·  S = current spot price  ·  r = financing / cost-of-carry rate  ·  t = time to expiry (years)
This is the same "cost of carry" logic behind every forward price: today's price, grown forward by the cost of holding the asset until settlement.
Worked example — Gold, 15 days to expiry
S = Rs. 68,500 (per 10g) · r = 7% p.a. · t = 15/365 = 0.0411 yr
F = 68,500 × e(0.07 × 0.0411)
F = 68,500 × e0.00288 = 68,500 × 1.00288
F ≈ Rs. 68,697

On expiry, contracts settle against the Final Settlement Price (FSP) — and how that's determined depends on what's being traded:

Commodity typeFSP is based on
Precious & base metalsThe polled price (from the spot price polling mechanism)
Brent CrudeAverage of the five intra-month BFOE (Brent-Forties-Oseberg-Ekofisk) assessments made by ICIS, on the NSE Brent contract's last trading day
Energy (other than crude)Prices from the LME and DGCX, or another source the exchange decides

Sec 2.9

Foreign Exchange Markets

India's forex story is really a 60-year journey from strict control to a market-determined rupee.

The big picture, first

Think of it like this

The forex market is the world's biggest currency mandi — every currency is priced against every other, around the clock. Unlike the stock market, most of it isn't one central exchange floor; it's a network of banks (called Authorized Dealers) dealing directly with each other and their clients (an OTC, or over-the-counter, market), with a smaller slice trading on formal exchanges like NSE and BSE.

The RBI sits above this network as both referee and player — regulating who can deal, and directly managing the country's own foreign exchange reserves.

How we got here: a short regulatory history

1939
Exchange Control introduced under the Defence of India Rules — a wartime, scarcity-driven response.
1947
Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA) gives this control a statutory footing.
1973
A far more comprehensive FERA replaces the 1947 version — RBI (and sometimes the Centre) can now control almost every forex dealing.
1991
Balance-of-payments crisis. Liberalisation begins; the Rupee is devalued in two steps.
1993
FERA amended further to ease restrictions as reforms continue.
1999
FEMA is enacted, replacing FERA entirely — effective from June 1, 2000.
Memory hook: "FERA controlled it, FEMA manages it."

(A useful general distinction worth knowing alongside this: FERA's violations were treated as criminal offences, while FEMA reframed most violations as civil/compoundable — the source text doesn't spell this out explicitly, but it's the standard characterisation of the shift.)

RBI as custodian of the reserves

The Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 makes RBI the custodian of the country's forex reserves and gives it the job of investing them. Reserve management follows a strict priority order:

Safety > Liquidity > Returns

Returns are pursued, but as an "embedded strategy" within a framework built primarily for safety and liquidity — not the other way around.

The RBI Act permits reserves to be invested in:

1Deposits with the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) and other central banks
2Deposits with foreign commercial banks
3Debt instruments of sovereign or sovereign-guaranteed issuers, with residual maturity up to 10 years
4Other instruments/institutions approved by RBI's Central Board
5Certain types of derivatives

How the market is structured today

  • RBI licenses banks and institutions as Authorized Dealers (ADs) — the only entities that can deal in forex on its behalf.
  • Liberalisation has steadily removed licensing and quantitative controls: residents can hold liberal amounts of foreign currency, open forex accounts in India, and remit money abroad each year for a wide range of permissible purposes (travel, education, medical treatment, business, and more).
  • Foreign investors can invest in Indian equities, G-Secs, corporate debt and mutual funds; Indian companies can raise External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs), including Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds (FCCBs) and Foreign Currency Exchangeable Bonds (FCEBs).

Hedging tools available

The Rupee's exchange rate: from peg to market

Post-1947
Fixed rate. Rupee pegged to the Pound Sterling, a legacy of British-era ties.
1971–73
Global Bretton Woods system collapses; most countries move to flexible/managed rates.
Sept 1975
Rupee is de-linked from the Pound Sterling — Britain's shrinking share of India's trade made a single-currency peg unrealistic.
1975–91
Rate managed against an undisclosed basket of major trading-partner currencies.
1991
Two-step devaluation amid the balance-of-payments crisis.
1992
LERMS — a temporary dual exchange rate system, as a bridge.
Mar 1, 1993
LERMS replaced by a single, unified, market-determined rate, based on real demand and supply.

Pricing a currency future

A forward or futures price for a currency pair isn't a forecast — it's simply today's spot rate adjusted for the interest-rate gap between the two currencies, calibrated so that no risk-free arbitrage is possible.

Theoretical currency futures price (continuous compounding)
F₀ = S₀ · e(r − rf)T
F₀ = theoretical futures price  ·  S₀ = spot rate (INR per unit of foreign currency)  ·  r = domestic risk-free rate (MIFOR, or as specified)  ·  rf = foreign risk-free rate (LIBOR, or as specified)  ·  T = time to expiry (years)
Worked example — USD/INR, 3 months to expiry
S₀ = Rs. 83.50 · r (INR) = 6.5% · rf (USD) = 5.0% · T = 3/12 = 0.25 yr
F₀ = 83.50 × e(0.065 − 0.05) × 0.25
F₀ = 83.50 × e0.00375 = 83.50 × 1.00376
F₀ ≈ Rs. 83.81 per USD

Notice INR needed more rupees per dollar in the future than on the spot date. That's not a prediction of rupee weakness — it's mechanical, because INR carries the higher interest rate of the two.

Interest Rate Parity (IRP)

IRP is the same idea stated as a principle: the currency with the higher interest rate must trade at a forward discount against the lower-interest currency, by just enough to cancel out the interest advantage. Otherwise, anyone could borrow the low-rate currency, convert it, invest at the higher rate, and lock in a risk-free profit — which the market won't allow to persist.

Interest Rate Parity
F = S · (1 + ia) / (1 + ib)
F = forward rate  ·  S = spot rate  ·  ia, ib = interest rates of the two countries
Worked example — USD/INR, 1-year horizon
S = Rs. 83.50 · ia (INR) = 6.5% · ib (USD) = 5.0%
F = 83.50 × (1.065 / 1.05) = 83.50 × 1.01429
F ≈ Rs. 84.69 per USD (1-year forward)
Why is this number different from the 3-month example above?

Different horizons, that's all — Rs. 83.81 was a 3-month forward, Rs. 84.69 is a full 1-year forward. The interest-rate gap compounds over a longer period, so the forward premium grows with time. Both examples are really the same formula; this one uses simple annual compounding as the text presents it, while the futures pricing formula above uses continuous compounding — two conventions for the same underlying idea, and it's worth not confusing the two.

Self-test

Check yourself

Six questions mixing both sections. Each reveal explains why the wrong options are wrong too — that's usually where the exam tries to trip you up.

Question 1
Which regulator currently oversees commodity derivatives exchanges in India?
  • A Forward Markets Commission
  • B RBI
  • C SEBI
  • D Ministry of Consumer Affairs
Reveal answer & explanation

Correct: C — SEBI. FMC was merged into SEBI in 2015, precisely to close the gap that let the NSEL crisis happen.

A was correct only before 2015. B confuses this with RBI's role as forex/reserves regulator — a different market entirely. D is linked to commodities historically (via the Essential Commodities Act) but isn't the markets regulator.

Question 2
An agri commodity has no history of government intervention or price manipulation, but its 5-year average deliverable supply is 8 lakh MT and Rs. 4,000 crore. How is it classified?
  • A Sensitive
  • B Broad
  • C Narrow
  • D Ineligible for trading
Reveal answer & explanation

Correct: C — Narrow. It misses both the Broad thresholds (needs ≥10 lakh MT and ≥ Rs. 5,000 Cr), so by elimination it falls into the residual "Narrow" bucket.

A is wrong — nothing here suggests government intervention or manipulation history. B is wrong because it fails the size test. D is a trap: "Narrow" is still a tradeable classification, not a ban.

Question 3
For a Non-Agri commodity, how are a client's long and short positions treated when calculating overall open position?
  • A Netted against each other
  • B Kept separate, higher of the two counted
  • C Simply added together
  • D Ignored below a threshold
Reveal answer & explanation

Correct: A — Netted. This is the opposite of the Agri rule.

B is the trap — that's exactly how Agri commodities are treated, not Non-Agri. C and D aren't supported anywhere in the framework.

Question 4
What is the minimum Initial Margin floor for a Nickel futures position?
  • A 1%
  • B 4%
  • C 5%
  • D 10%
Reveal answer & explanation

Correct: C — 5%. Nickel gets a specifically higher floor than every other commodity.

B (4%) is the floor for all other commodities — a classic swap-the-numbers trap. A (1%) is actually the Extreme Loss Margin rate, not the IM floor. D doesn't appear in the framework at all.

Question 5
In which year was the Indian Rupee de-linked from the Pound Sterling?
  • A 1947
  • B 1975
  • C 1991
  • D 1999
Reveal answer & explanation

Correct: B — 1975.

A (1947) is when FERA was first enacted — an unrelated legal milestone. C (1991) is the balance-of-payments crisis and devaluation, a later and separate event. D (1999) is when FEMA replaced FERA, also unrelated to the currency peg itself.

Question 6
INR interest rates are higher than USD interest rates. What does Interest Rate Parity predict for the forward market?
  • A More INR will be needed to buy one USD forward than on the spot date
  • B Fewer INR will be needed to buy one USD forward
  • C The forward rate will exactly equal the spot rate
  • D It's impossible to say without knowing the spot rate
Reveal answer & explanation

Correct: A. The higher-interest currency (INR here) trades at a forward discount against the other — meaning more of it is needed per unit of the lower-interest currency, exactly cancelling the rate advantage.

B describes the opposite relationship. C ignores the interest-rate gap entirely. D is wrong — IRP tells you the direction of the adjustment from the rate differential alone; you only need the spot rate to get the exact number, not the direction.
