1. Why risk management comes first pp. 10-13

What this tests: the idea that protection is a foundation for every other financial goal, and the scope of the course.

The preface's argument, step by step

  1. Most financial advice is about gain (growing money, reaching goals). This course is about the opposite: the possibility of loss. That guidance is called risk management, and the textbook calls it one of the most important parts of advice.
  2. Almost every goal needs money. Plans assume that cash flows will continue and assets will remain (and ideally grow).
  3. If cash flow stops or assets are destroyed, goals end or must be cut back.
  4. Maslow's hierarchy of needs: the lower levels (physiological and safety/security) must be met before higher ones. In finance, security needs (cash-flow management, risk management) come before higher goals such as retirement saving.
  5. For individuals and businesses, risk management protects against the financial impact of losing assets (property), income, health and life, plus legal liability, which can wipe out both income and assets.
  6. Insurance (risk transfer) is usually the client's primary tool. Most clients already hold some cover, so the advisor's job is to ask: is it the right type, in the right amount, and what happens if we change it?
Maslow levelPersonal needFinancial planning parallel
Higher levelsEsteem, self-actualisationRetirement lifestyle, legacy, philanthropy
Safety / securityProtection from criminals, illness, threatsRisk management, emergency fund
Physiological (base)Food, shelter, protection from the elementsCash-flow management, budgeting

The table's right-hand column is my mapping (Extra); the textbook states the principle that security comes before higher-level goals.

ExampleThe textbook's warning case. A client has saved diligently for retirement and other long-term goals but never managed risk exposures. An uninsured event happens. The savings and investments must be redirected to pay for the damage, and the money for retirement may be lost completely.

(Illustrative, in rupees.) Ravi has built a ₹40 lakh retirement corpus by 40 but has only a small employer health cover. A family member's surgery and ICU stay cost ₹18 lakh. Nearly half his corpus goes, and with it about ten years of compounding.

Course goal p. 10

By the end you should (1) understand the risk management need areas and how to address them, (2) evaluate a client's existing insurance against their needs, and (3) develop strategies to protect the client's financial wellbeing.

Hook"Protect before you project." Lock the foundation (cash flow and protection) before you build the upper floors (investments and retirement).

2. Meaning and treatment of risk (1.1.1) p. 13

What this tests: the textbook definition of risk and the logic of acting on likely losses and on unlikely-but-catastrophic losses.

Risk = the possibility of loss (uncertainty about whether a loss will occur, and perhaps when and how)
Level of riskTextbook examples
Everyday, low-levelIs the food in the fridge safe? Will I get to work on time? Will a branch fall on me or will I twist an ankle?
SubstantiveHarm in a car accident; outliving retirement income; disability ending my lifestyle; damage or burglary at home; being sued for misconduct or liability

"Not whether, but when"

  • For many losses, the better question is when, not whether, the loss will happen.
  • Loss likely soon → act to avoid it or at least minimise the financial exposure.
  • Loss unlikely soon, but financially catastrophic if it happens → do not ignore it; take protective action.
  • Some risks can be handled without insurance; others are best transferred to an insurer. Whatever the risk, management begins with identifying the types of risk and the exposures.

Example(Illustrative.) A 30-year-old's chance of dying this year is small, but if she is the sole earner for two young children the loss would be catastrophic. Low probability does not mean "ignore it". Compare a phone screen crack: likely, but cheap. That one she can simply absorb.

TrapDo not equate "low probability" with "low priority". The textbook explicitly says an unlikely but catastrophic loss should not be ignored. The decision combines likelihood and severity (see the three rules in Section 4 and the severity-frequency grid in Chapter 5, Section 11).

3. The four risk management techniques (1.1.2) pp. 13-14

What this tests: defining and spotting avoidance, minimisation (reduction), transfer and retention in a scenario. Textbook Examples 1 and 2 are built on this.

Risk management is the process of addressing concerns about both insurable and uninsurable risks: the identification, analysis and control of unacceptable risks. Finding the client's risk tolerance is part of data gathering, through insightful questions.

ExampleInconsistent attitudes (textbook). A client may be an avid mountain biker, happy with physical risk, yet have low tolerance for financial risk. Never assume one attitude carries over to another area.

TechniqueTextbook definitionTextbook examplesLimits / notes
AvoidanceNot doing things that expose you to the riskDo not ski, so you cannot hit a tree at 50 km/h. Cho forbids her daughter An to go mountain climbingNot always feasible: giving up driving is unreasonable where there is no practical alternative transport
Minimisation (reduction)Strategies that reduce the likelihood or severity of a lossSki within your ability, wear protective gear; wear a seat belt; check the brakes. An climbs with a guide, protective clothing and good fitnessThe risk remains; only its probability or size falls
TransferOne person or entity takes on the risk for the benefit of anotherA ski-lift ticket says the skier assumes all risks, so the resort transfers risk to the skier. Car insurance transfers damage and liability to an insurer. Aden and Jena buy life cover for their childrenInsurance is the most common method of risk transfer. Transfer can also be by contract (the lift ticket)
RetentionKeeping the risk yourselfSkiing with no life cover, accepting the risk; driving without car insurance (retaining property and liability risks)May be intentional or unintentional. Usually chosen when the loss is unlikely or small

Intentional versus unintentional retention

  • Intentional: the skier understands the risk and decides it is worth it.
  • Unintentional: the same skier does not understand the risk and assumes it without realising.
  • The advisor's job is to turn unintentional retention into proactive, informed choices. Typical gap: clients do not know that disability income insurance exists, or think the odds are too small to bother.

ExampleThe two textbook cases, side by side.

HookA-M-T-R, "A Mountain To Ride": Avoid (don't ride), Minimise (helmet), Transfer (insurance), Retain (ride and accept it). Retention has two faces: chosen or unknown.

Trap(1) "Risk affectation" is not a technique (textbook Example 1); the fourth is avoidance. (2) "Retention is always intentional" is false (Example 2). (3) Swapped definitions are a favourite distractor: "taking on risk for another's benefit" is transfer, not avoidance; "not doing risky things" is avoidance, not transfer. (4) The textbook uses minimisation and reduction as the same thing.

ExtraTechniques combine. A family with a car usually minimises (careful driving, servicing), transfers (motor insurance), and retains the deductible. Chapter 5 audits these combinations (Chapter 5, Section 16).

4. The role of insurance and the three rules of risk management pp. 14-15

What this tests: when insurance is the right tool, why "consider the odds" can argue against insuring, and the three rules (a review question).

When is insurance a good tool?

  • When the likelihood of loss is significant and/or the cost of a loss is substantial, and the risk can be transferred for a relatively affordable premium.
  • Never assume insurance is the only solution. Avoidance, minimisation or retention may suit a client's particular exposure. Other financial tools, such as an emergency fund, also manage loss.
  • Insurance is common in practice because avoiding most risks is neither practical nor desirable: an active, satisfying life means calculated risks.
  • Before insurance, each family bore its own losses: some were lucky, others not. Insurance spreads the loss across society: many small premiums reduce the financial impact on any one person. It protects against many pure risks because it is cheap compared with the potential losses.

Crossing the street: all the techniques in one scene

ActionTechnique
Look up and down the street before crossingReduction
Decide not to cross at allAvoidance
Simply cross and let events happenRetention

The three rules

RuleWhat it means (textbook)Example
1. Consider the oddsWhen a loss is very likely, insuring it may not make sense: insurers price mostly on likelihood, so a high-probability risk can cost almost as much as the loss itself(Illustrative) Insuring a phone you drop monthly would cost nearly the price of repairs
2. Don't risk a lot for a littleCompare the premium with the potential loss. Not insuring a home against catastrophe risks a lot (the home) for a little (the premium)Skipping home cover to save a small premium
3. Don't risk more than you can afford to loseIf the loss would exceed what you could absorb, transfer it. The same home example fits this rule tooA family that could not rebuild without help must insure the house

Hook"Odds, Lot, Afford" = "Only Lucky Amateurs skip insurance." Rule 1 asks how likely; Rules 2 and 3 ask how big compared with the premium and with my pocket.

Trap"Consider the odds" is the confusing one (it is Discussion Question 2). Students read it as "insure the likely losses". The textbook says the reverse: a very likely loss may be too expensive to insure, because the premium approaches the loss. Insurance works best for unlikely but large losses.

ExtraThe three rules map onto the classic severity-frequency grid in Chapter 5, Section 11: high severity and low frequency → transfer; low severity → retain; high severity and high frequency → avoid or reduce.

India"Spread the loss across society" is also how the Indian textbook frames insurance at the economy level: it absorbs individuals' and companies' risks, so they can recover from misfortune and take productive risks such as buying a home or starting a business. Textbook only

Example(Illustrative.) Buying shares of a company is speculative: you may gain or lose. Owning the company's factory building is a pure risk: it either burns or it does not; there is no upside from the fire. Only the factory can be insured.

HookPure = "Pain or nothing". Speculative = "Spin the wheel". Insurers only touch the first.

Textbook gapSection 1.2.3 "Major Types of Speculative Risk" is a heading with no content in your copy (p. 17). The only speculative example the chapter gives is gambling (1.2.1). If asked, common examples outside the textbook are investing in shares, starting a business and gambling.

ExtraWhy is speculative risk uninsurable? An insurer cannot pay you for a loss you chose in hope of a gain without turning insurance into a wager, which breaks the principle of indemnity (Section 14) and the "loss by chance" requirement (Section 10).

HookRisk = "might". Peril = "made it happen". Hazard = "made it more likely".

Example(Illustrative.) A Hyderabad flat burns. Peril: fire. Physical hazard: faulty wiring and stacked paint tins. Morale hazard: the owner never fixed the wiring because "the society policy covers it". Moral hazard: would exist only if the owner started the fire on purpose to claim.

TrapMoral and morale differ by intent. Deliberate deception = moral. Not caring = morale. Fire, hail, theft and illness are perils, never hazards. Impaired driving is classed as a physical hazard in this textbook.

The buying principle

  • Few people can insure against every possibility, and it would not be wise. Think carefully about each purchase.

  • Small losses may be upsetting but rarely cause significant financial harm. Large losses can be devastating.

Hook"Big first, small last." The pure-risk list follows a family's income: Income, Death, Disability, Disaster ("I Don't Dare Delay").

TrapAn option saying "a client should insure every possible loss" is wrong, and so is "small, frequent losses should be insured first". The textbook rule is the reverse: large losses first.

8. Personal risk tolerance and the six-step process (1.3) pp. 17-19

What this tests: the order of the six steps, the three types of risk exposure, and where property, liability and life gaps usually hide.

Why a set process

  • Risk management is complex and its consequences severe, so advisors need a focused, well-organised, consistent process. A defined method reduces the chance of being sidetracked or missing a risk.
  • Data gathering identifies risks; analysis evaluates options; recommendations go to the client. If you sell insurance you may implement directly; if not, you monitor implementation through a third party.
  • Transferring risk exchanges a known premium that is much smaller than a potentially devastating loss. Clients may also retain some risks.

The six steps

#StepWhat happens (textbook)
1Identify risk management goalsLearn what the client wants; define objectives, often in a formal risk management policy (similar to an investment policy statement). Evaluate all exposures
2Collect information to identify risk exposuresAlmost everything about the client: policy checklists, legal documents, risk analysis questionnaires, financial statements, existing policies, tax returns, investment statements, wills and trust documents. Every asset and activity is a potential exposure
3Analyse and evaluate the informationExamine each asset and activity for the three types of exposure (table below)
4Construct a risk management strategyDevelop risk treatment approaches (the techniques), select alternatives, and build the accepted ones into the strategy
5Implement the recommendationsMay mean restructuring existing policies and plans or buying new ones. The client decides, but you guide with action steps
6Monitor for needed changesThe client's situation and the available solutions change. Reviews uncover new or missed exposures, missing information, and actions to modify

Hook"Good Clients Always Choose Insurance Monitoring": Goals, Collect, Analyse, Construct, Implement, Monitor. It starts with Goals; Chapter 5's version starts with Identify the risks (Chapter 5, Section 18).

Three basic types of risk exposure (Step 3)

TypeBasisTextbook examples

2. Liability: contract lawObligations created by an agreement linked to an asset or activityBuying an asset creates liability to a lender; a club membership contract places responsibilities on the client

Example(Illustrative.) Priya buys a car on a loan. Asset exposure: theft or damage, and loss of use while it is repaired. Contract liability: she still owes the bank EMIs even if the car is wrecked. Tort liability: she injures a pedestrian while driving.

9. Underwriting and Lloyd's (1.4.1) p. 20

What this tests: the meaning and origin of "underwriting" (a review question), and why insurers reject some risks.

Origin of the word

Ocean marine insurance emerged in England in the 17th century (1600s). A ship owner listed the ship, its destination, the cargo and other details. Those willing to share the risk wrote their names under the portion of the cargo they would accept, with any conditions. Writing one's name under a risk = underwriting.

Underwriting decides: Is the risk acceptable? → At what price? → On what conditions?
  • Applications go to the insurer for underwriting; on acceptance the insurer issues a policy, completing the process.
  • Insurers do not accept all risks. Some potential costs exceed the insurer's ability to absorb, whatever the premium. Insurers are businesses that must be selective to stay profitable.
  • Seemingly uninsurable risks: a concert pianist's hands or a singer's voice cannot usually get true insurance, but cover may be available through Lloyd's of London or similar associations, whose members evaluate each specific risk and price it on the estimated loss potential. Normal requirements need not apply, but they still follow certain requirements.

HookUnder-write = write your name under the risk. Lloyd's = the specialist for the "one-of-a-kind" (the same point recurs in Chapter 3, Section 1).

TrapUnderwriting is not only accept-or-reject. It also sets the price and the conditions (loadings, exclusions). An option reading "underwriting only decides whether to issue a policy" is incomplete.

10. Requirements for an insurable risk; law of large numbers (1.4.1.1) pp. 20-21

What this tests: the four requirements (a review question), and the difference between catastrophe for a family and for an insurer.

#RequirementWhy the insurer needs itFails when… (Extra examples)
1Law of large numbers must applyMany similar exposure units make losses predictable, so premiums can be pricedOne unique heritage palace
2Loss must be by chance (accidental, fortuitous)Pooling only works for unexpected eventsA planned demolition; arson by the owner
3Loss must be measurable and definablePremiums need measurable losses; the loss cannot be nebulous, it must clearly have occurred"Loss of happiness"

Hook"Large, Lucky, Labelled, Limited": Large numbers, Lucky (by chance), Labelled (measurable), Limited (not catastrophic).

Catastrophic for whom?

IndividualInsurer
Loss of one houseFinancially catastrophic for the familyLittle financial difference
Loss of 10,000 houses—Could be catastrophic, even ending the company's viability

ExampleRobbery in a high-crime area (textbook). A business owner seeks robbery cover. The insurer may refuse, or charge a premium so high it is prohibitive, because the odds are too high. It might cover at an exorbitant premium; more likely it will decline.

Law of large numbers

  • With enough similar (homogeneous) exposure units, losses for the group become somewhat predictable. No one can say when one house will burn, but an insurer covering 20,000 houses can estimate how many will burn in a period.
  • Expected losses become the base for premiums (plus administrative expenses and other loadings). Underwriting also weighs construction quality, location and local claims history.
  • Measurability and the large numbers rule help keep the insurer solvent, and you want your insurer alive long enough to pay your claim.

Example(Illustrative.) An insurer covers 20,000 similar homes worth an average ₹50 lakh to rebuild. Past data shows about 1 in 1,000 is destroyed by fire each year: about 20 homes, or ₹10 crore in claims. Pure premium per home = ₹10 crore ÷ 20,000 = ₹5,000, before expenses and profit. With only 20 homes, the same 1-in-1,000 rate would be useless: one fire could wipe out years of premium.

Trap"Not catastrophic" is judged from the insurer's view, not the client's. A loss that would ruin a family (one house) is exactly what insurance is for.

IndiaInsurers handle a concentration of risk with reinsurance. The Indian textbook notes that for very large corporate risks, insurers may participate together or seek reinsurance when taking the cover alone. Textbook only

11. Structure and characteristics of insurance contracts pp. 21-22, 26-27

What this tests: the four contract characteristics, and why only the insurer is bound (Discussion Question 6).

An insurance policy is an agreement, generally a standard form contract, between insurer and policyowner that sets out the claims the insurer must legally pay. In exchange for the premium (consideration), the insurer promises to pay for losses caused by covered perils.

CharacteristicMeaning (textbook)Consequence
AleatoryOutcome depends on chance; the parties' financial participation is substantially unequalOne small life premium can trigger a large death claim
AdhesionThe insurer drafts the contract and is "stuck" with its terms; the applicant can only accept or refuse, not counter-offerRead the contract carefully: the other side wrote every rule. (Ambiguity is generally read against the drafter: Extra)
UnilateralOnly the insurer is legally bound to perform (in most jurisdictions)The insured must meet conditions to be paid, but cannot be forced to keep the policy
ConditionalConditions must be followed for the policy to payE.g., provide an inventory of household items before a contents claim is paid

Hook"A-A-U-C: All Agreements Uphold Conditions." Aleatory = alea, Latin for dice (chance). Adhesion = the insurer is glued to its own words. Unilateral = uni, one party bound. Conditional = "pay if you play by the rules".

TrapUnilateral versus conditional. Unilateral: only the insurer can be forced to perform. Conditional: the insured must meet conditions to collect. Both are true at once: the insured has no duty to continue, but if they want a claim paid they must follow the rules. Do not answer "the insured is not bound by anything".

Example(Illustrative.) Meena stops paying her motor premium. The insurer cannot sue her for the premium (unilateral), but her cover lapses. If she renews and later fails to report an accident within the time limit, the claim may be refused (conditional).

12. Valid contract, consideration, offer and acceptance (1.4.1.2) pp. 22, 25

What this tests: the five requirements of a valid contract and the meaning of consideration.

#A legally valid contract must…Plain meaning
1Pertain to a legal activityThe purpose must be lawful
2Be valid in its jurisdictionMeets local law
3Be between legal partiesThose able to contract (e.g., of age, sound mind)
4Have offer and acceptanceOne party offers, the other accepts

Hook"Lawful Locals Like Offers with Cash" (L-L-L-O-C): Legal activity, Local validity, Legal parties, Offer and acceptance, Consideration.

Consideration

  • A promise by one person to do something for another. Even a simple deal (cleaning a house for a fee) is a contract.
  • Consideration = the amount paid for goods or services, or some performance. Money is not essential.
  • In insurance: the insurer offers cover at a price (premium); the applicant agrees to the terms and pays (acceptance).

ExampleSaura and Kadar (textbook). Saura, a contractor, helps her neighbour Kadar find good suppliers for his remodelling; Kadar watches her home while she is on holiday. Each side's consideration is the other's performance: supplier information for home-watching. No money changes hands, yet it is a contract.

Trap"A contract requires a monetary payment" is false: consideration can be performance. And remember that in insurance the premium is the insured's consideration; the insurer's is its promise to pay covered losses.

13. The four key concepts; insurable interest pp. 22-23

What this tests: the four concepts that make an insurance policy operate, and insurable interest (a review question).

Hook"I In U S": Insurable interest, Indemnity, Utmost good faith, Subrogation. Insurance is "in us", the four pillars.

Insurable interest

  • Definition: the legal or equitable interest held by the insured in the insured property or life.
  • Economic view: it exists where the insured suffers a monetary or economic loss through damage or destruction of the subject matter, i.e., the person would pay or lose money, measurable in economic terms.
  • Creditor: a creditor who insures a debtor's property has neither a legal nor an equitable interest in it, but would still suffer an economic loss if it were destroyed, so insurable interest exists.
  • Employer and key employee: the employer has an insurable interest in the life of a key employee (death or permanent incapacity), because of the cost of finding and training a replacement.

When must it exist?

Common law, general insuranceBoth when entering the contract and at the time of loss
Some jurisdictionsConsumer legislation has removed the need to hold it at inception for property

Change of insurable interest

  • Some jurisdictions' insurers require notification if the nature of the interest changes.
  • If the policy has no notification clause, it stays valid without notice.
  • But at renewal the insured must disclose relevant changes, because renewal usually creates a new contract: every material fact arising since inception must be disclosed.

Example(Illustrative.) Arjun sells his flat in June but his home policy runs to December. If the flat burns in August, he has lost nothing, so he has no insurable interest at the time of loss and no claim under common law. 14. The principle of indemnity; valued policies p. 23

What this tests: "whole, not better than whole", its two exceptions (life and valued policies), and how replacement-value policies can revert to indemnity.

Indemnity: restore the insured to the position just before the loss — never better. Claim ≤ actual loss ≤ sum insured ≤ insurable interest
  • The insured can buy cover up to the extent of their insurable interest and cannot claim more than the loss.
  • Textbook example: house insured for $400,000 against fire, actually worth $500,000, destroyed. Recovery is $400,000 (the sum insured caps the claim). Recovering more than the loss would be a wager (speculation), against the nature of insurance.
  • Replacement value policies exist in some jurisdictions. The value is measured at the time of loss, so without regular inflation adjustments the sum insured may fall short. Conditions may say that underinsurance makes the policy revert to an indemnity basis (rather than replacement), which can mean a severe loss for the insured.

Where indemnity does not apply

ExceptionWhyWhat is paid
Life insuranceA contingent contract: the claim arises on a contingency (death). A human life cannot be valued like propertyThe face amount (death benefit)
Valued policiesInsurer and insured agree the value in advance, almost always with an appraisalThe agreed value, whatever the actual value at loss

ExampleThe gold pendant (textbook). Valued and insured at $2,000 two years ago. Gold rises, so the pendant is worth up to $3,000 when stolen: the insurer pays the agreed $2,000. If its value had fallen to $1,000, the insurer would still pay $2,000.

Hook"Back to zero, not into profit." Exceptions: "Lives and Labels": Life (contingent) and Labelled values (valued policies).

Trap(1) "Indemnity applies to all insurance" is wrong: not to life or valued policies. (2) In a valued policy the insurer pays the agreed value even if the item lost value; students assume indemnity would cut it. (3) The sum insured is a ceiling, not an entitlement: a $400,000 policy on a $500,000 house pays $400,000.

15. Utmost good faith and the duty of disclosure pp. 23-25

What this tests: who owes the duty, when (pre-contract, renewal, claims), what counts as material, and the life-insurance time limit.

Utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei)

  • Applies to potential insureds, current insureds and insurers. It means the highest degree of honesty and sincerity.
  • Pre-contractual duty: the most common form is the duty to disclose. The insured knows most about the risk, so must disclose every fact that might influence the underwriter's decision to accept, or to accept on altered terms.
  • Post-contractual duty: continues through the policy period, mostly around claims: how the insured makes a claim and how the insurer handles it. An insurer that deliberately delays a valid claim, hoping the insured gives up, is not acting in good faith.
  • In some jurisdictions simple mistakes are not a breach: good faith implies intention. Fraudulent claims and insurer delay tactics both breach it.

Duty of disclosure

  • Common law: each party must voluntarily disclose facts that might be material during negotiations. Failure to disclose a material fact lets the innocent party void the contract from the beginning.
  • The burden falls heavily on the insured, who knows the facts. Partners example: one partner signs the proposal; other partners knew facts that would have influenced the insurer. Their non-disclosure will likely let the insurer void the policy.
  • Poorly drafted questions make disclosure hard. Some jurisdictions now require insurers to specifically ask for the information they need, since insurers know best what they require.

16. Subrogation p. 25

What this tests: what subrogation is and how it extends indemnity (a review question).

Insurer pays the insured → insurer "steps into the insured's shoes" → sues the person who caused the loss → recovers what it paid
  • Technical definition: after settling the insured's claim, the insurer may sue the person who caused the covered harm to recover the amount paid.
  • It is an extension of indemnity: it ensures the overall payment does not exceed the loss (the insured cannot collect from both the insurer and the wrongdoer).

Example(Illustrative.) A truck driver rear-ends Neha's car, causing ₹1.2 lakh of damage. Her insurer pays her ₹1.2 lakh (less any deductible). The insurer then pursues the truck driver (or his insurer) for the amount. Neha cannot also sue the driver for the same ₹1.2 lakh: that would make her better off than before.

HookSubrogation = "substitute in the lawsuit". Indemnity says "not better than whole"; subrogation enforces it by stopping a double recovery.

TrapSubrogation happens after the insurer pays. It does not apply to life insurance (not indemnity). And it is the insurer who sues the third party, not the insured suing the insurer.

IndiaThe Indian textbook's motor total-loss procedure: the vehicle's keys are handed to the insurer and a letter of subrogation and indemnity, on notarised stamp paper, is submitted to settle the claim. Textbook only

17. Claims: notice and proof of loss pp. 25-26

What this tests: the claims process, and who must prove what.

  • Claims payment and loss handling is the "litmus test" of insurance's value: it is the actual "product" paid for.
  • Process: the insured notifies the insurer, broker or agent and completes the claim form. Some jurisdictions allow small home or contents claims by phone or online without a form.
  • Notice of loss: policies require notice within a specified time ("immediately", "promptly", or a set number of days). Notice lets the insurer investigate and reduce its loss (suing negligent parties, arranging salvage).
  • Life claims: doctors' reports, death certificates and similar are needed before the insurer admits liability.
  • Misuse of notice clauses: under common law insurers have sometimes used late notice to deny legitimate claims. Some jurisdictions now bar this but let insurers reduce the claim by the harm suffered (usually extra costs caused by the delay).
  • Proof of loss: most policies require "satisfactory" proof (valuation certificates, purchase receipts, wage statements). It may be a condition of recovery. The insured must also show that the loss falls within the policy terms.
  • Burden of exclusions: if the insurer will not pay, it must prove the loss is not covered because of an exclusion.
Who provesWhat
InsuredThat a loss happened, its size (proof of loss), and that it falls within the policy's cover
InsurerThat an exclusion applies (or that the insured breached the policy: see Section 18)

18. Proximate cause p. 26

What this tests: following a chain of events to decide whether a loss is covered.

  • Often a chain of events, not a single cause, leads to a loss. Examine the chain for the proximate (immediate, dominant) cause and whether it is excluded.
  • If an insured peril directly causes the loss, it is covered, provided no uncovered peril effectively interrupted the chain.
  • The final loss need not be the direct result of an insured peril, as long as the chain was proximately started by one.
  • Textbook example: fire policies do not mention water or smoke damage, but if the fire's cause is not an uncovered peril, water and smoke damage from firefighting are deemed fire damage.

Three classes of peril

ClassMeaning
Insured perilsNamed in the policy as insured
Excepted (uncovered) perilsNamed as excluded, either as causes of insured perils or as their results or consequences
Other perilsNot mentioned at all, but may form part of the chain

Shifting burden of proof

  1. The insured proves the cause of loss is covered.
  2. The onus moves to the insurer to prove a breach of the policy by the insured.
  3. If the insurer claims a breach, the onus moves back to the insured to prove the act or omission did not cause or contribute to the loss, wholly or partly.

Example(Illustrative.) Lightning (insured) strikes a house and starts a fire (insured); firefighters' water ruins the ground floor (not mentioned: an "other" peril). The chain started with an insured peril and nothing excluded broke it, so the water damage is treated as fire damage and paid. Contrast: an earthquake (excluded in the Chapter 3 homeowners composite) cracks walls; that cracking is not paid, though a fire it causes may be (concurrent causation).

Hook"Follow the dominoes back to the first one that matters." If the first domino is insured and no excluded domino breaks the line, everything that falls is covered.

Trap"Water damage is not listed in a fire policy, so it is not covered" is the classic wrong answer. Coverage depends on the chain, not just the last event.

20. Exclusion clauses, fraudulent claims and loss by own act p. 28

What this tests: how exclusions define cover, the causation limit on exclusions, and deliberate losses.

Exclusion clauses

  • Exclusions (exception clauses) define the boundaries of cover. Some are common to most policies (war, riots, civil commotion, radiation); others are specific to the type of cover.
  • Some jurisdictions stop insurers relying on an exclusion for a pre-existing defect or illness that the insured did not know about at inception.
  • Causation rule: if an exclusion says the insurer is not liable for some act or omission, the insurer cannot refuse the claim if that act or omission did not cause or contribute to the loss.

ExampleBald tyres (textbook). Cars must be roadworthy. An insured car with bald tyres is rear-ended at a traffic light: the tyres played no part, so the insurer cannot deny the claim. The same car slides off a wet road because of the bald tyres: now the insurer can rely on the exclusion.

Hook"No link, no excuse." An exclusion bites only when the excluded thing helped cause the loss.

Fraudulent claims

  • Insurance fraud usually means manipulating a claim, sometimes fabricating it entirely, even deliberately damaging the property.
  • Fraud is costly, and the cost ends up in everyone's premiums.

Loss by own act

  • Insurance exists for fortuitous, unexpected losses. Pooling, calculability and fair premiums all rely on that presumption.
  • A loss caused intentionally to make a claim is not recoverable: deliberately setting fire to your own house is fraud.
  • Jointly owned property: in some jurisdictions an innocent co-insured can still recover their share when the other co-owner commits arson. Not everywhere: an aggrieved spouse burning jointly owned property may leave the innocent spouse with nothing.

TrapThe innocent co-insured rule varies by jurisdiction. "The innocent spouse is always paid" and "is never paid" are both wrong as general statements.

22. Underinsurance and the coinsurance (average) clause pp. 29-30

  • Premiums assume people insure for full value. Underinsured property means the insurer collects too little premium to keep the pool viable.

24. Chapter 1 practice questions

Try each first. Questions are mine unless marked "textbook".

Q1. (Textbook Example 1) Which of the following is least likely a technique used to manage risk? (a) Risk minimisation (b) Risk transfer (c) Risk affectation (d) Risk retention

Answer

(c). "Risk affectation" is not a technique. The fourth technique is avoidance.

Q2. A client installs a burglar alarm and also buys a home contents policy with a ₹10,000 deductible. Name every technique in use.

Answer

Minimisation (alarm), transfer (policy) and retention (the deductible). Techniques usually work together.

Q3. A friend borrows Sunil's bike and leaves it unlocked outside a mall because "Sunil has insurance". Identify the hazard.

Answer

Morale hazard: carelessness or indifference because of insurance. It would be moral hazard only if someone faked a theft to claim.

Q4. Why would an insurer refuse to cover every house in a single coastal town against cyclone damage, even though it happily insures single houses?

Answer

Requirement 4: the loss must not be catastrophic to the insurer. One house is a manageable loss; thousands destroyed by one storm could threaten its viability. The concentration also undermines the law of large numbers, because the units are not independent.

Q5. An antique painting is insured on a valued basis at ₹5 lakh. It is stolen when its market value is ₹7 lakh. What does the insurer pay, and what if the market value had fallen to ₹3 lakh?

Answer

₹5 lakh in both cases. A valued policy pays the agreed value regardless of actual value at loss; it is an exception to indemnity.

Q6. A home with replacement value of ₹80 lakh is insured for ₹48 lakh. The policy requires 80% insurance to value. A kitchen fire causes ₹10 lakh of damage; the deductible is ₹25,000. What is paid?

Answer

Required = 80% × 80 = ₹64 lakh. Ratio = 48 ÷ 64 = 0.75. 0.75 × ₹10,00,000 = ₹7,50,000. Less deductible = ₹7,25,000.

Q7. A cyclist runs a red light and hits Rekha's parked scooter. Her insurer pays ₹40,000 for repairs. What can the insurer do next, and can Rekha also recover ₹40,000 from the cyclist?

Answer

The insurer can use subrogation to recover the ₹40,000 from the cyclist. Rekha cannot recover the same ₹40,000 again: indemnity forbids profit. (She may pursue any loss the insurer did not pay, such as her deductible.)

Q8. Lightning strikes a warehouse and starts a fire. Firefighters' water ruins stock on the floor below. The fire policy does not mention water. Is the water damage covered?

Answer

Yes. Proximate cause: the chain began with an insured peril and no uncovered peril broke it, so water and smoke damage from firefighting count as fire damage.

Q9. Motor policies require roadworthy vehicles. A car with worn brakes is hit from behind while parked. Can the insurer refuse the claim on the roadworthiness exclusion?

Answer

No. The worn brakes did not cause or contribute to the loss (the same logic as the textbook's bald tyres). If the car had crashed because it could not stop, the exclusion could apply.

Q10. In a life insurance proposal, Farah does not mention a past heart operation. Which doctrine is breached, what is the common-law remedy, and how does India's Sec 45 change the picture after year 3?

Answer

Utmost good faith (duty of disclosure). Common law lets the insurer void the contract from inception. Under Sec 45 of India's Insurance Act, no life policy can be called in question on any ground after three years from the policy date (or revival).

25. Chapter 1 review and discussion questions pp. 32-33, 163-165

Review questions

1. What are the four primary risk management techniques?

Answer

Avoidance; minimisation or reduction; transfer; retention.

2. What are the three rules of risk management?

Answer

Consider the odds; don't risk a lot for a little; don't risk more than you can afford to lose.

3. How would you define risk, peril and hazard?

Answer

Risk is the possibility of loss; perils are the causes of loss; hazards increase the chance of loss by a peril.

4. What is underwriting and how does it relate to insurance?

Answer

It decides whether a risk is reasonable to accept, at what price and on what conditions. Applications go to underwriting; on acceptance the insurer issues a policy and completes the process.

5. What are the requirements for an insurable risk?

Answer

Law of large numbers applies; loss by chance (accidental, fortuitous); loss measurable and definable; loss not financially catastrophic.

6. How does a loss catastrophic for an individual differ from one catastrophic to an insurer?

Answer

One house can ruin a family but barely affects an insurer; 10,000 houses could end the insurer's viability.

7. How would you describe insurable interest?

Answer

The legal or equitable interest the insured holds in the insured property or life; economically, the insured would suffer a measurable monetary loss from damage or destruction of the subject matter.

8. What is the principle of indemnity and how does it apply?

Answer

Policies restore the insured to the pre-loss position, not better. Cover is limited to insurable interest, and the insured cannot claim more than the loss.

9. What is subrogation and how does it relate to insurance?

Answer

After paying, the insurer may sue the person who caused the loss to recover its payment. It extends indemnity by ensuring total payment does not exceed the loss.

10. What are riders and endorsements and how do they differ?

Answer

They modify coverage or terms. They essentially describe the same thing; some policies say rider, others endorsement.

11. Why is underinsurance a problem for insurers, and how do they compensate?

Answer

Premiums assume full-value insurance, so underinsurance starves the pool. Policies carry an average or coinsurance clause: only total losses are paid at the insured value; for partial losses the underinsured bear a pro-rata share.

1. Introduction: which risks matter to an advisor p. 34

What this tests: the scope of "risk exposures", including liability that arises without any action by the client.

  • People face risks daily: eating, going to work, travelling, crossing the street. Some have little lasting effect; others can change a life.
  • Advisors care most about risks with financial implications: lifestyle and health issues; loss to a house, car or other personal property; liability exposures; and loss of life.
  • Some liability exposures need no action by the individual. Textbook example: a tree on your property falls onto a neighbour's house.
  • Minor exposures can often be handled by lifestyle changes. Substantive risks need substantive measures, including insurance to transfer the financial impact to a third party.

Example(Illustrative.) A coconut tree in Lakshmi's compound falls during a storm and smashes the neighbour's car. She did nothing that day, yet she may face a liability claim as the owner. This is why liability cover (Coverage E, Chapter 3, Section 2) matters even to careful people.

Hook"Small risk, small fix; big risk, big tool." Lifestyle change for the minor ones; insurance for the substantive ones.

2. Financial obligations: existing and potential (2.1) p. 34

What this tests: linking "how much loss can I bear?" to the decision to transfer.

  • A person should ask how much financial loss they can reasonably sustain, i.e., the damage they are capable of incurring or willing to incur.
  • When the potential loss gets too high, the rule "don't risk more than you can afford to lose" applies (Chapter 1, Section 4).
  • Any loss with too high a financial cost is a candidate for risk transfer (insurance).
Can I absorb this loss without derailing my goals?   Yes → consider retaining  |  No → candidate for transfer

Example(Illustrative.) Arvind has a ₹6 lakh emergency fund. A ₹15,000 scooter repair: he can absorb it, so retaining is reasonable. A ₹20 lakh hospital bill or a ₹1 crore liability claim: he cannot, so those are candidates for transfer.

Extra"Capable" and "willing" are different. A wealthy client may be able to absorb a ₹10 lakh loss but unwilling to. That is risk tolerance (Chapter 1, Section 3), and it legitimately changes the answer.

5. Health issues and the "buy before the event" rule pp. 35-36

What this tests: why insurance must be in place before the loss, and why people put it off (Review Question 3).

  • Health and lifestyle often go together, but people have less choice over health: many conditions are unrelated to personal choices.
  • The real concern is preparing for and mitigating the financial loss when a health event happens. Some events cause hundreds of thousands of dollars of loss through medical costs and lost income from disability.
  • Appropriate insurance, plus government benefits, can minimise the damage.
  • Why people buy insurance (textbook): insurance does not prevent events, just as life insurance does not prevent death. People buy it to protect against financial loss from a covered event.
You cannot insure a loss after it happens ⇒ you cannot get proper medical cover for a condition once it is diagnosed ⇒ buy BEFORE the event

ExampleHuman nature (textbook). People don't want an umbrella until it starts to rain. They don't lock the car until something has been stolen. After the event, it is too late. The advisor's job is to discuss these risks and encourage action before the event.

Hook"Buy the umbrella while the sky is blue."

TrapReview Question 3's answer is not "to prevent losses". Insurance does not prevent events; it protects against the financial loss from them.

Q1. (Textbook Example 2) Which statement is most likely correct? (a) Risk avoidance occurs when one entity takes on the risk for another. Q2. A 62-year-old retiree's main worry is that her corpus will run out if she lives to 95. Which risk is this, and what is its mirror image?

Answer

Longevity risk (outliving retirement money). Its mirror is premature death. Life insurance protects against the second, annuities against the first.

Q3. A client with no health cover is diagnosed with a heart condition and now wants a policy that pays for its treatment immediately. What principle from Chapter 2 does this illustrate?

Answer

You cannot insure a loss after it happens, or get proper medical cover for a condition already diagnosed. Insurance must be bought before the event. (In India, a pre-existing condition may be covered after a waiting period of up to 3 years.)

Answer

In one line: you pay a small, certain amount (premium) so that the insurer takes on a large, uncertain loss. You are moving the risk to the insurer and being brought back "towards wholeness" after a covered loss.

Not every risk is insurable

  • Underwriting decides whether a risk is accepted.
  • A risk that is too big fails the rules of acceptable risk (the risk cannot be catastrophic). Chapter example: homes in areas prone to severe storms.
  • Other risks fail the law of large numbers: you need many similar exposure units to predict losses.
  • Lloyd's associations often cover singular (one-of-a-kind) risks that traditional insurers will not, because syndicates of specialists provide the funding. History: Edward Lloyd's Coffee House, Tower Street, London, first mentioned in 1688. It specialised in shipping information, which led to marine insurance.
  • Monoline cover = one specific risk or peril. Multi-line / package = several covers in one policy (today's norm).

ExampleA unique heritage mansion has no pool of "similar houses", so a standard insurer may decline it (fails the law of large numbers). A Lloyd's syndicate of specialists can price it. A coastal town that floods every monsoon is a catastrophic concentration, so a standard insurer may refuse the whole area.

HookLloyd's = the odd-one-out insurer. Unique risk, syndicate of specialists. Monoline = "mono" = one peril. Package = many covers in one box.

Property insurance basics pp. 42-43

  • "Property" here means real estate: homes, outbuildings, warehouses, offices, condos, townhouses, apartment buildings.
  • Insurance covers the buildings, not the land.
  • Commercial property needs specialised insurance, so the chapter focuses on personal property.
  • Insurance policies are legal contracts and vary by jurisdiction. The chapter gives a composite of standard cover found in many territories.

ExampleA residence is valued at $500,000, of which $100,000 is land. Cover of $400,000 is full insurance, because the missing $100,000 is the land, which cannot be insured.

4. How much cover? Values, replacement cost and coinsurance pp. 45-48

What this tests: choosing the right value to insure, and the coinsurance penalty calculation, the most calculation-heavy topic in this chapter.

Three values of a home

ExampleTwo near-identical houses stand in the same city. One is in a prestigious locality and sells for far more. Rebuilding both costs about the same, so both need similar insurance. Insure on replacement cost, never market value.

HookM-A-R: Market = what the buyer pays; Assessed = what the taxman says; Replacement = what the builder charges. Insure the builder's bill.

How much to buy

  • Minimum sensible level: replacement-cost cover of at least 80% of the cost to rebuild.
  • More thorough: insure to 100% of replacement value, which protects you if the home is completely destroyed.
  • Replacement cost depends on age, construction materials, location, square footage and number of rooms. Check that additions, improvements and extra outbuildings are counted. Other structures are normally covered up to 10% of the dwelling cost, but detached garages or gazebos with hot tubs may need more.
  • Replacement value is measured at the time of loss, not when the policy was bought. Inflation guard endorsements and annual agent reviews exist for this reason.

Why coinsurance exists

Premiums are priced per unit of cover (e.g. $3.50 per $1,000), assuming people insure close to full value. Most losses are partial, so people are tempted to insure at 50%. If everyone did, the premiums collected would not pay the claims. So policies require insurance of at least 80% of replacement cost for partial losses to be paid in full.

Payment = (Amount of insurance carried ÷ Amount of insurance required) × Loss − Deductible
VariableMeaning
Amount carriedThe dwelling limit you actually bought
Amount required80% × replacement cost at time of loss
LossSize of the partial loss
DeductibleSubtracted after the ratio is applied

HookSay it aloud: "Carried over Required, times Loss, minus Deductible." And: carry 80 or share the pain.

Rules to apply in order

  1. Required amount = 80% × replacement cost at time of loss.
  2. If carried ≥ required: the ratio is 1, so pay Loss − Deductible (never above the policy limit).
  3. If carried < required: pay (Carried ÷ Required) × Loss − Deductible. The chapter says the insurer pays the formula result or the actual cash value, whichever is greater, and that the formula usually gives the higher benefit.
  4. The policy limit is the ceiling, even if the home meets 80% of replacement cost. A rider (e.g. 115% or 125% of replacement value) can lift this.

Why "inland marine"? (origin story)

Early property insurance covered ocean cargo, but only while on the ship. Cargo moved inland on barges, floated up rivers, so "inland marine" covered the "floated" leg. When trains and trucks replaced barges the name stuck, and the cover grew to any personal property in transit or portable.

Defence and settlement

Historically the insured could force the insurer to fight claims in court, because settlement was seen as an admission of guilt. Today liability cases are viewed in economic terms. If settling costs less than fighting (even if the insured could win), the insurer generally settles out of court, and few policies let the insured prevent it.

  • Life insurance is unique because everyone eventually dies, so every policy still in force pays a death benefit.
  • Insurers can take this on because aggregate mortality is predictable: they know roughly how many people in a group die each year, even if they cannot know who.

Term life insurance

  • Covers the insured for a specified period (term).
  • Originally the only product was annually renewable term: premium buys one year of cover, renewable each year at a higher premium as the insured ages.
  • Usually renewable only to about age 90, and renewal premiums become exorbitant in the 60s and 70s, so most policyowners let term cover lapse then.
  • Lapse is not a failure: term cover should match a specific, temporary need.

ExampleA couple buys a 20-year term policy when their child is born, to replace lost income if a parent dies while the child is at home. When the child is an independent adult, the policy has done its job and can lapse.

11. Whole life, limited pay and endowment (3.3.2) pp. 64-66

What this tests: what makes a policy "permanent", what "guaranteed" means in a traditional policy, and how whole life, limited pay, modified whole life and endowment differ.

Cash value (permanent) insurance

  • "Permanent" policies are designed to stay in force as long as the insured is alive.
  • The insurer manages this by retaining part of the premium as cash value, which offsets the rising cost of insurance as the insured ages. So the initial premium is much higher than term for the same death benefit at the same issue age.
  • Why pay more? (1) The client wants a payout whenever they die: an inheritance, or a bequest to a charity, even if they live to 100. (2) The cash value can be used, and with dividends it may eventually pay the premiums, so the policy "pays for itself".
  • Permanent family: whole life and endowment (traditional); universal life, variable life and variable universal life (non-traditional); joint life.

Whole life

  • Far more premium is collected early than a one-year term would need, so the premium can stay level later. This is the same averaging idea as a 20-year or 30-year term.
  • The policy runs generally to age 100 or older. The extra early premium builds cash value after policy expenses.
  • Actuarially designed to guarantee the death benefit and a minimum increasing cash value. Premium, death benefit and cash value are all guaranteed while premiums are paid.
  • The cash value is invested in the insurer's general account: a very conservative portfolio, mainly real estate and bonds. Returns are safe but relatively low.
  • If the policy pays dividends, values can exceed the guaranteed illustration. They never fall below the guarantee, unless the owner took a loan or withdrawal.
  • Client signal: "I want to play it safe and be certain of the growth" points to whole life.

Variations

  • Endowment: a whole life design that endows at a specified age (age 100 or earlier). At that age the cash value equals the death benefit, and the insurer normally sends the owner a cheque. Sometimes the policy can be extended or converted to an annuity. Once popular as a savings vehicle, now less so.
  • Limited pay whole life: cover for life, but premiums stop sooner (for example at 65, or after 20 years). Premiums are somewhat higher than standard whole life, but the policy is paid-up much earlier.
  • Modified whole life: combines a term period (for example 10 years) with a later whole life period and premium. Lower premium at first. Often used for children: at 18 (or up to 25) it switches to whole life at a higher premium, and cover continues whatever happens to the child's insurability.
  • Other variants: hybrid, graded premium, single premium and adjustable life. Adjustable life was the forerunner of universal life.

12. Universal, variable and variable universal life (3.3.3) pp. 66-71

What this tests: bundled versus unbundled, fixed versus flexible, who bears investment risk, and universal life Type A versus Type B. Expect a "which policy fits this client" question.

Universal life (UL): the unbundled policy

  • Whole life is bundled: face amount, mortality charges, premium and cash value are inseparable.
  • UL unbundles mortality charges, expense charges and interest rates and keeps them separate. It is often labelled flexible premium adjustable life. Flexibility is the real distinction: premiums can vary and may even be skipped, provided the cash value can cover the charges.
  • How a premium is used: the insurer deducts the cost of insurance (mortality expense) and an expense load. The expense load is generally level for life, while mortality cost rises every year. The balance stays in cash value and earns interest.
  • Cash value grows while premiums plus interest exceed charges, and declines if charges exceed them. The "UL bucket" picture: premiums pour in, charges leak out, interest tops up.
  • The risk premium (term premium) is the cost of covering the net amount at risk at any time.

Example(Illustrative, simplified.) A UL policy starts the year with a cash value of Rs 5,00,000. The owner pays Rs 1,00,000. The insurer deducts a Rs 5,000 expense load and Rs 12,000 cost of insurance, leaving Rs 5,83,000, and credits 5% interest: 5,83,000 × 1.05 = Rs 6,12,150. Next year the cost of insurance is higher, and if the owner pays nothing the cash value may fall. That is the lapse risk.

Other UL features

  • Premium levels: minimum required (to keep the policy in force), target (based on assumed interest and expenses), maximum (the most the insurer accepts).
  • Credited interest has four possibilities: (1) minimum guaranteed in the contract; (2) current rate, usually guaranteed one year or less; (3) blended rate, mixing older and newer premiums at different rates; (4) rate on loaned amounts, which may be current or lower.
  • Secondary guarantee UL: a level annual premium through the premium-paying years even though it may generate no cash value. In essence, a long-term level-premium term policy.
  • Lapse risk: early UL assumed unrealistically high interest, so values fell and policies lapsed. Even now, a flexible-premium owner who pays too little may find that, as the insured ages and costs rise, there is not enough cash value. They must raise premiums or the policy lapses. Mortality costs rise with age in every life policy, but this is most visible in UL.
  • UL was among the first policies to use money-market funds.

Variable life (VL) and variable universal life (VUL)

  • Variable life is built on the whole-life platform (fixed premium, bundled), but the cash value goes into subaccounts (equities, bonds, money market), similar to mutual funds or unit trusts, not the general account. Introduced in the Netherlands, later popular in the UK.
  • VL has a guarantee that the death benefit never falls below the initial face amount. The cash value is not guaranteed. The owner assumes all investment risk. A prospectus is required.
  • VUL is to UL what VL is to whole life: flexible premium plus subaccounts. Higher return potential, and potential for loss. The owner must watch performance so there is enough value to sustain the policy for life.
  • VUL usually has no guaranteed death benefit. If values collapse, the whole contract can lapse with no residual cover. An optional guaranteed death benefit rider costs extra. VUL guarantees only the mortality charges and the right to keep the policy in force.

Example(Illustrative.) A VUL owner pays a small premium for years, and the subaccounts fall in a market crash. The cash value is Rs 1,50,000, but this year's cost of insurance and charges are Rs 1,80,000. Unless the owner pays more, the policy lapses and cover ends, which is why a guaranteed death benefit rider can be worth its cost for someone who needs lifelong cover.

Subaccounts versus mutual funds

  • Subaccount names may match famous mutual funds, but they are not mutual funds. Their structure differs, regulators give them different identification codes, and their fees are generally higher, so performance differs.
  • Taxable mutual funds distribute capital gains every year. Life policy and annuity values grow tax-deferred, so subaccounts can be managed without regard to tax efficiency.
  • They are securities, so anyone selling them must be properly licensed or registered.

Beneficiaries: revocable, irrevocable, per capita, per stirpes pp. 74-75

  • Primary is paid first. A contingent beneficiary is paid if the primary cannot be found, has died, or declines. Several layers (secondary, tertiary) are possible.
  • Revocable: owner can change at will (the usual case). HookStirpes = the stem of a tree. Money flows down the branch. Per capita = heads counted equally, so the surviving child gets diluted.

15. How much life insurance? Human life value and needs analysis (3.3.5) pp. 78-83

What this tests: the calculator mechanics (BEGIN mode, real rate, three-step education) and the idea behind capital utilisation versus capital retention. This is the most calculation-heavy part of the chapter, and Example 3 in the chapter is built on it.

The thinking behind it

  • Financial risk from death can be retained or transferred. Ask: "When you die, who will suffer economic loss?" and "Are there enough liquid assets to cover final expenses, debts, taxes and income for dependants?"
  • Life insurance is a cash substitute: the need at death is cash. As the estate grows, retain more risk. But anyone with dependants will rarely outgrow the need altogether.
  • "Needs" means a minimum, but clients also have wants (keeping the lifestyle, fulfilling dreams). Do not assume survivors just need to "get by". Results are an educated guess, so review regularly.
  • All calculations assume the death happens tomorrow. Do the analysis for each spouse separately, since liabilities, final expenses and education may match but income replacement will not.

Method 1: human life value, five steps p. 78

  1. Estimate future average annual earnings.
  2. Subtract income taxes, insurance premiums and personal living expenses. This is the income the family uses, and becomes PMT, needed at the beginning of each year.
  3. Estimate the productive years left (N), until retirement.
  4. Choose a discount rate (I/YR).
  5. Calculate the present value as an annuity due (PVAD), calculator in BEGIN mode.

16. Annuities (3.3.6) pp. 83-90

What this tests: the longevity-risk logic, immediate versus deferred, fixed versus variable versus indexed, and the payout options (especially the ones that leave money to heirs).

HookLife insurance protects against dying too soon. An annuity protects against living too long. They are "flip sides". An annuity is not life insurance, even though life insurers sell both.

  • In time-value terms, an annuity is a stream of payments. Actuaries use mortality factors so the income can last a lifetime, which is why insurers (who employ actuaries) sell them.
  • When capital and interest turn into an income stream, the contract is annuitised. Annuitisation can be for life or a period (10 or 20 years). Many annuities need not be annuitised at all: the owner can simply withdraw. Taxes, surrender charges and penalties may apply to withdrawals.

17. Health insurance: cost sharing and managed care (3.4, 3.4.1, 3.4.2) pp. 90-94

What this tests: deductible, coinsurance, copayment and MOOP (and the arithmetic that uses them), and the managed-care plan types HMO, EPO, PPO and POS.

Why and where

  • A major medical claim is among the most devastating losses a family can face. Governments in many territories provide a base level of care. It costs no premium, but has gaps, especially if you want a type or level of care outside the base benefits.
  • Private cover reduces that risk, but there is still out-of-pocket cost: deductibles, copayments, coinsurance and a high MOOP. Keep an emergency fund to go with the plan.
  • Group cover is usually cheaper (employers bargain collectively). Some plans have a waiting period, for example 60 days. Privately purchased cover usually costs much more. Individuals can cut cost by self-insuring: a higher deductible, copayment or coinsurance.
  • Two groups may need cover: citizens and expatriates, with different needs.

How much cover? p. 99

  • Costs vary a great deal by location and institution; get local quotes. Premium rises with age, so buying at 60 costs less than at 70. Health, gender and optional benefits also matter.
  • Sizing: start with the cost of nursing-home care (usually the dearest), then subtract income that continues whether the client is at home or in a facility (government pensions, employer pensions, annuities, investment income). Hybrid policies pp. 99-100

A hybrid pairs life insurance or an annuity with LTC cover. It costs more than either alone, but less than both bought separately. If the insured dies without needing care, the death benefit or account value is paid. If care is needed, it is used.

Is it worth it? pp. 100-101

  • Very wealthy (multimillion assets): can pay out of pocket, so probably no need. Low assets: premiums are likely too high. The middle group has the hard choice (care quality, freedom of choice, premium, asset and income protection).
  • Premiums can exceed $1,000 a year. The average LTC need for someone over 65 is around three years, so many choose a benefit period near that.
  • A "catastrophic" LTC policy combines a very long elimination period (for example 12 months) with a long benefit period, to guard against a 10- or 20-year need at a more reasonable premium. Only for those who can pay the early costs.

19. Personal disability income insurance (3.5) pp. 101-110

What this tests: definitions of disability, partial versus residual, the renewal ladder (noncancelable down to cancelable), and group coordination.

Why it matters pp. 101-102

  • Long-term disability can be more financially devastating than death: medical costs rise and income stops. Some call it "the living death".
  • Mortality = the risk of death; morbidity = unhealthiness or disease. Life insurance covers mortality; disability and health insurance cover morbidity.
  • Underwriting differs: life insurers approve or decline, then assign a rate class (preferred, standard, substandard). Disability insurers may insure with exclusions, such as no cover for disability from a previously troubled back. A person who is already disabled may still qualify for life insurance. A person declined for life may still get disability cover.
  • Manage the risk with an emergency fund plus disability insurance, ideally both. Good unearned (investment) income reduces the need.

The three main considerations p. 102

ConsiderationDetail
1. Benefit amountUsually 60% to 70% of earned income at issue. Investment or other unearned income is not counted (and may reduce the cover offered)
2. Benefit period (and elimination period)Short-term: about 6 months to 2 years. Long-term: to age 65, occasionally for life. The elimination (waiting) period is the policy's deductible: longer wait, lower premium
3. Definition of disabilityDecides whether you are paid (see the table below)
  • Premiums, cover periods and limits also depend on occupation: white-collar workers generally get better terms than blue-collar workers.
  • Cover can be accident-only (cheaper, since accidents are a smaller cause of disability) or accident and sickness. Sickness-only almost never occurs.

Example(Illustrative.) Monthly earned income $6,000. The insurer will write 60% to 70%, so a benefit of $3,600 to $4,200 a month. A further $1,000 of monthly rental income would not increase that.

Other provisions and premium value pp. 107-109

  • Misstatement of age: if found at claim time, the benefit is adjusted to what the premium would have bought at the correct age.
  • Grace period: generally 30 days. Reinstatement: usually needs proof of insurability, and all back premiums.
  • Relation of earnings: protects the insurer from a person who is better off disabled. If income at claim time is substantially lower than at purchase, the insurer may reduce the benefit to fit current earnings and return the excess premiums, usually with interest. It is "financial underwriting at the time of claim".
  • Conversion: group policies generally cannot convert to individual cover. A few carriers allow it, mostly for professionals, within about 30 days. If you have the right, convert first and then shop for better terms.

Example(Chapter's case.) A policy costs $550 a year for a $1,000 monthly benefit, with a 90-day elimination period. One-year disability: 9 payable months = $9,000 (it takes about 16 years of premiums to pay this much: 9,000 ÷ 550 = 16.4). Five years: 57 payable months = $57,000. Twenty years: 237 months = $237,000. Compare: car cover of $300,000 liability plus $50,000 property damage often costs about $1,200 a year, yet income earned over a working life is a far larger asset.

How much to buy p. 108

  1. Add up monthly expenses.
  2. Add extra costs caused by the disability.
  3. Subtract work-related costs that stop (clothing, transport).
  4. Subtract other income (investment income).
  5. Subtract other disability cover (group plan, government benefits).
  6. The remainder is roughly the cover needed. Limits: insurers will not write above about 60% to 70% of income, premiums are high, and coordination of benefits may cut benefits.

Example(Illustrative.) Rs 60,000 monthly expenses + Rs 10,000 extra disability costs − Rs 8,000 work costs − Rs 12,000 investment income − Rs 25,000 group long-term benefit = Rs 25,000 a month still to be covered individually.

20. Business-related cover (3.6) pp. 110-115

What this tests: matching a business exposure to the right cover, and who owns, pays for and benefits from each policy.

Businesses can use the same kinds of policies as individuals, but the terms and beneficiaries are different, and they also have specialised exposures.

Key person cover pp. 110-111

  • A key person is one on whom the business depends to stay viable. It can be an executive, a salesperson, a manager, even an administrator. Key means vital, not senior.
  • Key person life insurance: the business (with the person's consent) applies for, pays for, and is owner and primary beneficiary of the policy. The money helps the business replace the person, hire temporary staff or continue cash flow.
  • When the key person is also a partner or majority shareholder of a closely held company, a buy-sell agreement funded by life insurance protects both the business and the family: the policy pays the business (or other owners), who pay the heir in exchange for the deceased's share.
  • Side effect: properly structured, a key person policy can encourage the employee to stay.
  • Key person disability income: pays the company so it can hire a replacement. Typically elimination period of 30 to 180 days and benefit period up to about 2 years. May be a lump sum or monthly. Take care that company payments to the disabled person do not interfere with their personal disability income.
  • Disability buy-out: aimed at owners. If an owner is permanently disabled, the others will want to buy the share. The policy funds the buy-out and belongs in the business continuation plan.

Example(Illustrative; the chapter gives no formula.) A salesperson brings in Rs 2 crore of revenue at a 20% margin, so Rs 40 lakh of profit a year. It would take about two years to replace them, so the exposure is roughly Rs 80 lakh. The company buys a policy for about that amount on the salesperson's life, naming itself beneficiary.

Business overhead expense (BOE) pp. 112-113

  • Pays the business's fixed expenses if the key professional is disabled and cannot generate income: salaries, legal and accounting, dues, rent or mortgage, loan interest, utilities, maintenance, vehicle costs, equipment lease, insurance premiums, taxes.
  • Not a replacement for personal disability income cover. It keeps the business alive until the owner returns or sells (a business with staff sells for more).
  • Elimination period up to about 90 days (shorter than personal cover because the business needs the money soon). Benefit period usually up to 24 months.
  • Owner chooses a monthly benefit based on usual expenses, but the policy pays actual expenses up to the limit. Some policies carry unused benefits forward. Often noncancelable or guaranteed renewable. Premiums may be a tax-deductible business expense, depending on the jurisdiction.

Example(Illustrative.) A doctor runs a clinic with Rs 3 lakh of monthly overheads and buys BOE with a Rs 3 lakh monthly limit. After a disabling accident and the elimination period, a month in which actual overheads are Rs 2.6 lakh is paid at Rs 2.6 lakh, not Rs 3 lakh. The doctor's own income needs personal disability cover.

HookBOE = Bills Of the Enterprise: it pays the office's bills, not the owner's pay. Key person: the Business owns it, pays it and collects it.

Front end or back end? Review question 38

25. Test yourself

Try each question first, then open the answer. Questions 1 to 10 cover general insurance, liability and term life; questions 11 to 25 cover the rest of the chapter, and the last one is an integrated case.

Q1. Which homeowners coverage pays the medical bills of a neighbour who is injured at your home, regardless of fault? (a) Coverage C (b) Coverage D (c) Coverage F (d) Coverage A

Answer

(c) Coverage F. The key words are "not living at the residence" and "regardless of fault". C is personal property, D is loss of use, A is the dwelling.

Q2. Replacement cost at time of loss is $500,000. The owner carries $350,000. Kitchen fire loss is $30,000; deductible $2,000. What does the insurer pay?

Answer

Required = $500,000 × 80% = $400,000. Ratio = $350,000 ÷ $400,000 = 0.875. Apply: 0.875 × $30,000 = $26,250. Subtract deductible: $26,250 − $2,000 = $24,250.

Check: carried is below required, so a penalty applies. The result is below $28,000 (what full payment would be).

Q3. Replacement cost at time of loss is $600,000. The owner carries $480,000. Loss is $40,000; deductible $1,000. What does the insurer pay?

Answer

Required = $600,000 × 80% = $480,000. Carried equals required, so the ratio is 1. Payment = $40,000 − $1,000 = $39,000. Edge case: exactly 80% means no penalty.

Q4. Which of these would a Comprehensive Personal Liability (CPL) policy cover? (a) A libel claim by a neighbour (b) Injury caused while driving your registered car (c) A visitor's injury caused by your negligent act at home (d) Fire damage to your own garage

Answer

(c). Libel is intentional, a registered car needs its own liability cover, and CPL is liability not property cover, so your own garage is not insured.

Q5. A physician treated a patient in 2012, changed insurers in 2014, and the patient filed a claim in 2017. Which insurer pays under (a) an occurrence form, and (b) a claims-made form?

Answer

(a) The 2012 insurer (policy in force when the error occurred). (b) The 2017 insurer (policy in force when the claim was made).

Q6. A client says: "My house is worth $800,000 on the market, so I will insure it for $800,000." Evaluate this.

Answer

Market value includes the land (which cannot be insured) and location premium. Insure the replacement cost of the building at the time of loss. Aim for 100%, never below 80% to avoid the coinsurance penalty, and review regularly or add an inflation guard endorsement.

Q7. Two people slip on the loose step outside your front door: a neighbour's child and your spouse. Under Section II, who gets medical payments from Coverage F?

Answer

The neighbour's child does, with no negligence required. Your spouse does not: household members are insureds, and insureds cannot be liable to themselves, so F excludes them.

Q8. A court orders you to pay $700,000 after a covered car accident. Your auto liability limit is $250,000 and your umbrella limit is $1,000,000. How is it paid?

Answer

The auto policy pays first: $250,000. The umbrella then pays the difference: $450,000. Total $700,000. (This assumes the claim is covered by the umbrella; an excluded item such as an owned aircraft would not be.)

Q9. The power fails across your whole neighbourhood and the food in your freezer spoils. Is it covered under the property agreement's exclusions?

Answer

No. The power failure exclusion removes cover for losses from interruptions of power that take place away from the premises. A fault at your own house is a different situation, because that is not the off-premises interruption the exclusion describes.

Q10. In two sentences, explain malpractice versus errors and omissions, with an example of a professional who needs each.

Answer

Malpractice insurance covers professionals whose activities can cause physical harm, such as a surgeon. Errors and omissions insurance covers professionals who can cause financial harm to clients, such as an accountant, lawyer, stockbroker or financial advisor. Hook: Medical versus Economic.

Q11. A client takes a 20-year home loan and wants cover that tracks the falling loan balance. Which term version fits, and how do the premium and the benefit behave?

Answer

Decreasing term. The premium stays level while the death benefit falls each year, roughly following the loan's amortisation. It is the mirror image of annually renewable term (rising premium, level benefit) and is cheaper than level term with the same starting benefit.

Q12. A universal life policy has a face amount of Rs 50 lakh and an account value of Rs 12 lakh. What is paid on death under (a) Type A and (b) Type B?

Answer

(a) Rs 50 lakh: Type A pays the face amount (the insurer is at risk for Rs 38 lakh). (b) Rs 62 lakh: Type B pays face plus account value, so the insurer stays at risk for Rs 50 lakh. Hook: B = Both.

Q13. A Rs 90 lakh policy names Asha, Bala and Chitra (siblings) as equal primary beneficiaries. Chitra dies before the insured, leaving two children. How is it paid (a) per capita and (b) per stirpes?

Answer

(a) Per capita: equal shares among the living, so Asha, Bala and Chitra's two children make four people: 90 ÷ 4 = Rs 22.5 lakh each. (b) Per stirpes: Asha and Bala keep their full share, Rs 30 lakh each, and Chitra's Rs 30 lakh passes down her line: Rs 15 lakh to each child. Check: 30 + 30 + 15 + 15 = 90. Per stirpes does not dilute the surviving primary beneficiaries.

Q14. A client stops paying premiums on a whole life policy. (a) She wants some cover for life without paying more. (b) Her husband wants the full original face amount for as long as the cash value can buy it. Which nonforfeiture option does each need, and what happens if nobody chooses?

Answer

(a) Paid-up reduced amount: same policy type, smaller face amount, for life. (b) Extended term: the same face amount but only for the period the cash value buys. If nobody chooses, a whole life policy defaults to extended term. The third option is taking the cash (C-P-E).

Q15. Capital retention. A client's family needs Rs 6,00,000 a year. Discount rate 8%, inflation 4%. Liabilities Rs 50 lakh, final expenses Rs 10 lakh, education Rs 40 lakh, existing cover Rs 75 lakh. Find the net new cover.

Answer

Real rate = 1.08 ÷ 1.04 − 1 = 3.8462%. Income capital = 6,00,000 ÷ 0.038462 = Rs 1,56,00,000. Gross = 50 + 156 + 10 + 40 = Rs 256 lakh. Net = 256 − 75 = Rs 181 lakh (Rs 1.81 crore).

Trap: using 8% − 4% = 4% gives Rs 1.5 crore of income capital and a net of Rs 175 lakh. Real rate is (1 + r) ÷ (1 + i) − 1. (Illustrative rupee figures, not from the chapter.)

Q16. Chapter Example 3. Future earnings $75,000 a year, personal living expenses, premiums and taxes $25,000, 20 years to retirement, discount rate 3.5%. Liabilities $400,000, final expenses $50,000, education $200,000, existing insurance $250,000. Find the additional cover needed.

Answer

Income = $75,000 − $25,000 = $50,000. BEGIN mode, N = 20, I/YR = 3.5, PMT = 50,000, solve PV = $735,492. Gross = 400,000 + 735,492 + 50,000 + 200,000 = $1,385,492. Less existing $250,000 = $1,135,492.

The usual wrong answers: forgetting to deduct existing cover ($1,385,492), or using END mode (about $1,110,620).

Q17. A health plan has a $3,000 deductible, 80% coinsurance and an $8,000 MOOP. (a) A covered bill of $20,000: who pays what? (b) Later in the same year, another covered bill of $10,000. Assume the deductible and coinsurance both count toward the MOOP.

Answer

(a) Insured: $3,000 + 20% × ($20,000 − $3,000) = 3,000 + 3,400 = $6,400. Insurer: $13,600.

(b) The deductible is already met (annual, cumulative). Coinsurance alone would be 20% × $10,000 = $2,000, but only $8,000 − $6,400 = $1,600 is left before the MOOP. Insured pays $1,600; insurer pays $8,400. Afterwards the insurer pays 100% for the rest of the year.

The chapter's own case ($2,000 deductible, 80%, $5,000 service) gives $2,600 and $2,400. How the MOOP interacts with the deductible varies by plan, so (b) uses a stated assumption.

Q18. Meera wants to keep her orthopaedic surgeon, who is outside any plan's network, and she travels often. Which managed care plan suits her better, an HMO or a PPO, and what is the trade-off?

Answer

A PPO (or a POS plan). It allows out-of-network care, at higher deductibles and coinsurance. An HMO requires its own physicians and hospitals, uses a gatekeeper, and pays nothing outside the network except in emergencies. The biggest HMO versus PPO difference: HMOs do not pay outside the network; PPOs often pay a reduced amount.

Q19. An LTC policy pays $150 a day with a five-year benefit period and a 90-day elimination period. (a) What is the maximum benefit pool (use 30-day months, as the chapter does)? (b) If care costs $200 a day, what does the client pay during the elimination period? (c) Which of these triggers benefits: unable to dress only; unable to bathe and feed; Alzheimer's with all ADLs intact?

Answer

(a) $150 × 30 = $4,500 a month × 60 months = $270,000. (b) $200 × 90 = $18,000 out of pocket. (c) Bathing and feeding (two ADLs) and Alzheimer's (cognitive impairment triggers benefits on its own). Unable to dress only is one ADL, which is not enough.

Q20. Pre-disability earnings $8,000 a month. Benefit $4,800 (60%). The policy has a residual provision that pays when income falls by more than 20%, using the chapter's flat 25% of the benefit. After returning to work the client earns $5,600. What is the benefit and the total income?

Answer

Loss = (8,000 − 5,600) ÷ 8,000 = 30%, above the 20% threshold, so the residual benefit applies. Flat 25% × $4,800 = $1,200. Total = $5,600 + $1,200 = $6,800.

Extra: many real policies pay in proportion to the loss, which would give 30% × $4,800 = $1,440 and $7,040. For this exam, follow the chapter's flat-percentage method.

Q21. Insurer A raised the premium on all its disability policyholders in a class. Insurer B can never change the premium. Name each provision, and say which is stronger. Which provision lets an insurer refuse renewal on any anniversary?

Answer

A is guaranteed renewable (cannot cancel, can raise the premium for the whole class). B is noncancelable (cannot cancel and cannot change the premium), the strongest and dearest. An insurer that can refuse renewal on any anniversary is renewable at the option of the company; cancelable policies can be cancelled any time on notice.

Q22. A software firm's chief technology officer is disabled for 14 months and the firm hires a contractor. Which cover would have helped? Who should own it, pay for it and benefit? What are the typical elimination and benefit periods?

Answer

Key person disability income. The firm owns, pays and benefits, so it can hire a replacement. Typical elimination period 30 to 180 days; benefit period up to about 2 years. Not to be confused with business overhead expense (pays fixed bills such as rent and salaries) or a disability buy-out (funds buying a disabled owner's share).

Q23. Indexed annuity. (a) The index rises 15% and the participation rate is 80%. (b) The index falls 10%. (c) Illustrative index levels 2000, 2200, 2000, 2400 at successive anniversaries: what do point-to-point and ratchet credit over the three years?

Answer

(a) 80% × 15% = +12% (before any cap or spread). (b) 0%: no loss, no gain. (c) Point-to-point: (2,400 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = +20%. Ratchet: year 1 +10%, year 2 0% (the index fell, nothing is lost), year 3 +20% from the reset level 2,000, so 1.10 × 1.00 × 1.20 = +32%. Ratchet locks in each year's gain.

Q24. A 65-year-old buys a single-life annuity and dies two years later. What happens to the remaining value, and which two payout options protect against this?

Answer

With single-life payments, income stops at death and the insurer keeps what is left. The refund option returns any remaining principal and earnings at the end of the annuity period. Period certain pays for at least a minimum number of years. (A fixed amount payout, by contrast, pays a fixed sum for as long as principal and earnings last.)

Q25. Integrated case. Kiran, 40, is a self-employed consultant in Hyderabad earning Rs 24 lakh a year. He has a spouse, two children aged 8 and 5, a Rs 60 lakh home loan and no employer benefits. Which covers from this chapter does he need, and why?

Answer
  • Life: term cover sized by needs analysis (LIFE): the Rs 60 lakh loan, income replacement (capital retention if the family should keep the principal), final expenses, education, less existing cover. Level term for income and education; optionally decreasing term for the loan.
  • Disability income: his earning power is his biggest asset and he has no group cover. Aim for 60% to 70% of earned income, the best definition he can get (own occupation), and noncancelable or guaranteed renewable terms. In India, check availability and use accident and critical illness cover to fill gaps.
  • Health: family cover with attention to deductible, coinsurance and MOOP (in India, a floater plus a top-up) and an emergency fund.
  • Liability: errors and omissions (professional indemnity) for a consultant, and an umbrella if his assets justify it.
  • Later or if relevant: LTC planning closer to retirement; business overhead expense if he rents an office and pays staff.

No single order is "correct". Examiners look for matching each exposure to the right cover and giving a reason.

General insurance: home, property, vehicles (Q1-11)

1. How much land value is included when insuring a home, and why?

Answer

None. You cannot insure land. (Section 1)

2. What coverages are in the two sections of a standard homeowners policy?

Answer

Section I: A dwelling (with attached additions and building materials on site), B other structures, C personal property, D loss of use. Section II: E comprehensive liability; F medical payments to others, claims expenses and damage to property of others. (Section 2)

3. Why are Coverages A and B not on a renter's policy, and what is always included?

Answer

A and B protect the dwelling and outbuildings, which renters are not responsible for. Renter's insurance always includes Coverage C for the insured's belongings. (Section 5)

4. Which perils are covered under basic, broad form and open peril cover?

Answer

Basic: 11 perils (fire and lightning, windstorm and hail, explosion, riot and civil commotion, vehicles, aircraft, smoke, vandalism and malicious mischief, glass breakage, theft, volcanic eruption). Broad: those 11 plus falling objects; weight of ice, snow or sleet; heating or air-conditioning damage; accidental discharge or overflow of water; freezing of plumbing; artificially generated electrical current; and expanded basic perils. Open peril: everything not specifically excluded (so "all risks" is a misnomer). (Section 3)

5. House valued at $400,000, insured for $300,000, 80% coinsurance, $1,000 deductible, $20,000 fire. What is paid?

Answer

Required = $320,000. 300,000 ÷ 320,000 = 0.9375; × $20,000 = $18,750; less $1,000 = $17,750. (Section 4)

6. Actual cash value versus replacement cost?

Answer

Replacement cost pays what it takes to replace the property, up to limits. ACV is replacement cost minus depreciation.

7. Why choose guaranteed replacement cost over simple replacement cost?

Answer

Even 80% or 100% cover can fall short when a disaster (e.g., a hurricane) drives up material and labour costs. The guarantee covers rebuilding even above the insured amount.

8. What is the problem with basic personal property cover, and the fix?

Answer

It is typically 50% of dwelling cover, which may be inadequate, and valuables have low special limits. Fix: a personal property endorsement or inland marine policy. (Section 6)

9. On what basis is inland marine usually written?

Answer

Open perils; little or no deductible (or higher, by choice); stated (appraised) value rather than replacement cost or ACV.

10. Main difference between auto and homeowners medical payments?

Answer

Auto med pay sits outside the liability agreement and covers the insured, family and occupants. Homeowners med pay sits inside the liability agreement and covers no household member: exactly opposite. (Section 7)

11. How does a standard car policy define the insured?

Answer

The named insured or any family member living in the household; anyone authorised to use the covered car; anyone the insured authorises to drive it.

Liability (Q12-16)

12. Civil versus criminal liability?

Answer

Civil: held accountable for causing financial loss to another. Criminal: violating the law, with prosecution and penalties such as imprisonment. One act can create both. (Section 8)

13. What is a tort and how does it relate to liability?

Answer

Harm (physical, emotional or financial) caused to another; the wrongdoer is the tortfeasor. Torts may be intentional (almost never insurable) or unintentional (liability insurance covers these). Torts are civil and focus on financial damage.

14. Absolute and vicarious liability, and employers?

Answer

Absolute: imposed where negligence cannot specifically be shown; employers can be held liable to employees this way. Vicarious: liable for another's acts (parents for children; employers when one employee harms another).

15. Umbrella versus standard liability policy?

Answer

A basic (CPL) policy has low limits; an umbrella (excess, catastrophic) policy has very high limits and takes over where basic policies stop, protecting against very large awards.

16. Two types of professional liability cover, and who uses each?

Answer

Malpractice (medical professionals) and errors and omissions (everyone else). (Section 9)

Life insurance (Q17-32)

17. Two broad divisions of life insurance?

Answer

Term (a specified period) and permanent (throughout life).

18. How does regular term work?

Answer

Level death benefit and level premium over the term; usually renewable each year at increasing rates afterwards. (Section 10)

19. How can a policy cover someone for life?

Answer

Permanent policies keep part of the premium as cash value to offset the rising cost of insurance with age, so the initial premium is much higher than term for the same cover. (Section 11)

20. Where is cash value invested in whole life, UL and VUL?

Answer

Whole life: the general account (conservative real estate and bonds). UL: interest-bearing investments such as money market funds. VUL: the equivalent of collective (mutual fund) investments. (Section 12)

21. Why is UL called unbundled and flexible premium adjustable life?

Answer

It separates mortality charges, expense charges and interest rates, and allows flexible premiums. Flexibility is its real distinction.

22. UL Type I (A) versus Type II (B)?

Answer

A: level death benefit. B: death benefit rises with cash value; the owner effectively buys one-year term equal to that year's cash value, so both are paid at death.

23. VL versus VUL?

Answer

Like whole life versus UL: VL is bundled and fixed; VUL is unbundled and flexible. Both invest in sub-accounts / mutual-fund equivalents.

24. The two joint life types and when to use each?

Answer

First-to-die pays on the first death: ideal for business buy-sell agreements. Second-to-die (last-to-die, survivorship) pays after both die: estate liquidity for taxes or a bequest at the second death. (Section 13)

25. Three potential parties in a life policy?

Answer

Policyowner, insured, beneficiary. There may be fewer; one person (via the estate) can be all three. (Section 14)

26. How do the grace period and reinstatement relate?

Answer

The grace period (usually 30 or 31 days) keeps the policy in full force after a missed due date. After it, the policy lapses; reinstatement revives it if past-due premiums are paid and (normally) evidence of insurability given, with no cover between lapse and reinstatement.

27. Nonforfeiture options?

Answer

Cash, paid-up reduced amount, extended term insurance.

28. Why be careful making a beneficiary irrevocable?

Answer

The owner then needs the beneficiary's written permission to change beneficiaries, borrow, surrender or assign the policy absolutely or collaterally.

29. Five dividend options?

Answer

Cash; accumulate at interest; reduce premiums; buy paid-up additions; buy one-year term (the fifth dividend option).

30. Two methods (besides rules of thumb) for the amount of life cover?

Answer

Human life value and needs-based. (Section 15)

31. Capital retention: $75,000 inflation-adjusted income, real rate 2.9126%, no existing cover. How much?

Answer

$75,000 ÷ 0.029126 = $2,575,019 (textbook). With the exact real rate (0.03 ÷ 1.03) it is $2,575,000. Principal is never depleted.

32. Child aged 10 enters a 4-year university at 18; first-year cost today $20,000; tuition inflation 5%; discount rate 7%. Cover needed today?

Answer

Inflate: 8N, 5%, PV 20,000 → FV $29,549.11. Real rate (1.07 ÷ 1.05 − 1) = 1.9048%. BEGIN: 4N, 1.9048%, PMT 29,549.11 → PVAD $114,923.62. Discount: 8N, 7%, FV 114,923.62 → PV $66,886.60 (about $66,887).

Annuities (Q33-37)

33. Fundamental difference between annuities and life insurance?

Answer

Flip sides: life insurance protects against dying too soon; annuities against living too long. (Section 16)

34. How many times can the payout option be changed after annuitisation?

Answer

None. Annuitisation is generally irrevocable.

35. How can a variable annuity address inflation?

Answer

Fixed payments lose purchasing power; variable annuities invest in securities, so returns may keep pace with inflation.

36. Straight life annuity at the annuitant's death, and how the refund option helps?

Answer

Payments stop and the insurer keeps the rest. The refund option pays any remaining principal and earnings to a beneficiary.

37. Period certain versus fixed amount?

Answer

Period certain pays for at least a minimum number of years, based on the account sum. Fixed amount pays a set sum for as long as principal and earnings last.

Health, LTC and deductibles (Q38-46)

38. How do homeowners, motor and health deductibles differ?

Answer

Same function (paid before benefits; a form of risk retention). Auto and health deductibles come off the front of the calculation; homeowners deductibles come off the back. Only health deductibles can be met for the year and then reset. (Section 22)

39. Copayments versus coinsurance?

Answer

Coinsurance: a percentage split of covered costs after the deductible (insurer pays 100% once the out-of-pocket maximum is met). Copayment: a set amount per service regardless of deductible or coinsurance. (Section 17)

40. What is a gatekeeper in managed care?

Answer

The primary care physician, whom you must see first (except emergencies), and who decides on referrals, so controls the care you receive.

41. Biggest coverage difference between HMOs and PPOs?

Answer

HMOs pay nothing out of network; PPOs often pay a reduced amount out of network.

42. Why might someone with good health insurance still need LTC cover?

Answer

Regular health insurance usually does not pay for long-term or extended care. (Section 18)

43. Highest and second-highest levels of LTC?

Answer

Skilled nursing (24-hour registered nurse under a doctor), then intermediate (less intensive nursing or rehabilitation).

44. What are ADLs and how do they apply?

Answer

Bathing, feeding, transferring, dressing, using the bathroom, continence. Inability to perform any two (usually physician-certified) triggers benefits; Alzheimer's or dementia alone also triggers them.

45. What is the elimination period on LTC and disability policies?

Answer

A period after diagnosis with no benefits, typically 90 days, similar to a deductible.

46. What does LTC pay when a family member provides care, and one solution?

Answer

Typically nothing. A respite care benefit pays another caregiver so the family caregiver can take a break.

Disability and business (Q47-56)

47. Why is disability called a "living death"?

Answer

Significant medical costs plus inability to function fully or earn income. (Section 19)

48. Three most important disability considerations?

Answer

Benefit amount; benefit period (including elimination period); definition of disability.

49. Partial versus residual disability benefit?

Answer

Partial: reduced benefit (e.g., 50%) for a shorter time when the insured returns part-time. Residual: reduced benefit possibly for the whole benefit period when income falls (usually by more than a stated percentage, e.g., 20%) even working full-time.

50. Four primary disability definitions?

Answer

Any occupation (most restrictive); modified any occupation (qualified by education, training, experience); own occupation (most liberal, sometimes via a specialty letter or rider); split definition (own occupation for 2 to 5 years, then stricter).

51. How is a loss-of-income policy different?

Answer

It pays when income falls due to illness or injury even if the insured keeps working; occupation and duties are not significant factors.

52. Noncancelable versus guaranteed renewable?

Answer

Both guarantee renewal for the full term. Noncancelable also fixes the premium as stated; guaranteed renewable lets the insurer raise premiums for a whole class.

53. General purpose of key person insurance?

Answer

To protect the business, and fund it while it adjusts, if a person vital to its operation dies or is disabled. (Section 20)

54. Business overhead expense versus individual disability cover?

Answer

BOE is not a personal income replacement; it keeps the business viable by paying salaries and expenses for a period. Shorter elimination period; benefit period usually no more than two years.

55. Which liability exposure is covered by neither CPL nor commercial liability?

Answer

Motor vehicles: buy motor vehicle liability cover. An umbrella covers motor liability when its underlying cover includes it.

56. Why might a non-profit board member need liability cover?

Answer

Board members can be held liable for the organisation's activities (religious institutions, hospitals, community associations and similar).

1The big picture: three choices, in order

Textbook: Chapter 4 introduction and 4.1, pp. 123 to 124

Many advisors know investments better than insurance. The chapter fixes that by walking through the due diligence (or "due care") an advisor owes the client when buying insurance. Think of it as three decisions made in a fixed order.

OrderDecisionKey questionMost important test
1Insurance companyCan this insurer keep its promise for decades?Financial solvency and stability
2Intermediary (agent or broker)Will this person listen, explain and service the policy?Inclination to service
3PolicyWhich type and features fit the client?Adequate amount first, policy type second

HookPut on your C-A-P before you buy: Company (solvency) → Agent (service) → Policy (amount first, type second). One "most important" word for each: solvency, service, sufficiency.

ExtraWhy the order matters. When you buy insurance you are buying a promise to pay in the future. The best policy from a weak insurer is worthless if the insurer fails before the claim. So the company check comes first, even before price.

No truly global body oversees insurer solvency. The textbook uses the U.S. as a guide, not a model. Each U.S. state has an insurance commissioner; together they form the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC), which writes model rules. One rule, the watchlist, uses 12 financial ratios.

RBC ratios measure the minimum capital an insurer needs to support its operations, because its primary purpose is paying valid claims.

Hook"Some Honest Officers Like A Long Inspection": Size and age, History, Operating ratios, Lapse ratio, Average policy size, Lines of business, Investment return.

ExampleLapse ratio check. An insurer has 1,20,000 policies in force at the start of the year; 15,000 terminate during the year.

Lapse ratio = 15,000 ÷ 1,20,000 = 0.125 = 12.5%

12.5% is above the 10% trigger, so investigate: are illustrations being missed, premiums uncompetitive, service weak, or is the insurer in trouble?

ExampleInvestment return sanity check. Insurer A earns 4.5% on its assets and credits 6% on its universal life policies. The 1.5-point gap has to come from somewhere (surplus, new premiums, or future cuts), so treat the 6% as unsustainable. Insurer B earns 9% when peers earn 5%: ask what extra risk it is taking.

ExtraLapse ratio versus persistency. The textbook says the lapse ratio "is known as" persistency. Strictly they are mirror images: persistency is the share that stays. In the example above, persistency is 100% − 12.5% = 87.5%. If an exam option says "high persistency is a warning sign", that is wrong; high lapse (low persistency) is the warning sign. Agents have persistency ratings too.

Trap"Very large average policy size" does not mean "strong company". It signals mainly term business, which can mean fewer assets supporting each rupee of cover. The textbook's key test is insurance in force relative to assets.

8Choosing an insurance policy

The advisor, not the insurer or agent, is best placed to decide the policy type and features that support the client's objectives.   • The total life need is often very high, and families cannot or will not buy enough to cover everything.
  • So prioritise: for example, final expenses plus income while children are at home.
  • That is a reason to explore term rather than permanent insurance: it is affordable, so the need is more likely to be fully covered.

11Insure or not? Cost versus likelihood

Textbook: Chapter 5 introduction, pp. 140 to 142

Chapter 5 pulls the whole course together into a plan. The major risk areas are property, liability, life, health (including long-term care), and disability and loss of income, plus one more: failure of others (loss caused by someone's wrong action, or inaction they should have taken). We cannot predict when a loss will occur, but we can ask whether the client could be affected, how much it would cost, and whether the premium is reasonable.

ExampleThe shed (textbook). A home-built shed cost $1,200. Insuring it against collapse costs $600 a year.

$1,200 ÷ $600 per year = 2 years of premium = full replacement cost

So there is little reason to insure the shed (unless it holds valuable contents). But the $1,000,000 home in front of it should be insured adequately.

Likelihood changes the answer too. If collapse could only come from an earthquake and the ground has had none for 500 years, the risk is remote. If the shed sits in heavy-snow country and the peril is the weight of snow, collapse becomes far more likely.

FactorQuestionEffect on decision
Replacement cost (severity)Could the client pay for repair or replacement without real harm?If not (no money, or using funds would hurt other goals) ⇒ consider insurance
Likelihood (frequency)How probable is the loss from this peril?Very unlikely ⇒ lower priority; probable ⇒ stronger case to protect
Premium versus valueIs the premium out of line with what is insured?Yes (like the shed) ⇒ skip the insurance

Applying it to people, not just property

AreaTextbook view
DisabilityActuaries can estimate the odds of disability; data is available from insurers.
HealthMore nebulous: we do not know who will get seriously ill, but we know it happens and costs can be high. Less of an issue where the state pays fully.
LifeMortality tables give odds for a class of people, never an individual. Valuing a life is inexact; impact varies with life stage (young mother of three versus single older person). Cover must still be weighed against premium: a spouse may feel "immeasurable", but insuring "immeasurable" would cost too much.
General principle: Reduce or avoid when practical → otherwise transfer (insure) → but only if the cost-benefit test favours it

ExtraThe classic severity-frequency grid (standard in CFP courses; the textbook's shed logic and Chapter 1's three rules point the same way):

Hook"Severity decides IF; frequency decides the PRICE." A big, rare loss is the sweet spot for insurance; a small, frequent one is cheaper to carry yourself (Chapter 1: don't risk a lot for a little, don't risk more than you can afford to lose, consider the odds).

18Optimisation: audit, implementation, trade-offs, road map

After prioritising, make optimal recommendations. After needs analysis and goals, the next most important step is to determine available resources, assess what can be avoided or reduced, and decide what to retain (including deductibles, which add up; many people enjoy a low premium until a big deductible hits their first claim). 5.4.2 Implementing the chosen approaches

Advantages and disadvantages of a solution

  • The test is simple: does it appropriately address the need?
  • "Best" may be cheapest, but low cost is not necessarily good value (though it may be the only way to meet the need).
  • Risk management is often the most reasonable compromise: not fully covering all risks can be acceptable if the client understands the consequences.
  • Combinations work, such as a policy with a higher deductible than ideal. "Some coverage is better than no coverage."
  • Exception: when legal requirements apply, buy full required cover and modify other goals instead.
  • Best solutions give the most positive result after cost-benefit analysis and fit the client's ability to implement, so they may be stepped or staggered.

1. According to the textbook, what should be the first consideration when selecting an insurance company?

2. Which agency publishes a "qualified solvency rating" using public records only, at no charge to the insurer?

  2. Moody's
  3. Standard & Poor's
  4. Fitch
Answer

c. S&P publishes two ratings: the claims-paying ability rating (on request) and the qualified solvency rating (public records, free).

3. An insurer has 5 of the 12 NAIC watchlist ratios outside the usual ranges. What follows?

  2. It goes on the watchlist, which may lead to immediate attention, targeted action or no action
  3. Nothing, since fewer than 6 ratios are out of range
  4. It must merge with a stronger insurer
Answer

b. Four or more out-of-range ratios trigger the watchlist; the regulator's response depends on which ratios and by how much.

4. A reinsurer fails to pay its share of claims to the primary insurer. Which risk-based capital category is this?

Answer

Credit risk: default on amounts due from policyholders, reinsurers or creditors. Asset risk would be a default on the insurer's own investments.

5. An insurer starts the year with 2,50,000 policies in force; 30,000 terminate. Calculate the lapse ratio and say what an advisor should do.

Answer

30,000 ÷ 2,50,000 = 12%. That is above the 10% trigger, so investigate why (missed illustrations, high premiums, poor service, or financial trouble), comparing with the industry. Persistency is 88%.

  3. X must be using reinsurance
  4. The gap is normal and can be ignored

b. The textbook says paying 5% or 6% while earning 4.5% "does not make much sense".

7. Which agent quality does the textbook say most often determines an agent's overall quality?

Inclination to service. If an agent will not listen, make the extra effort or execute competently, nothing else compensates.

8. "Draft authority" refers to:

  4. An insurer's power to reject applications

b. It speeds up small claims, up to each agent's limit.

9. Application: Rohan, licensed only for health insurance, takes a life insurance application and first premium from Neha and gives her a receipt. Neha dies two weeks later. Is the insurer likely to pay?

Answer

Yes, most likely. This is apparent (ostensible) authority: Neha had no way of knowing Rohan's limits, and the agent's acts can legally bind the insurer. The insurer may pursue Rohan, but that does not remove its duty to honour the policy.

10. Collecting premiums and ordering medical exams fall under which type of agent authority?

Answer

Implied authority: not written into the agency contract but assumed as a necessary part of doing business.

11. A client wants lifetime cover, wants to vary premium payments, and does not want market exposure. Which policy fits?

  1. Variable life
  2. Whole life
  3. Universal life
  4. Variable universal life
Answer

c. Flexible premium without markets is universal life. VUL adds markets; variable life has a fixed premium.

12. A client needs large cover now, has tight cash flow, and expects income to rise within three to five years; she wants predictable premiums and some cash accumulation. Which policy does the textbook match?

Answer

Modified whole life. The "3 to 5 years" income clue is the giveaway; it starts with a lower premium that later steps up.

13. Which statement about rating agencies is most accurate?

  2. They are unreliable and should be ignored
  3. Despite flaws, they remain the most widely available, most reliable source; use more than one
  4. Every insurer is rated by all four agencies

14. Application: A client's handmade garden shed is worth ₹20,000 and insuring it against collapse would cost ₹9,000 a year. It stores only old garden tools. What should the advisor recommend?

Answer

Retain the risk (skip the insurance). Premiums would equal the shed's value in just over two years, the same logic as the textbook's $1,200 shed with a $600 premium. Reconsider only if valuable contents are stored there or the house itself is at risk.

Reduction. Avoidance is "easiest and most difficult"; retention is simple to assess; transfer is easiest to quantify.

20. India: A policyholder's death claim of ₹40 lakh was rejected 14 months ago. Can the family go to the Insurance Ombudsman now?

Answer

No. The amount is within the ₹50 lakh limit, but a complaint must be filed within one year of the insurer's rejection. Their remaining routes would be the consumer commission or courts (subject to those forums' own limitation rules, which I have not checked).

21. India: Vikram names his brother as nominee on his term policy and leaves a will giving everything to his wife. Is the brother a "beneficial nominee" under Sec 39?

Good customer service; offers the cover needed at a competitive price with good underwriting; price-shop; history; and above all financial solvency and stability.

2. The four main rating agencies and what they rate?

Answer

Standard & Poor's, Moody's, Fitch, A.M. Best. All rate insurers' financial stability and well-being.

3. What is a lapse ratio and how is it used?

Answer

The percentage of in-force policies terminated each year (the textbook links it to persistency). Compare with the industry; 10% or more needs investigation: weak results versus illustrations, high premiums, poor service, or financial trouble.

4. Factors when evaluating an insurance agent?

Answer

Competence and inclination to service; experience, training and education; history in a specialisation; overall reputation.

5. Express versus implied authority?

Answer

Express: written in the agent's contract, defining the exact scope. Implied: not written, assumed as part of doing business in the insurer's name (premiums, applications, medical exams).

6. Is the insurer usually liable when an agent abuses apparent authority?

Answer

Yes. The agent's acts legally bind the insurer; it may press charges against the agent but must still honour the policy.

7. Primary concern of securities and insurance regulators?

Answer

Solvency (prudent management and financial discipline) plus ethical, compliant conduct by people dealing with the public. Insurance is vested in the public interest, so insurers must keep their promises to indemnify future losses.

Chapter 5

1. Why assess an individual's exposure to financial risk?

Answer

You cannot protect against every possible risk; it is impractical and excess insurance drains cash flow needed for other goals.

2. Questions for insuring high-value fine art?

Answer

Professional appraisal? Replace/repair or cash? Antiques at full value or depreciated? New items covered automatically, how much, how long? Covered outside the home (e.g., museum)? Covered in other residences and in transit?

3. Six final expense areas?

Answer

Funeral costs; unpaid medical expenses; outstanding loans and debts; estate-settlement costs; adjustment period fund; miscellaneous.

4. Five ongoing income categories for a surviving spouse and children?

Answer

Dependent income for children; extra income for the spouse while children are home; education; spouse's supplement after children leave and before retirement; supplemental retirement income.

5. Questions when evaluating existing coverage?

Answer

See the checklist in Section 16: purpose, limits, deductibles, duration, beneficiaries and legal documents, overlap and gaps, renewal provisions, health conditions, life adequacy and type, gaps for transfer (liability, professionals, employers, directors), household staff legal requirements, cost-effectiveness and insurer soundness.

6. Steps in a risk management strategy?

Answer

Identify the risks; review (and inspect) the current situation; analyse; select the most appropriate technique; implement; monitor.

1. Needs analysis in three steps p. 183

  • Once life insurance is needed, the next question is how much. Too much is expensive and diverts money from other goals (investments); too little can be disastrous. Hence a rigorous method: needs analysis.
  • It focuses on the dependents' actual income needs if the earner dies. Needs change, so re-examine periodically.

2. Present value of an annuity; ordinary annuity versus annuity due pp. 184-186

PV (ordinary annuity) = PMT × [1 − (1 + i)−n] ÷ i
SymbolMeaning
PVPresent value
PMTPeriodic payment
nNumber of compounding periods
iInterest (discount) rate per period
  • PV turns a stream of payments into one value at a chosen date (not necessarily today, but some time before a stated future date). It is a valuation tool.
  • The formula does not include inflation: it discounts at market interest rates, not the inflation rate.
  • Rearranged, it solves for the PMT that pays off a loan or equals a current price.

Ordinary annuity versus annuity due

Ordinary annuityAnnuity due
Payment timingEnd of each periodBeginning of each period
Formal definitionEach payment belongs to the period before its dateEach payment belongs to the period after its date
Another way to see it—An ordinary annuity whose term starts and ends one period earlier
ExamplesInstalment loans; coupon bonds. Most annuities are ordinaryRent paid at the start of the period
Calculator modeENDBEGIN
Annuity due = Ordinary annuity × (1 + i)   (applies to both PV and FV, so the annuity due is always larger)

ExampleTextbook: $50 a year for 3 years at 7%.

Ordinary50 × [1 − 1.07−3] ÷ 0.07 = 50 × 2.6243$131.22
Annuity due131.22 × 1.07$140.40
Difference140.40 − 131.22$9.18

The two worked equations are images in the PDF, so I rebuilt them; my figures reproduce the textbook's stated difference of 9.18.

Hook"Due = Day one." Rent is due on day one; a loan EMI arrives at month-end. Annuity due = ordinary × (1 + i).

TrapThe textbook warns that an ordinary annuity is sometimes called an "immediate annuity", which is misleading, because "immediate" sounds like payments at the start. Do not confuse this maths term with the immediate annuity product in Chapter 3, Section 16.

5. Effect of inflation on the life insurance payout p. 188

  • The Morrison figures ignore inflation, so the proceeds may prove inadequate when received.
  • The textbook cites the Reserve Bank of Australia: historical inflation of 5.3% from 1951 to 2012.
  • How to cope without predicting inflation: the proceeds are not all needed at once. Invest the balance; interest income helps offset inflation because interest rates have historically been higher than inflation. Interest is taxable, but future tax rates cannot be predicted, so no allowance is made. Other variables: the surviving spouse may go back to work.

Ordinary: 3,60,000 × [1 − 1.06−30] ÷ 0.06 = ₹49,55,339. Annuity due: × 1.06 = ₹52,52,660, about ₹2.97 lakh more.
