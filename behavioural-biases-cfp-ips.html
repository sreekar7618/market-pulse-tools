Investment Planning & Strategy · FPSB India curriculum · 21 biases

Twenty-one predictable ways a rational investor stops being one

Your syllabus splits behavioural finance into three families. This document covers every bias on the list: what it feels like from the inside, a diagram of the mechanism, an Indian example you will actually recognise, the damage it does to a portfolio, and what the exam expects an adviser to do about it.

One rule governs the whole topic

All 21 biases fall into two buckets, and the bucket decides the treatment. This single distinction is worth more marks than the definitions themselves.

Cognitive errors are faults in reasoning — bad statistics, bad memory, bad information handling. The client is trying to think correctly and failing. Give them data, a written process and a checklist, and the behaviour improves. You moderate (correct) a cognitive bias.

Emotional biases come from feeling and impulse, not from faulty logic. Explaining the maths to a loss-averse client does not stop the fear. You largely adapt (accommodate) — you build the plan so the bias cannot do much damage.

Both of your first two families — behavioural (belief perseverance) and information processing — are cognitive errors. Only the third family is emotional.

Jump to any bias. Each entry follows the same shape: the idea in one sentence, the mechanism, an Indian example, the portfolio damage, and the fix.

Family one · six biases · cognitive errors

Behavioural biases: belief perseverance

These six share a single engine. Once a belief is formed, the mind works to keep it alive — by ignoring contrary evidence, updating too slowly, searching only for agreement, or rewriting memory after the fact. The belief is protected; reality is edited. Because the failure is one of reasoning, all six can be moderated with process and evidence.

Cognitive dissonance

Holding a belief and a contradicting fact at the same time is uncomfortable — so the mind quietly discards the fact instead of the belief.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Belief perseverance

How it works

Once you have committed — money, reputation, an opinion said out loud — new information that says you were wrong creates mental discomfort. There are two cheap ways out and one expensive one. The expensive one is changing your mind. The cheap ones show up as two classic sub-behaviours:

  • Selective perception — you register the supporting news and genuinely fail to notice the contradicting news.
  • Selective decision-making — you take a further action that justifies the first one, most often by adding more money to a failing position.

Indian example

The bank that was going to be the next HDFC

An investor buys Yes Bank at around ₹350 on the thesis that it is a high-growth private bank. Then the RBI's asset-quality divergence reports appear, the auditor resigns, and the promoter's pledged shareholding is reported. Rather than revisit the thesis, he decides the auditors are being unreasonably conservative and the promoter will surely raise capital. He stops reading the negative coverage. At ₹120 he buys more, "to average down" — which is only sensible if the original thesis still holds, and its truth is exactly what is in question.

What it does to a portfolio

Losers are held long past the point where the story broke, and fresh capital is poured into the weakest holding rather than the best available opportunity. The portfolio slowly becomes a museum of past decisions the owner is defending.

How to moderate it

  • Write the thesis down before you buy, in one page, and include the falsifier: "I am wrong if gross NPAs cross 5% or the promoter pledges more than 30%."
  • Review holdings against that written falsifier, not against the price.
  • Treat averaging down as a fresh purchase requiring a fresh thesis, not a continuation of the old one.

Do not confuse with confirmation bias. Dissonance is about the discomfort of contradiction and how you dispose of it. Confirmation is about where you go looking for information in the first place. They usually travel together.

Conservatism

New information arrives, and the view moves — but far too little. The prior forecast keeps most of its weight when it deserves very little.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Belief perseverance
  • Under-reaction

How it works

Statistically, this is imperfect Bayesian updating. A good forecaster combines the prior view and the new evidence in proportion to how informative each is. A conservative forecaster keeps the prior nearly intact and treats strong new evidence as a minor adjustment. Two things drive it: the effort of processing complex new data, and the reluctance to admit the earlier view was wrong.

The market-level consequence is under-reaction to news, which is one of the standard explanations for post-earnings-announcement drift — prices continue moving in the direction of the surprise for weeks afterwards because everyone updated too slowly on day one.

Indian example

The target price that will not move

An analyst has a ₹1,200 target on an FMCG company built on 12% volume growth. The quarter arrives: rural volumes fall, palm oil costs spike, and gross margin drops 400 basis points. The structural story has changed. The revised target comes out at ₹1,150 — a token 4% cut, because a 25% cut would be an admission that the original model was wrong. Six months later the stock is at ₹820 and the target has been "revised" four more times in small steps.

What it does to a portfolio

Deteriorating positions are sold late and in slices. Asset allocation stays anchored to a view formed years ago — an investor who decided in 2015 that "IT is a steady 15% grower" carries that assumption through an entirely different demand environment.

How to moderate it

  • Ask the blank-slate question: "If I held no position and saw only today's facts, what would I forecast?" Then use that number.
  • Force yourself to state, before the event, how much your view should change under each outcome.
  • When information is genuinely hard to interpret, get it interpreted rather than defaulting to the old view.

Do not confuse with anchoring. Anchoring fixes on an arbitrary number (the purchase price, the 52-week high). Conservatism clings to a previously formed view or forecast. Both produce under-adjustment; the thing being clung to differs.

Confirmation

You look for evidence that you are right, notice it when it appears, and quietly filter out the rest.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Belief perseverance

How it works

This is a bias of search and weighting. Supporting evidence is sought out, found easily, and accepted at face value. Contrary evidence is either not looked for, or subjected to a much harder test before it is believed. Social media makes it almost frictionless: the follows, the groups and the recommendation algorithm all converge on your existing position within a week.

Indian example

Two ways it shows up

The tip stock. An investor buys a smallcap on a Telegram call. He joins three more groups on the same stock, reads only the bull thread on X, and treats every 5% up-day as proof of the thesis. The two research notes questioning receivables never get opened.

The employer's stock. An Infosys or TCS employee holds a large ESOP position plus fresh purchases. Every internal announcement, client win and appraisal confirms that this is a great company. What he cannot see from inside is the sector-level risk, and that his salary, bonus and portfolio all depend on the same employer.

What it does to a portfolio

Concentration. Confirmation bias is the main psychological route to a portfolio with 60% in one company or one sector, held with total conviction. It also keeps investors in bad positions long after an honest search would have exited.

How to moderate it

  • Write the bear case yourself, in the strongest form you can manage, before you buy.
  • Deliberately read the best-argued opposing view — and give it the same benefit of the doubt you give the bull case.
  • For advisers: appoint a designated dissenter in the review meeting, whose job is to argue against the recommendation.
  • Check exposure at the household level, including salary and employer stock.

Representativeness

Classifying something new by how much it resembles a familiar pattern, instead of by the actual odds.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Belief perseverance
  • Two sub-forms

How it works

The mind classifies fast by resemblance. That is efficient in daily life and disastrous with probabilities, because resemblance ignores how common each category is. Two named sub-forms appear in the syllabus:

  • Base-rate neglect — judging by the stereotype while ignoring how rare the category is. "This company is like a young Titan" ignores that a tiny fraction of consumer companies ever compound like Titan.
  • Sample-size neglect — treating a small sample as if it revealed the underlying quality. Three good quarters is noise; it gets read as skill. The technical name for the error is the law of small numbers.

Indian example

Base rate

A new EV two-wheeler company lists. The pitch: "This is India's Tesla." The resemblance is real — same sector, same founder energy, same growth language. The base rate is not mentioned: of the several hundred auto and auto-component companies listed in India over three decades, a handful produced Tesla-like returns and many did not survive a demand cycle.

Sample size

A smallcap fund tops the one-year chart. Money floods in on the strength of four quarters of data, and the manager is called a star. This is exactly why SEBI insists every advertisement carries the reminder that past performance may not be sustained — the regulator is legislating against sample-size neglect.

What it does to a portfolio

Chasing last year's winning fund, paying growth-stock multiples for companies that merely look like growth stocks, and rotating into whichever theme currently resembles the last big winner.

How to moderate it

  • Look up the base rate before the story. How many companies in this category actually delivered what is being promised?
  • Insist on a sample long enough to matter — for a fund, a full market cycle and ideally 7 to 10 years with the same manager.
  • Ask what is different about this case, not what is similar.

Illusion of control

Believing you can influence an outcome that is, in fact, out of your hands — usually because you are busy doing something.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Belief perseverance

How it works

Effort, choice, familiarity and involvement all raise the felt sense of control even when they change nothing about the odds. People throw dice harder for a high number and gently for a low one. Investors do the same thing with screens, indicators and trades: the activity is real, the influence is not.

It compounds because outcomes in markets are noisy. When a controlled-feeling action is followed by a good outcome — which happens roughly half the time — the sense of control is reinforced.

Indian example

The screen-watcher

A trader with a ₹10 lakh account watches the NSE screen from 9:15 to 3:30, places 20 to 30 intraday trades a day, and has six indicators on the chart. He genuinely believes the constant monitoring is what keeps him safe. What the monitoring actually produces is brokerage, STT, exchange charges, GST and short-term capital gains tax at 20%, all of which are certain, against a return that is not. The same illusion appears in gentler form in the long-term investor who rearranges his portfolio every month "to stay on top of it."

What it does to a portfolio

Over-trading and its full cost stack; under-diversification, because concentrated positions feel more controllable than a diversified one; and comfort with illiquid or unlisted holdings on the grounds that the investor "knows the promoter."

How to moderate it

  • Split every decision into what you control (savings rate, costs, asset allocation, tax, time horizon) and what you do not (next quarter's price). Manage only the first list.
  • Keep a trade log with entry reason and outcome. The record usually dissolves the illusion faster than any argument.
  • Compare the portfolio's return net of all costs and taxes against a plain index fund over the same period.

Do not confuse with overconfidence. Overconfidence is "I predict better than others." Illusion of control is "my actions affect the outcome." A person can be humble about forecasting and still believe that watching the screen protects him.

Hindsight

After the outcome is known, the past looks far more predictable than it was — and you remember having predicted it.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Belief perseverance
  • Memory bias

How it works

Memory is reconstructive. Once you know how the story ended, the mind rebuilds the earlier state of knowledge in a way that fits the ending. The genuine uncertainty you felt at the time is edited out, and the signals that turned out to matter become "obvious" while the equally loud signals that pointed elsewhere are forgotten. Hindsight is dangerous mainly because it manufactures overconfidence: if the past was easy to call, the future must be too.

Indian example

March 2020, remembered from 2021

The Nifty falls from about 12,400 in January 2020 to roughly 7,600 in March. At the time, investors were pulling money out, nobody knew the length of the lockdown, and the recovery path was unknowable. By mid-2021 the same investors describe it as "an obvious once-in-a-decade buying opportunity — everyone knew the market would bounce." Their SIPs, several of which were paused in April 2020, say otherwise. The same rewrite happened after the Adani–Hindenburg episode: the leverage was "always visible", though the stocks had risen for two years while it was visible.

What it does to a portfolio

Inflated belief in one's own forecasting, leading to bigger and more concentrated bets. It also corrupts evaluation: fund managers and advisers are judged on whether the outcome was foreseeable in retrospect rather than on whether their process was sound at the time.

How to moderate it

  • Keep a dated decision journal. Write the forecast, the confidence level and the reasoning at the moment of deciding. Memory cannot then rewrite it.
  • When reviewing any past event, force yourself to list the two or three ways it could plausibly have gone otherwise.
  • Evaluate advisers and managers against what was knowable then, not against what happened.

Do not confuse with outcome bias. Hindsight is about memory — "I predicted this." Outcome bias is about judgement — "it worked out, so it was a good decision." One rewrites the past; the other misgrades it.

Family two · eight biases · cognitive errors

Information processing biases

Here the belief is not the problem — the handling of the data is. Numbers are given the wrong weight, money is sorted into buckets that do not exist, the same fact produces different decisions depending on how it is worded, and the most vivid or most recent evidence crowds out the most relevant. These are still cognitive errors, so they too respond to education, data and written process.

Anchoring and adjustment

An initial number sticks in the mind and every later estimate is pulled towards it, even when the number was arbitrary or is now irrelevant.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Information processing

How it works

Faced with an uncertain quantity, the mind grabs a starting value and adjusts from it. The adjustment is almost always too small, and the starting value need not be meaningful — in the classic experiment, a spun roulette wheel changed people's estimate of an unrelated statistic. In markets, the ready-made anchors are the purchase price, the 52-week high, a round number, and the last analyst target you read.

Indian example

"I will sell when it comes back to my cost"

An investor buys a telecom stock at ₹500. Competitive intensity destroys the business economics and fair value now looks like ₹230. He refuses to sell below ₹500, because that is his number. But ₹500 exists nowhere except in his own transaction history — the company does not know it, the market does not know it, and it carries no information about future value. He holds for six years for a price that only he considers meaningful.

The same anchor works upward: after an IPO lists at ₹1,000 and falls to ₹600, buyers arrive because it is "40% off", anchoring on an issue price that was itself set by a merchant banker.

What it does to a portfolio

Losers held indefinitely, winners sold at arbitrary round numbers, and valuation work that quietly reverse-engineers itself to land near the current market price.

How to moderate it

  • Value the asset before you look at the price, and write the value down.
  • Ask the replacement question: "At today's price, would I buy this today?" If not, the position is a fresh purchase you would not make.
  • Treat the purchase price as what it is — a sunk fact about the past with no bearing on future returns.

Mental accounting

Money is sorted into separate mental pots by where it came from or what it is for, and treated differently in each pot — although rupees are identical.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Information processing
  • Fungibility ignored

How it works

Money is fungible: a rupee of salary, a rupee of bonus and a rupee of lottery winnings buy exactly the same things. Mental accounting breaks that. Each pot gets its own risk appetite and its own rules, and the pots are never netted against each other. Shefrin and Statman built Behavioural Portfolio Theory on this observation: real investors hold a layered pyramid — a safety layer, an income layer, an aspiration layer — rather than one integrated portfolio.

The critical technical consequence is that correlations between layers are never considered. The investor optimises each pot in isolation and ends up with a whole that is inefficient, however sensible each part looks.

Indian example

Three pots, one household

Source-based. The ₹1 lakh Diwali bonus goes into weekly options on the Nifty, because it is "found money". The salary goes into a fixed deposit, because it is "hard-earned". The same person is simultaneously extremely risk-seeking and extremely risk-averse.

Purpose-based. ₹6 lakh sits in a savings account at around 3% labelled "for the child's education in 2031", while a personal loan at 14% runs in parallel. Netting the two would earn a guaranteed 11%, but the education money is emotionally untouchable.

Income versus capital. A retiree will spend the dividend but never sell a share, so she loads the portfolio with high-dividend PSU stocks and ignores total return — even though a systematic withdrawal from a balanced fund would be better diversified and more tax-efficient.

What it does to a portfolio

Overall allocation drifts far from what any single risk profile would justify, cash sits idle beside expensive debt, and portfolios tilt to dividend-paying stocks for reasons unrelated to their merit.

How to moderate it

  • Draw one household balance sheet. Goals may stay separate on paper; the asset allocation is decided once, at the top.
  • Net idle cash against expensive borrowing before adding any new investment.
  • Teach total return: a rupee of dividend and a rupee of capital gain spend identically, and in India the dividend is taxed at slab rate while long-term capital gains are not.

Framing

The same information, worded two ways, produces two different decisions.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Information processing
  • Mis-selling risk

How it works

Choices are evaluated against a reference point supplied by the wording. Present an outcome as a gain and people turn risk-averse; present the identical outcome as a loss and the same people turn risk-seeking. Nothing about the underlying numbers changes. Narrow framing is the related error of judging one investment in isolation instead of by what it does to the whole portfolio.

For your exam, note the professional implication: a risk-tolerance questionnaire is itself a frame. Ask "how much of your ₹50 lakh would you accept losing in a bad year?" and you get one answer; ask "what portfolio value would you be uncomfortable falling below?" and the same client gives a different, often inconsistent one.

Indian example

Two honest sentences, two decisions

"This fund has beaten a fixed deposit in 8 of the last 10 rolling five-year periods." Sounds compelling.
"This fund has failed to beat a fixed deposit in 2 of the last 10 rolling five-year periods." Sounds risky. Identical fact.

Insurance runs on the same mechanism: an insurer advertises a 98% claim settlement ratio, never "2 in 100 claims are rejected." And traditional endowment or ULIP plans are framed as "you get your money back plus a bonus", a frame under which nobody computes the roughly 4 to 6% internal rate of return that would be obvious if it were quoted as a CAGR.

What it does to a portfolio

Mis-stated risk tolerance, purchases of poor products that were framed attractively, and rejection of good products described in loss terms.

How to moderate it

  • Restate every proposition in both directions before deciding. If your answer flips, the frame was doing the work.
  • Convert everything to a common neutral unit: rupees and CAGR, net of costs and tax.
  • Judge each investment by its effect on the total portfolio, not on its own.

Availability

Estimating how likely something is by how easily an example comes to mind — so vivid, recent and heavily advertised events feel far more probable than they are.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Information processing
  • Four sub-forms

How it works

Ease of recall is used as a proxy for frequency. Four sources of distorted recall are named in the literature:

  • Retrievability — what is easiest to remember, usually because it was dramatic or heavily repeated.
  • Categorisation — you can only search the categories you have. Ask for "good investments" and most Indian investors search a list containing property, gold, FDs and a few stocks; REITs, InvITs and global equity are not in the search space at all.
  • Narrow range of experience — a software engineer overestimates the prospects of IT; a Surat diamond trader overestimates his own trade.
  • Resonance — you overweight what matches your own preferences and identity.

Indian example

Two directions, same bias

Too optimistic. During a hot IPO stretch, every listing gain is on television and in the family group. Retail investors conclude that IPOs are near-free money. The listing losses of the same period are not memorable and are not recalled.

Too pessimistic. An investor who watched the 2008 crash — or whose father lost money in the Harshad Mehta era — keeps 100% of his savings in FDs and gold for thirty years. A single vivid memory outweighs a century of long-horizon equity data, and inflation quietly does the damage the crash never got to do.

What it does to a portfolio

Fund and stock selection driven by advertising and news rather than data; whole asset classes excluded because they are not in the recall set; and permanent over- or under-weighting to equity depending on which memory happens to be dominant.

How to moderate it

  • Use base rates and long data series rather than recalled instances. Rolling returns over 15 years, not the last headline.
  • Work from a written selection checklist so the universe considered is the real universe, not the memorable one.
  • Deliberately look for the un-newsworthy cases: the IPOs that listed flat, the funds that quietly closed.

Do not confuse with recency. Availability is about ease of recall from any point in time — a 1992 memory can dominate. Recency is specifically about the latest data getting the most weight.

Ambiguity aversion

Preferring a known risk to an unknown one — choosing the bet whose odds you can see, even when the unseen bet is objectively at least as good.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Information processing
  • Ellsberg paradox

How it works

There is a difference between risk (outcomes unknown, probabilities known) and ambiguity (probabilities themselves unknown). People pay a real premium to avoid the second. In Ellsberg's experiment, subjects prefer drawing from an urn with a visible 50–50 mix over an urn with an unknown mix, and — revealingly — they prefer it whichever colour they are betting on, which is logically impossible if their beliefs are consistent.

Note that some texts, including the CFA classification, place ambiguity aversion among emotional biases because the discomfort of the unknown is felt rather than reasoned. Your FPSB list puts it under information processing. Follow the FPSB grouping in the paper, and be aware the treatment sits somewhere in between: education genuinely reduces ambiguity, which is why it behaves like a cognitive error in practice.

Indian example

Home bias, and the assets that never get bought

An investor holds 100% of his equity in Indian stocks. Indian equity is not less risky than global equity — it is simply less ambiguous to him. He reads the Indian news, knows the companies, understands the tax. Adding a global fund would reduce portfolio volatility through lower correlation, but the unfamiliarity feels like danger rather than diversification.

The same investor avoids REITs and InvITs entirely — not after analysing them, but because the structure is unfamiliar — and keeps the money in FDs and gold, where the outcome distribution feels legible.

What it does to a portfolio

Severe home bias, entire asset classes left out, over-allocation to the familiar few, and a portfolio that is less diversified than its owner believes.

How to moderate it

  • Education is the direct antidote: ambiguity is, by definition, missing information.
  • Start with a small allocation. Familiarity grows with exposure, and the position size limits the cost of the learning.
  • Show the correlation and volatility numbers for the whole portfolio with and without the unfamiliar asset, so the diversification benefit is visible.

Self-attribution

Successes are credited to your own skill; failures are blamed on bad luck, bad markets or bad advice.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Information processing
  • Feeds overconfidence

How it works

Two protective habits run together: self-enhancing, taking undue credit for good outcomes, and self-protecting, refusing responsibility for bad ones. The result is an accounting system in which the ledger can only show skill. Because losses never get attributed to the decision-maker, no learning takes place, and confidence rises with experience regardless of results.

Indian example

The 2021 portfolio, reviewed in 2022

In FY 2021, an investor's smallcap portfolio is up 90%. He concludes he has a talent for picking stocks — never mind that the Smallcap 250 index rose about the same amount, so his selection added nothing. In 2022 the portfolio falls 30% and the explanation changes: the US Fed, the Russia–Ukraine war, FII selling. Both years were driven mostly by the market. Only one was attributed to it.

What it does to a portfolio

A steady build-up of overconfidence, which then produces bigger position sizes, more trading and less diversification. It also blocks the feedback loop that would otherwise correct every other bias on this list.

How to moderate it

  • Do proper attribution: separate the market return (beta), the sector effect and your actual selection. Ask whether the portfolio beat the Nifty 500 or merely rode it.
  • For every winning position, write down honestly how much was the thesis and how much was the market.
  • For every losing position, write down the part that was your decision before naming anything external.

Note the classification. Your FPSB list places self-attribution under information processing. The CFA framework treats it as a belief-perseverance bias. Either way it is a cognitive error, and the treatment is the same.

Outcome

Judging the quality of a decision by how it turned out, rather than by how sound it was when it was made.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Information processing

How it works

In any activity with a large luck component, good decisions sometimes lose and bad decisions sometimes win. Outcome bias collapses that distinction. It matters more than it sounds, because it determines what people copy: reckless strategies that happened to pay off get imitated, and sound processes that had a bad run get abandoned at exactly the wrong moment.

Indian example

The friend who tripled his emergency fund

In March 2020 a colleague puts his entire ₹8 lakh emergency fund into one smallcap. By 2021 it is worth ₹25 lakh, and the office decides he is brilliant. He was not. He took an undiversified, unhedged, liquidity-destroying bet with money reserved for a job loss, during a pandemic, and the dice came up right. Had he needed the money in May 2020, the same decision would have been catastrophic. Good outcome, bad decision.

The mirror image: a well-diversified investor with 60% equity and 40% debt underperforms in 2021 and is told he is too cautious. Sound decision, unremarkable outcome.

What it does to a portfolio

Risky behaviour is rewarded and imitated. Fund managers are fired after one weak year and hired after one strong one — which is, mechanically, selling low and buying high.

How to moderate it

  • Grade the process separately from the result. Was the information available, the position sized sensibly, the risk understood?
  • Evaluate over full market cycles and on risk-adjusted measures, not on one year of absolute return.
  • Ask what would have happened in the other plausible states of the world, not just the one that occurred.

Distinguish the trio. Hindsight: "I knew this would happen." Outcome: "It happened, so the decision was good." Self-attribution: "It happened because of me." Examiners like to test these three together.

Recency

The most recent stretch of data gets almost all the weight, and is then projected forward as if it were the norm.

  • Cognitive error
  • Moderate it
  • Information processing
  • Extrapolation

How it works

Recall favours the end of a sequence, so the last two or three years become the mental model of "normal". This is the engine of every performance-chasing cycle: recent returns attract flows, flows push prices higher, higher prices make the recent returns look even more convincing, until the cycle breaks. It also quietly corrupts financial plans, because the return assumption used for a thirty-year projection gets set by the last three years.

Indian example

The smallcap cycle of 2023–24

The smallcap index delivers an extraordinary run through FY 2024. Inflows into smallcap funds surge to record levels — precisely at the point of highest valuation. The regulator becomes concerned enough that AMCs are required to run and publish liquidity stress tests, and several funds voluntarily restrict lump-sum inflows. Investors arriving at that moment had a three-year mental model of smallcaps and no memory of 2018, when the same category fell more than 25% while the Nifty was up.

The planning version is quieter and more damaging: a client insists on 18% for his retirement projection because that is what the last five years delivered. The plan then requires 18% to work.

What it does to a portfolio

Buying high and selling low with great conviction, allocation drifting towards whatever just performed, and financial plans built on assumptions that cannot be sustained.

How to moderate it

  • Use 10 and 15-year rolling returns rather than trailing one and three-year numbers.
  • Rebalance on a written rule — annually, or on a band such as 5% drift — so the decision does not depend on how the recent past feels.
  • Use deliberately conservative return assumptions in projections. A plan that works at 10% and delights at 13% is safer than one that needs 15%.

Family three · seven biases · emotional

Emotional biases

These arise from feeling and impulse rather than faulty reasoning, which is why explaining the mathematics rarely fixes them. A client who understands loss aversion perfectly still panics in a 30% drawdown. The professional response shifts from correcting to accommodating: build the plan, the products and the review process so that the bias has little room to cause harm — and moderate only where the bias genuinely threatens the client's ability to meet essential goals.

Loss aversion

A loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equivalent gain feels good — so decisions are organised around avoiding the realisation of losses.

  • Emotional bias
  • Adapt to it
  • Prospect theory
  • Disposition effect

How it works

Kahneman and Tversky's value function is defined over gains and losses from a reference point, not over final wealth, and it is markedly steeper on the loss side. Three consequences follow, and all three are examinable:

  • The disposition effect — selling winners too early to lock in the pleasure of a gain, and holding losers too long to avoid realising the pain of a loss. Precisely backwards, both for returns and for tax.
  • Risk-seeking in the loss domain — once underwater, people take bigger risks to get back to even than they would ever take from a standing start.
  • Myopic loss aversion — the more often you check the portfolio, the more losing days you see, and the less equity you can tolerate holding.

Indian example

The two positions in the same demat account

An investor holds Bajaj Finance, up 22%, and a small NBFC bought at ₹80, now ₹19. He sells the winner — "book profits, it has run up" — and holds the loser, waiting to "get back to ₹80". Two years later the compounder has doubled again and the loser is at ₹11. He has also paid 20% short-term capital gains tax on the one position he chose to close.

The myopic version: an investor checking his app four times a day sees a red screen on roughly 45% of days. He concludes he cannot handle equity and moves to 30% equity at age 34 — a decision driven entirely by his checking frequency, not by his time horizon.

What it does to a portfolio

The disposition effect drags returns and raises the tax bill. The equity allocation ends up far below what the time horizon justifies. Losing positions accumulate and never leave.

How to adapt to it

  • Reduce the checking frequency. Quarterly reviews rather than a daily app habit — this single change does more than any explanation.
  • Report at the portfolio level rather than position by position, so individual red numbers do not drive decisions.
  • Pre-commit in writing to the exit rule at the time of purchase, when no loss is being felt.
  • Use tax-loss harvesting to reframe selling a loser as a deliberate, beneficial action rather than an admission of failure.

Overconfidence

Rating your own knowledge, judgement and forecasting ability higher than the evidence supports.

  • Emotional bias
  • Adapt to it
  • Two forms

How it works

Two distinct forms are named in the syllabus:

  • Prediction overconfidence — the confidence interval is far too narrow. Asked for a 90% confident range, most people give a range that contains the true answer about half the time. In portfolio terms, downside risk is systematically understated.
  • Certainty overconfidence — probabilities are set too high. What is called "an 80% chance" turns out right closer to 60% of the time.

Add the better-than-average effect — most drivers, and most investors, rate themselves above the median — and self-attribution bias, which ensures that experience only ever confirms the self-assessment. Overconfidence is classified as emotional because it is fundamentally about protecting self-esteem, not about arithmetic.

Indian example

The F&O statistic everyone has read and nobody applies

SEBI's studies of individual traders in the equity derivatives segment have repeatedly found that the large majority — roughly nine in ten — lose money, with sizeable average losses. The number is widely reported and widely known. Every new entrant has seen it, and concludes that it describes the other nine. That is overconfidence in its purest measurable form.

The quieter version: a senior professional with 55% of his net worth in his employer's stock plus his salary from the same company, confident he understands the business better than the market does.

What it does to a portfolio

Excessive trading and its costs, position sizes that ignore the chance of being wrong, under-diversification, and a persistent underestimate of how bad a bad year can be.

How to adapt to it

  • Impose hard position limits in writing — no single stock above a set percentage — so the bias cannot express itself in size.
  • Keep a trade log and review realised performance against a benchmark. Confronting the record works better than confronting the belief.
  • Ask for forecasts as ranges with an explicit confidence level, then check how often the outcome falls inside them.
  • Where conviction is high, allow it — but in a small, ring-fenced satellite portfolio rather than in the core.

Distinguish from optimism. Overconfidence is about my ability: "I can pick the winner." Optimism is about my fortune: "things will work out for me." One is a claim of skill, the other a claim on luck.

Optimism

Believing that good things are more likely to happen to you, and bad things more likely to happen to other people.

  • Emotional bias
  • Adapt to it
  • Under-insurance

How it works

People consistently rate their own chance of illness, job loss, accident or business failure below the population average, and their own chance of above-average returns above it. This is not a statistical error the person would correct if shown the data — it is a self-protective stance that makes the future bearable. That is why it sits in the emotional family.

In financial planning, optimism does its damage in the assumptions rather than in the portfolio. The asset allocation may be perfectly sensible while the plan around it quietly assumes nothing goes wrong.

Indian example

The plan that assumes a smooth life

A 38-year-old software professional in Hyderabad has a ₹5 lakh employer health cover and no personal policy, because he is fit and has never been hospitalised. He has no separate term cover beyond the employer's, one month of expenses in the bank, and a retirement plan built on 15% returns, no career break, and expenses growing at 5% while healthcare in India has been inflating considerably faster. Every one of those assumptions is defensible on its own. Together they describe a life in which nothing goes wrong.

What it does to a portfolio

Chronic under-insurance, an inadequate emergency fund, a savings rate set too low because future income is assumed to rise, and projections that only work in the good case.

How to adapt to it

  • Stress-test the plan in front of the client: run job loss, a five-year period of poor returns, a serious medical event, and living to 95.
  • Use deliberately conservative assumptions — lower returns, higher inflation, longer life. If the plan survives those, optimism costs nothing.
  • Present insurance and the emergency fund as the price of being allowed to take equity risk elsewhere, not as pessimism.

Self-control

Knowing what the long term requires and consuming today anyway, because present satisfaction outranks a distant goal.

  • Emotional bias
  • Adapt to it
  • Hyperbolic discounting

How it works

People discount the future steeply and inconsistently. A rupee today is valued far more than a rupee next year, and — the crucial part — the discount rate itself falls with distance, so preferences reverse. Every January, saving from March feels easy; every March, it does not. The failure is not one of knowledge. The client can recite the compounding argument perfectly while not acting on it.

Indian example

The SIP that starts next year, every year

A 30-year-old earning ₹18 lakh knows he should invest ₹25,000 a month for retirement. He starts "after the wedding", then "after the car loan", then "after the house". Meanwhile a ₹9 lakh car has been financed on EMI and the annual bonus has funded a holiday three years running.

The arithmetic he already understands: ₹25,000 a month from age 30 to 60 at 11% builds roughly ₹7 crore. Starting the same SIP at 40 builds roughly ₹2.2 crore. Ten years of delay costs about two-thirds of the corpus — and the ten years cost him a car and three holidays.

The second-order damage: realising the shortfall at 48, he moves aggressively into concentrated equity to catch up, taking exactly the wrong risk at the wrong time.

What it does to a portfolio

A savings rate below what the goals require, retirement funded last, and excessive risk taken late in an attempt to make up lost ground.

How to adapt to it

  • Automate and remove the monthly decision: SIP dated within a day or two of salary credit, before the money is available to spend.
  • Use commitment devices — EPF, NPS and PPF work partly because the money is genuinely hard to withdraw.
  • Step-up the SIP automatically with the annual increment, so a rise in income does not become a rise in consumption by default.
  • Give the discretionary impulse a legitimate home: a fixed, capped "spend it" allocation reduces the pressure on the disciplined part.

Status quo

Leaving things as they are, because doing nothing requires no decision and carries no felt responsibility.

  • Emotional bias
  • Adapt to it
  • Default effect

How it works

Change requires effort, a decision, and the acceptance that the outcome is now your doing. Inaction requires none of these. So whatever is currently in place wins by default, and the strength of the default is enormous — it is the single most reliable finding in behavioural policy, and it is why automatic enrolment transforms pension participation.

The important point for an adviser is that status quo bias is invisible. Nothing happens, nobody complains, and a portfolio quietly becomes unsuitable over a decade.

Indian example

The portfolio that was set once

A client's holdings: two endowment policies bought in 2011 by a relative in the insurance business, a dividend-paying fund chosen from a 2013 magazine list, and the NPS Tier-I allocation left on whichever option was selected on the enrolment form. Nothing here was ever re-chosen. The endowment plans yield around 5%, the fund has drifted from its mandate, and the NPS equity share has never been revisited across two salary doublings and one child. Ask why, and the honest answer is that no moment ever arrived that demanded a decision.

What it does to a portfolio

Allocation drifts far from target because rebalancing never happens; legacy products stay long past their usefulness; and the risk profile silently diverges from the client's actual circumstances.

How to adapt to it

  • Make the desirable action the default: auto-rebalance, auto step-up, automatic annual review with a scheduled date.
  • Create the decision moment deliberately — a calendar review turns "no decision" into an explicit choice.
  • Change in small, staged steps rather than one large restructuring, which is easier to postpone.

Distinguish the inaction trio. Status quo: "I never got around to it." Endowment: "I don't want to give it up — it's mine." Regret aversion: "What if I sell and it goes up?" All three produce identical behaviour and need different conversations.

Endowment

Something becomes more valuable to you simply because you own it.

  • Emotional bias
  • Adapt to it
  • Ownership premium

How it works

In the classic experiment, people given a mug demand roughly twice what people without one are willing to pay for it. Ownership itself shifts the valuation. Two forces drive it: loss aversion, since giving up what you hold is coded as a loss, and the emotional history attached to the object — who gave it to you, what it represents, what selling it would say about you.

Indian example

The shares that came from grandfather

A client inherits a large holding in an old public-sector company that his grandfather bought in 1978. It is now 45% of his portfolio, has underperformed the Nifty for fifteen years, and pays a dividend taxed at his slab rate. He will not sell — not because he has valued the business and disagrees with the market, but because they are "grandfather's shares". The same reasoning protects ancestral land that yields nothing, family gold that is never used, and ESOPs held years after vesting because they represent the work.

What it does to a portfolio

Dangerous concentration in inherited or granted assets, a large illiquid share of net worth, and rebalancing that can never be completed because one large holding is off-limits.

How to adapt to it

  • Ask the swap question: "If this arrived today as ₹40 lakh in cash, would you buy this holding with it?" A no makes the position a fresh, unjustified purchase.
  • Sell in stages over several years rather than all at once — this respects the attachment while reducing the risk, and manages capital gains.
  • Separate the memory from the asset. Retain a token holding, or redirect the proceeds to something that honours the source explicitly.
  • Where the emotional cost of selling is genuinely high and the client's essential goals are safe without it, accommodating the holding is a legitimate professional choice.

Regret aversion

Avoiding a decision because of how bad it would feel if it turned out wrong — which usually means avoiding action altogether.

  • Emotional bias
  • Adapt to it
  • Drives herding

How it works

The anticipated pain of a bad outcome is not symmetric between acting and not acting. Errors of commission — something you did that went wrong — hurt considerably more than errors of omission — something you failed to do. Since inaction feels cheaper, the default becomes paralysis.

It is also the main psychological driver of herding. Being wrong alongside everyone else produces far less regret than being wrong alone, so following the crowd is emotionally cheap even when it is financially expensive.

Indian example

The lump sum that never gets deployed

A client sells a property and holds ₹60 lakh in his savings account. The Nifty is near an all-time high. He will not invest, because if the market falls 15% next month he will have to live with having done that. He also will not commit to staying out, because a rally would be its own regret. Two years pass. The money earns 3% while inflation runs at 5%, and the guaranteed real loss produces no regret at all, because nobody ever decided on it.

The herding version: applying to an IPO because the whole office is applying. If it lists down, the loss is shared, and shared losses barely register as mistakes.

What it does to a portfolio

Large cash drag and delayed deployment; losers retained because selling would confirm the error; herding into whatever is currently crowded; and an allocation more conservative than the goals can afford.

How to adapt to it

  • Remove the single big decision. A systematic transfer plan over 6 to 12 months means no one date can be the wrong date — this is the standard, and highly effective, accommodation.
  • Write an investment policy statement in advance, so later actions are the execution of an earlier decision rather than fresh choices to be regretted.
  • Name the cost of inaction explicitly, in rupees, so the omission becomes as visible as the commission would be.

The pairs that cost marks

Most exam errors are not failures of recall — they are collisions between biases that produce identical behaviour. Learn the distinguishing question for each cluster.

ClusterWhat separates themThe question that decides it
Cognitive dissonance / Confirmation / Conservatism Dissonance disposes of a contradicting fact. Confirmation controls where you look. Conservatism keeps an old forecast almost unchanged when new facts arrive. Is the client rejecting a fact, avoiding it, or acknowledging it and barely moving?
Hindsight / Outcome / Self-attribution Hindsight rewrites what you believed. Outcome misgrades the decision using the result. Self-attribution assigns the cause to yourself when it suits. Is the claim "I knew it", "it worked so it was smart", or "it worked because of me"?
Anchoring / Conservatism Anchoring clings to a specific number, often arbitrary. Conservatism clings to a previously formed view. Is there a number being defended, or a conclusion?
Availability / Recency / Representativeness Availability uses ease of recall. Recency specifically overweights the latest data. Representativeness uses resemblance to a category. Is the driver vividness, timing, or similarity?
Overconfidence / Optimism / Illusion of control Overconfidence overrates ability. Optimism overrates fortune. Illusion of control overrates influence. Is the claim about my skill, my luck, or my effect on the outcome?
Status quo / Endowment / Regret aversion All three produce inaction. Status quo is inertia, endowment is attachment to ownership, regret aversion is fear of the consequence of acting. "I never got round to it", "it's mine", or "what if I'm wrong"?
Loss aversion / Risk aversion Risk aversion is a stable preference for certainty. Loss aversion is asymmetry around a reference point — and it makes people risk-seeking once they are underwater. Is the client avoiding uncertainty, or avoiding the realisation of a loss?
Mental accounting / Framing Mental accounting sorts money into pots. Framing changes the decision through wording. Related, but one is about the money's label and the other about the question's wording. Is the money being segregated, or the question being worded?

The adviser's decision

When to moderate and when to adapt

Naming the bias is half the answer. The other half is what you do about it, and the profession's guideline turns on two variables: whether the bias is cognitive or emotional, and how much room the client's wealth gives for error. The formal version of the second variable is standard-of-living risk — the chance that the client fails to meet essential lifestyle needs.

Why adapting is not surrender

A theoretically optimal portfolio the client abandons in the first drawdown returns less than a slightly conservative one he holds for twenty years. Accommodation buys adherence, and adherence is worth more than the last few basis points of efficiency.

The limit of adapting

Accommodation stops where essential goals are threatened. If a 45-year-old with an under-funded retirement wants 80% in fixed deposits because equity frightens him, adapting fully means agreeing to a plan that fails. Here you moderate — with staged entry, education and stress-tested projections — even though the bias is emotional.

Practical sequence in a client meeting

  • Identify the behaviour, not the label. Clients do not describe themselves as loss-averse.
  • Classify: cognitive or emotional.
  • Assess standard-of-living risk against the client's goals.
  • Choose moderate or adapt, and write the chosen response into the investment policy statement so it survives the next market shock.

Self-test · fourteen cases

Name the bias

Read the case, decide before you open it. The answer gives the bias, the family, and the reason the nearest alternative is wrong — which is where the marks actually sit.

Ravi bought a pharma stock at ₹640. It is now ₹310 and the company has lost its largest US approval. He says he will exit "the day it touches ₹640 again".

Anchoring and adjustment — information processing, cognitive, moderate it.

The sell decision is tied to his own purchase price, a number with no bearing on the company's value. Not loss aversion alone: the giveaway is the specific number being defended rather than the general reluctance to book a loss. In practice both are usually present, and a strong answer says so.

Priya keeps ₹8 lakh in a savings account earning 3% for her daughter's college fund in 2032, while paying 13% on a car loan. She says the education money cannot be touched.

Mental accounting — information processing, cognitive, moderate it.

Money is fungible; the pots are mental, the 10% spread is real. Netting the two would earn a guaranteed 10% with no risk. Not status quo — she is actively defending the segregation, not merely failing to act.

An investor's smallcap portfolio rose 85% in the year the smallcap index rose 80%. He credits his research. The next year he is down 28% and blames FII outflows.

Self-attribution — information processing in the FPSB list, cognitive, moderate it.

Self-enhancing on the way up, self-protecting on the way down. Not overconfidence itself — overconfidence is the result; self-attribution is the accounting method that produces it. Attribution analysis against the benchmark is the practical fix.

A client has held ₹60 lakh from a property sale in his savings account for two years. He will not invest at an all-time high and will not commit to staying out either.

Regret aversion — emotional, adapt to it.

Both branches carry anticipated regret, so he stays at the fork. The standard accommodation is an STP over 6 to 12 months, which removes the single decision that could be wrong. Not status quo: he is not failing to notice, he is actively unable to choose.

After watching three friends make listing gains, an investor applies to every IPO, convinced this is the easiest money in the market.

Availability — information processing, cognitive, moderate it.

Probability is being estimated from the vivid, easily recalled cases. The flat and negative listings are not remembered because they were never discussed. Recency is a defensible second answer if the emphasis is on the last few months specifically; availability is better here because the driver is memorability, not timing.

A 41-year-old with two children has no term insurance and one month of expenses saved. He is fit, has never been ill, and expects his income to keep rising.

Optimism — emotional, adapt to it.

Bad outcomes are things that happen to other people. Not overconfidence — nothing here is a claim about his skill or judgement, only about his fortune. The response is to stress-test the plan against job loss and a medical event rather than to argue about probabilities.

An analyst's ₹1,400 target survives a quarter in which margins collapse and guidance is withdrawn. The revised target is ₹1,340.

Conservatism — belief perseverance, cognitive, moderate it.

Strong new evidence produces a token adjustment. Not anchoring: what is being defended is a forecast built from a model, not an arbitrary number. The blank-slate test — "what would I forecast with no prior position?" — is the correcting question.

A client will not sell a PSU holding that is 40% of his portfolio and has lagged the index for a decade, because his grandfather bought it in 1978.

Endowment — emotional, adapt to it.

Value is attached to ownership and its history rather than to the business. Not status quo, which is inertia without attachment. Use the swap test — "would you buy this with the cash today?" — and sell in stages over several years.

A colleague put his entire emergency fund into one smallcap in March 2020 and tripled it. The office now treats him as an expert and copies his trades.

Outcome bias — information processing, cognitive, moderate it.

The decision is being graded by its result. It was undiversified, unhedged and used money reserved against job loss during a pandemic — a poor decision with a good outcome. Not hindsight, which would be the colleague claiming he had predicted the recovery.

An investor sold Bajaj Finance after a 20% gain but has held a stock down 78% for four years. He describes himself as patient.

Loss aversion, specifically the disposition effect — emotional, adapt to it.

Gains are realised early to lock in pleasure; losses are carried to avoid pain. It also costs him tax, since the realised gain was short-term. Adapt by reviewing at portfolio level, reducing checking frequency, and pre-committing to exit rules at purchase.

A client rejects a global equity fund. He has not analysed it; he says he does not understand foreign markets and prefers to stay with what he knows.

Ambiguity aversion — information processing in the FPSB list, cognitive, moderate it.

The unfamiliar is being treated as riskier rather than as merely unknown, producing home bias. Education is the direct antidote because ambiguity is missing information. Note that the CFA framework classifies this as emotional; the FPSB grouping is what your paper follows.

A client's NPS allocation, chosen on the enrolment form in 2012, has never been changed across two promotions, a marriage and a child.

Status quo — emotional, adapt to it.

No decision was ever taken to keep it; the default simply won. Not endowment, since there is no attachment to the specific allocation. The fix is structural: make the good option the default and schedule a review date that forces an explicit choice.

An investor moves ₹12 lakh into smallcap funds in early 2024 after three exceptional years, expecting the run to continue.

Recency — information processing, cognitive, moderate it.

The most recent stretch has become the model of normal and is being extrapolated. Note the mechanism: the money arrives at the highest valuation and therefore the lowest expected return. Use 10 and 15-year rolling returns and a written rebalancing rule.

A trader watches the screen from open to close, runs six indicators, and places 25 trades a day. He says the constant monitoring is what protects his capital.

Illusion of control — belief perseverance, cognitive, moderate it.

Effort and involvement are being mistaken for influence over the outcome. Overconfidence is a reasonable partial answer, but the specific claim here is about control through activity, not superior forecasting. Split the decision into what he controls — costs, sizing, allocation — and what he does not.
