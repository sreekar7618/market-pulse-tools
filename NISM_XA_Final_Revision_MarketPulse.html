Module 1 Personal Financial Planning 37 marks · Ch 1–4 · highest weightage

Financial planning — concept, need, scope

Chapter 1

Definition: the process of meeting life goals through the proper management of finances. Goals — not products — are the starting point.

Six-step process (this sequence is asked directly):

  1. Establish and define the client relationship (scope, terms, fees)
  2. Gather client data and determine goals
  3. Analyse and evaluate the client's financial status
  4. Develop and present the plan / recommendations
  5. Implement the recommendations
  6. Monitor and review

Scope: cash flow & budgeting, risk management & insurance, investment planning, tax planning, retirement planning, estate planning.

Sequence hook — R.G.A.D.I.M.: Relationship → Gather → Analyse → Develop → Implement → Monitor. Note that implementation comes after presentation, and monitoring is a step, not an afterthought.
"Financial advisory" vs "execution" — advice is the recommendation; execution is placing the transaction. SEBI treats these as separable roles. A financial plan is not a product recommendation list.

Cash flow, budgeting and contingency

Chapter 3.1–3.5, 3.8

  • Inflows: salary, business income, rent, interest, dividends, capital gains, maturity proceeds.
  • Outflows: fixed / mandatory (EMIs, rent, premiums, school fees, taxes) vs variable / discretionary (travel, dining, shopping).
  • Surplus = inflows − outflows. Surplus is what funds goals. A plan with no surplus is not a plan.
  • Contingency reserve: 3–6 months of expenses (not income), parked in liquid assets. Higher for single-income households, business owners, unstable jobs.
Contingency is sized against essential monthly expenses including EMIs, never against monthly income. And it is emergency money — it is not "short-term investment", so returns are secondary to instant access.

Budgeting sequence: record actual spends → categorise → set targets → monitor variance → revise. Pay-yourself-first: route savings out on salary day, budget the remainder.

Debt: purpose, leverage and responsible borrowing

Chapter 4.1–4.5

  • Good debt funds an appreciating or income-producing asset (home, education, business). Bad debt funds consumption (credit card revolve, personal loan for a holiday).
  • Leverage amplifies both directions. Borrowing at 9% to earn 12% adds value; the same borrowing when the asset returns 4% destroys it.
  • Debt counselling — restructure, consolidate, prioritise, negotiate, and change the behaviour that created the debt.
Financial leverage → effect of debt in the capital structure Operating leverage → effect of fixed costs on operating profit Combined leverage → Operating × Financial
A leveraged household is a see-saw with a longer arm — the same push moves you further, up or down.

EMI vs tenure, prepay vs invest, faster payoff

Chapter 4.11–4.15 · classic caselet

When the floating rate rises the lender offers two options:

Keep EMI, extend tenureKeep tenure, raise EMI
Monthly cash flowUnchangedTightens
Total interest paidHigherLower
SuitsStretched budgetsAnyone with surplus

Prepay or invest? Compare like with like:

Prepay if: post-tax cost of the loan > post-tax expected return on the investment Invest if: post-tax expected return > post-tax cost of the loan
  • Prepaying is a risk-free, guaranteed return equal to the loan rate — the investment side must beat it on a risk-adjusted basis, not just on paper.
  • Early prepayment saves far more interest than late prepayment, because interest is front-loaded.
  • Avalanche = clear the highest-interest debt first → mathematically optimal. Snowball = clear the smallest balance first → behaviourally motivating.
If the loan carries a tax deduction (e.g. housing loan interest), use the effective post-tax rate, not the headline rate, before deciding to prepay.

Market structure and intermediaries

Chapter 5.4

  • Money market — maturity up to 1 year, RBI-regulated. Capital market — beyond 1 year, SEBI-regulated.
  • Primary market — issuer raises fresh capital, money goes to the company. Secondary market — investors trade among themselves, the company receives nothing.
  • Intermediaries: merchant bankers, underwriters, stock brokers, depositories (NSDL, CDSL) and DPs, RTAs, custodians, clearing corporations, credit rating agencies, debenture trustees, investment advisers, research analysts.
Depository holds securities; clearing corporation guarantees the trade (novation — it becomes counterparty to both sides); the exchange only matches orders.

Why isn't every primary issue a public issue? Because a company may want speed, lower cost, targeted investors or confidentiality — private placement and rights routes exist precisely to avoid the disclosure burden and timeline of a public offer.

  • Book building: price band with a floor and cap; retail may bid at cut-off price; anchor investors are allotted before the issue opens and are locked in.
  • ASBA is mandatory — funds stay blocked in the applicant's own bank account until allotment. UPI is the retail mandate mechanism.

  • Retail individual investor = application value up to ₹2 lakh.
A bonus issue does not change the shareholder's wealth or the company's net worth — only the number of shares and the price per share. Same for a stock split. A rights issue does bring in fresh money.

Secondary market and impact costcovered before

Chapter 6.2

  • Rolling settlement — India settles on a T+1 basis; the ex-date now effectively falls on the record date itself under T+1.
  • Circuit filters / price bands curb volatility at the scrip level; market-wide index circuit breakers halt trading at 10%, 15% and 20%.
  • Impact cost — the cost of executing a trade relative to the ideal mid-price. It is a measure of liquidity, and it is the criterion used for index inclusion. Lower impact cost = more liquid.
  • Indices in India are free-float market capitalisation weighted — only shares available for public trading are counted.
Impact cost is not brokerage, STT or any statutory charge. It is a liquidity measure derived from the order book depth.

Technical analysis — only what gets asked

Chapter 8.7

  • Dow theory: the market discounts everything; three trends (primary, secondary, minor); trends must be confirmed by volume and by both averages.
  • Support = price floor where buying emerges; resistance = ceiling where selling emerges. Once broken, roles reverse.
  • Moving averages: a short MA crossing above a long MA is bullish (golden cross); below is bearish (death cross). MAs are lagging indicators.
  • RSI: above 70 overbought, below 30 oversold. MACD: momentum via the difference of two EMAs. Bollinger bands: volatility envelope around an MA.
  • Patterns: head and shoulders (reversal), double top/bottom (reversal), triangles and flags (continuation).
Technical analysis studies price and volume. Fundamental analysis studies the business. If an option mentions earnings, management or balance sheets in a technical question, it is wrong.

  • Price and yield move in opposite directions — always.
  • YTM assumes the bond is held to maturity and every coupon is reinvested at the YTM. Both assumptions rarely hold — that is YTM's core weakness.
  • Yield curve shapes: normal (upward), inverted (recession signal), flat, humped. Theories: pure expectations, liquidity preference (longer term demands a premium), market segmentation / preferred habitat.
Current yield ignores capital gain or loss at maturity, so for a discount bond current yield understates the true return and for a premium bond it overstates it. Ranking for a discount bond: coupon rate < current yield < YTM.

Module 4 Investment Through Managed Portfolio 23 marks · Ch 11–13 · pure number recall

Mutual fund fundamentals

Chapter 11.1–11.3

NAV = (Total assets − Total liabilities) ÷ Number of units outstanding
  • Three-tier structure: Sponsor → Trustees (hold assets in trust for unitholders) → AMC (manages). Constituted as a trust under the Indian Trusts Act; SEBI-regulated.
  • Open-ended — continuous buy/sell at NAV, variable corpus. Close-ended — fixed corpus, listed on exchange, may trade at a discount to NAV. Interval — open only in specified transaction periods. ETF — traded on exchange like a share, needs a demat account, price tracks NAV via arbitrage.
  • Direct plan — no distributor commission, lower TER, higher NAV. Regular plan — includes distribution cost.
  • Documents: SID (scheme details), SAI (statutory information), KIM (abridged, accompanies the form). Riskometer has six levels from Low to Very High. Debt schemes also carry a Potential Risk Class matrix.
  • Performance is benchmarked against Total Return Index (TRI), not price index.
A close-ended fund's market price can differ from its NAV — that is normal. An open-ended fund always transacts at NAV (adjusted for load), never at a market price.
"Dividend" was renamed IDCW — Income Distribution cum Capital Withdrawal. It is paid out of the scheme's own NAV, so the NAV falls by exactly that amount. It is not extra return.

Scheme categories, costs and transactions

Chapter 11.5–11.10

  • SEBI categorisation: Equity (11 categories) · Debt (16) · Hybrid (6) · Solution-oriented (2 — retirement, children's, with a 5-year or goal lock-in) · Other (2 — index/ETF, FoF). An AMC may run only one scheme per category, with limited exceptions.
  • TER covers management fee, RTA, trustee, audit, marketing — it is charged daily and reflected in NAV. It is slab-based on AUM, and lower for index funds/ETFs and for direct plans.
  • Exit load is separate from TER and goes back into the scheme. Stamp duty 0.005% on every purchase / switch-in.
  • Cut-off timing: 3:00 pm for most schemes; 1:30 pm for liquid and overnight purchases. NAV applicability depends on realisation of funds, whatever the amount.
  • Systematic transactions: SIP (invest periodically, rupee-cost averaging) · STP (move between schemes) · SWP (withdraw periodically) · Triggers (act on a preset event).
  • Segregated portfolio / side-pocketing — ring-fences a defaulted debt holding so fresh investors are not hit and existing holders keep the recovery claim.
SIP is a mode of investing, not a product, and it does not guarantee profit — it averages cost, which helps in falling and volatile markets and mildly hurts in a straight-up market.
SIP puts money in. SWP takes money out. STP moves money across. In, out, across.

  • Correlation ρ = +1 → no diversification benefit; portfolio risk is just the weighted average. ρ = −1 → risk can be driven to zero at the right weights. Anything below +1 gives some benefit.
  • Efficient frontier — the set of portfolios giving the highest return for each level of risk. Nothing exists above it; anything below it is inefficient.
  • Optimum portfolio — the point where the investor's highest attainable indifference curve is tangent to the efficient frontier. It is investor-specific, not universal.
  • Estimation risk — MPT inputs (expected returns, variances, correlations) are estimates. Optimisers are extremely sensitive to them and tend to maximise the estimation errors, concentrating into whatever asset had the most overstated return. Handled with constraints, resampling or Black–Litterman.
Diversification eliminates unsystematic (company-specific) risk only. Systematic / market risk cannot be diversified away — which is exactly why CAPM prices beta and not total risk.

The Investment Policy Statement (IPS)

Chapter 15.3–15.6 · the governing document of the whole portfolio

What it is: a written agreement between adviser and client that sets out how the portfolio will be run — the objectives, the constraints, the permitted asset mix, the benchmark, and the responsibilities of each side. It is drawn up before any money is invested, and it is the reference point every later decision is measured against.

Structure — what an IPS must contain:

SectionContents
1. Scope and purposeClient identity, assets covered, the adviser's role, governing regulations, fee basis
2. ObjectivesReturn requirement and risk tolerance — the only two objectives
3. ConstraintsLiquidity · Time horizon · Tax · Legal & regulatory · Unique circumstances
4. Asset allocation policyStrategic weights plus a permitted range around each — the band is what makes tactical shifts legitimate
5. Permitted and prohibited investmentsEligible instruments, exposure caps by sector/entity/asset class, anything the client bars
6. BenchmarkChosen in advance, appropriate to the mandate, investable and measurable
7. Rebalancing policyMethod (calendar or threshold), trigger levels, who authorises it
8. Monitoring and reviewReporting format and frequency; review at least annually or on a material life change
9. Duties and responsibilitiesWhat the adviser owes; what the client must disclose and keep updated

Why it is important:

  • It is the anchor in a falling market. Decisions made calmly in advance protect the client from decisions made in panic. When the client wants to sell everything, the IPS is what you both agreed to in daylight.
  • It converts vague wishes into testable targets. "I want good returns" cannot be managed; "8% real return over 15 years, maximum 12% drawdown tolerance" can.
  • It defines accountability. Performance is judged against the stated benchmark and mandate, not against whatever index happened to do best.
  • It is the compliance record. Under the SEBI IA Regulations, the client agreement, risk profile and suitability assessment must be documented, communicated, reviewed at least annually and retained for 5 years. The IPS is where that evidence lives.
  • It gives continuity. If the adviser, the relationship manager or the firm changes, the portfolio's logic survives in writing.
  • It is the reference in a dispute. A grievance is settled by what was agreed, not by what either party now remembers.
Risk tolerance has two halves. Ability to take risk is financial — income stability, net worth, time horizon, dependants. Willingness is psychological. When the two conflict, the IPS must be built on the lower of the two, and the adviser's job is to educate rather than override. A young client with huge capacity but a terror of volatility gets the conservative allocation.
Return objective must be stated after inflation, tax and fees, and it must be consistent with the risk tolerance. An IPS demanding a 15% return with a 2% risk tolerance is not a plan — it is a contradiction the adviser has to resolve at the outset.
Two objectives, five constraints. If a question asks you to identify an objective and the option says liquidity, tax or time horizon — that is a constraint, not an objective.

Unique needs and preferences — ESG and idiosyncratic constraints

Chapter 15.7 · the fifth constraint

"Unique circumstances" is the catch-all constraint: any client-specific restriction that is neither legal nor tax-driven, but which the portfolio must respect anyway. These are recorded in the IPS and designed around — not argued away.

Sustainable / ESG investing — the most common expression of a values preference. Five approaches, in rising order of involvement:

ApproachMethod
Negative / exclusionary screeningRule out sectors outright — tobacco, alcohol, gambling, arms, fossil fuels
Positive / best-in-classKeep every sector but hold only the highest ESG scorers within each
ESG integrationFold ESG factors into ordinary financial analysis as risk factors, with no exclusions
ThematicTarget a theme — clean energy, water, financial inclusion
Impact investingSeek a measurable social or environmental outcome alongside the financial return
  • Backed in India by SEBI's BRSR (Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report) disclosures for the top listed companies, and a dedicated ESG sub-category within thematic equity mutual funds.
  • Active ownership / stewardship — voting and engagement — is the sixth lever, used by institutions rather than retail clients.
Any screen narrows the investable universe. That raises tracking error against a broad benchmark and can concentrate sector exposure. The adviser must disclose this cost and pick a comparable ESG benchmark, not judge the portfolio against the broad index it was never allowed to hold.

Idiosyncratic constraints you should be able to name:

A unique preference is a constraint the adviser accepts and documents, not a mistake to be corrected. The fiduciary duty is to explain the return or risk cost of the preference and then build the best portfolio that honours it — overriding a client's stated values because you think the maths is better is a violation of the mandate.

Our running example (Raj's fund): return 18%, benchmark 12%, risk-free 6%, market SD 15%, beta 1.3.

MetricValueReading

  • Sharpe uses σ (total risk) → right when the portfolio is the investor's entire wealth, or when it is not well diversified.
  • Treynor uses β (systematic risk) → right when the portfolio is one component of a larger diversified holding.
  • Sortino only penalises returns below the target — fairer than Sharpe, which punishes upside volatility too.
  • Tracking error = standard deviation of (portfolio return − benchmark return). Index funds want it near zero; active funds need it to earn their alpha.
  • VaR — maximum expected loss over a period at a given confidence level. It says nothing about the size of losses beyond that threshold.
Sharpe and Treynor rank portfolios differently when a portfolio is poorly diversified — Treynor will flatter it because it ignores the diversifiable risk the investor is actually carrying.

Benchmarking and performance attributioncovered before

A valid benchmark is specified in advance, unambiguous, measurable, investable, appropriate to the manager's style, and reflective of current investment opinion.

Brinson attribution breaks excess return into three parts:

EffectAnswers
AllocationDid the manager overweight the sectors that did well? (top-down decision)
SelectionWithin each sector, did the manager pick the better stocks? (bottom-up decision)
InteractionThe combined effect of overweighting a sector and picking well inside it
Allocation = which bucket. Selection = what's in the bucket. Interaction = doing both right at once. The three must sum to the total excess return over the benchmark.
Peer group comparison must be like-for-like — a mid-cap fund compared against a large-cap peer set produces a meaningless ranking. Survivorship bias also inflates peer averages, because closed and merged poor performers drop out of the data.

Segregation — Regulations 22 and 22A (this is where mock tests catch people):

  • An RIA earns only fees from the client. No commissions, no trail, no product-linked consideration — from anyone, ever.
  • An individual adviser cannot be an adviser and a distributor at the same time. Non-individuals must segregate the two at the client level, through separate divisions and separate personnel.
  • Segregation applies at the level of the family of the client, not just the individual — you cannot advise the husband and distribute to the wife.
  • An IA may recommend specific schemes and securities — that is the job. What it cannot do is get involved in execution for consideration.
  • Reg 22A permits an execution facility only under strict simultaneous conditions: direct schemes only, no consideration of any kind, optional for the client, and no bundling with the fee.
"The adviser made no personal gain" is never a defence — not under PFUTP and not under the IA Regulations. Absence of profit does not cure a violation.
Clients must never hand investment money to the adviser. An IA has no custody infrastructure; funds go directly from the client to the AMC or broker. Only the advisory fee is payable to the IA.
Risk profiling and suitability (Reg 16 & 17) are compulsory, documented, communicated to the client, and reviewed at least annually. Advice given without a completed risk profile is a violation regardless of how good the advice was.

Ethics and fiduciary dutycovered before

Chapter 19

  • Fiduciary duty — the adviser must act in the client's best interest, ahead of their own. This is a higher bar than "suitability" and far higher than "disclosure".
  • Conflicts of interest must be avoided where possible, and fully disclosed where unavoidable. Disclosure alone does not sanitise a conflict that could have been avoided.
  • Ethical dilemma — a situation where two defensible principles pull in opposite directions, so any choice sacrifices something. It is not simply "right vs wrong".
  • SEBI's investor guidance — deal only with registered advisers, insist on a written agreement, never hand over investment funds, never rely on assured-return promises, and check credentials on the SEBI website.

Ethical right vs ethical restraint — this pair is asked directly, so keep the two definitions clean:

Where restraint actually operates — the legal/ethical grid. Restraint lives entirely in the shaded box:

EthicalUnethical
LegalThe intended zone — do it← ethical restraint belongs here. Permitted, but you decline
IllegalRare and still not available to youNever — this is a violation

What restraint looks like in advisory practice:

  • You are entitled to charge up to the 2.5% AUA cap. You restrain to a fee that is proportionate to the work actually done.
  • You are entitled to recommend a product that clears the suitability test. You restrain when a cheaper, simpler product serves the client equally well.
  • You are entitled to churn a portfolio within the mandate. You restrain because activity that does not serve the client is not advice.
  • You are entitled to use a client's good outcome in your marketing. You restrain because a selected result misleads by omission.
  • You are entitled to trade personally in a security. You restrain ahead of recommending it to clients, to avoid front-running the advice.
  • You are entitled to stay silent about a shortcoming the client has not asked about. You restrain from silence, because a fiduciary discloses unprompted.

Why it matters: regulation can only describe the minimum. Advisory work is full of situations no rule anticipated, and the client cannot verify the adviser's choices — the information gap is the whole reason the relationship needs trust. Restraint is what fills the space the rulebook leaves empty, and it is the practical content of the fiduciary standard.

Legality is the floor, not the target. Fiduciary duty tells you the client comes first; ethical restraint is the day-to-day mechanism by which you actually put them there.
"It was legal" and "the client agreed" are both weaker than the fiduciary standard. If a compliant action is still against the client's interest, the fiduciary answer is not to do it.
Ranking of duties when an option list forces a choice: client's interest → integrity of the market → employer → self. The self always comes last.

Grievance redress — the escalation chains

Chapter 20 · the sequence is the answer, not the forum

The universal first step: raise it with the entity itself and wait out the stipulated period. Only then does any external forum have jurisdiction.

CAPITAL MARKETS — SCORES 2.0, live since 1 April 2024 Market participant / intermediary / listed company ↓ entity must file its Action Taken Report within 21 calendar days SCORES (SEBI Complaint Redress System) ↓ dissatisfied — request within 15 days First level review by the Designated Body (e.g. the exchange, AIBI) ↓ still dissatisfied — request within 15 days Second level review by SEBI ↓ or opt out at any point into ODR portal (SMART ODR — conciliation, then arbitration) SAT sits outside this chain — it hears appeals against SEBI orders, within 45 days.
BANKING — one scheme since 12 November 2021 The bank / NBFC / payment system participant ↓ no reply, or unsatisfactory reply, within 30 days RBI Integrated Ombudsman (RB-IOS 2021) file within 1 year of the reply, or 1 year + 30 days if no reply ↓ appeal against the Ombudsman's award Appellate Authority (Deputy Governor, RBI) RB-IOS repealed and merged the three earlier schemes: Banking Ombudsman Scheme 2006 Ombudsman Scheme for NBFCs 2018 Ombudsman Scheme for Digital Transactions 2019 There is now one ombudsman — no split by type of complaint.
INSURANCE The insurer's Grievance Redressal Officer ↓ no response in 15 days, or response unsatisfactory Bima Bharosa (IRDAI's portal — formerly called IGMS) ↓ not attended within 15 days, or still unsatisfactory Insurance Ombudsman (Insurance Ombudsman Rules 2017) file within 1 year · award limit ₹30 lakh
PENSION — route depends on who runs the scheme Pension scheme run by a mutual fund → SEBI, via SCORES Pension scheme run by an insurer → IRDAI NPS (PFRDA Redressal of Subscriber Grievance Regulations 2015): Level 1 CRA / nodal office, through CGMS ↓ unresolved after 30 days Level 2 NPS Trust ↓ unresolved after 21 days, or unsatisfactory Level 3 Ombudsman appointed by PFRDA

Consumer disputes — Consumer Protection Act 2019: District Commission up to ₹50 lakh · State Commission ₹50 lakh to ₹2 crore · National Commission above ₹2 crore.

NPS order — get this right way round. It is CRA → NPS Trust → Ombudsman. The Ombudsman is the final level, not the middle one. Reversing these two is the single most common error on this topic.
Banking is now one ombudsman, not several. The old split — Banking Ombudsman for bank services and credit cards, a separate scheme for ATM and digital transactions, another for NBFCs — was repealed in November 2021. If an option offers that split as the current position, it is describing a scheme that no longer exists.
Escalation is sequential and time-gated. Approach the entity first, wait the stipulated period — 21 days for capital markets, 30 days for banking and NPS level 1, 15 days for insurance — and only then escalate. A complaint filed straight at the ombudsman is rejected on that ground alone.
Pension questions are decided by who runs the scheme, not by the word "pension". MF-managed pension scheme → SEBI/SCORES. Insurer-managed → IRDAI. Only actual NPS goes down the CRA → NPS Trust → Ombudsman chain.
SAT hears appeals against orders of SEBI, IRDAI and PFRDA. It is not a first-instance investor grievance forum, and it is not where you take a complaint about your broker.
Every chain has the same shape: entity → sector platform → ombudsman or tribunal. If an option skips the entity, it is wrong. If it skips the waiting period, it is wrong. If it puts the ombudsman anywhere but last, it is wrong.
