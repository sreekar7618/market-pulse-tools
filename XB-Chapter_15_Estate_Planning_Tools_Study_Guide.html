15.1

The Big Picture

Every estate-planning tool answers one question: does it work while I'm alive, or only once I'm gone? That single split organises the whole chapter — get comfortable with it first, and everything else slots in underneath.

Fig 15.1 — Classification of estate planning tools (Sec. 15.1)
💡
Why the split matters A Will only ever speaks after death — that's the whole point of it. A Trust, Gift, or POA works immediately. That timing difference is why the exam loves to test "which tool for which situation" caselets.
15.2

Wills

The workhorse of estate planning. A Will only activates after death, stays fully private and changeable until then, and follows a precise statutory playbook — the Indian Succession Act, 1925 ("ISA").

Definition & roles 7 types Who can make one Registration Revocation Codicils Succession certificate Executor
1

What exactly is a Will?

Sec. 2(h) & Sec. 62, ISA 1925

A Will is the testator's legal declaration of intent about their property, meant to take effect only after death. Three roles to keep straight:

  • Testator — the person making the Will
  • Legatee — the person named to receive a portion of the estate
  • Executor — the person named to administer the estate

Until death, the Will is a confidential, fully revocable document — the testator can tear it up and start over any number of times, with zero legal friction.

✓
Why bother writing one No confusion among heirs · lets the testator address special situations (minor children, a widowed daughter, special needs) that generic inheritance law ignores · can disinherit a fraudulent/disobedient family member · tax advantages · and appoints a trusted executor instead of leaving it to a court-appointed administrator.
2

Seven types of Will

Sec. 15.2.2

The exam likes to describe a scenario and ask "what kind of Will is this?" — know these by their trigger condition, not just their name.

01

Conditional / Contingent

Takes effect only if a stated condition occurs. If the condition fails or was invalid to begin with, the Will itself is not enforceable.

02

Joint & Mutual

Two+ people write one document, but law treats it as two separate Wills, each probated individually. "Mutual" adds reciprocal benefit — each is the other's legatee.

03

Duplicate

A signed, attested copy held by the executor/trustee. If the testator destroys their copy, both copies are treated as revoked.

04

Holograph

Entirely handwritten by the testator. Considered strong evidence of genuineness precisely because it's fully in their own hand.

05

Concurrent

Two Wills for property in two different countries, treated as independent — unless the two estates are linked, in which case both go to probate together.

06

Sham

Executed with every formality, but with no real testamentary intent — done for some other ("collateral") purpose. Courts treat this as a nullity.

07

Privileged / Unprivileged

A soldier in actual warfare, an airman, or a mariner at sea can pronounce a Will orally before 2 witnesses — that's privileged. Every other Will is unprivileged.

3

Legal requirements & testamentary capacity

Sec. 59, ISA 1925

Four essentials for a valid Will: testamentary capacity & sound mind, knowledge of contents, freedom from undue influence/fraud/coercion, and a voluntary act.

Who's eligible to write one:

  • A person of sound mind — as long as they're not a minor
  • A married woman — for any property she could alienate on her own during her lifetime
  • A deaf, dumb, or blind person — if they understand what they're doing
  • Someone with ordinary insanity — during a lucid interval when they're of sound mind

Testamentary capacity specifically means the testator can comprehend: the extent of their property, their relationship to the people who might reasonably expect a bequest, and the effect of what they're putting in the Will.

4

Registration of Wills

Sec. 17 & Sec. 18, Registration Act 1908

Registration is optional, not compulsory — an unregistered Will is fully genuine. But registering has real upside: it can't be tampered with, destroyed, or stolen; certified copies are tightly controlled (only to the testator/agent while alive, and only against a death certificate afterward); and it sits in safe custody at the Registrar's office.

The Registrar can visit the testator instead of requiring an office visit for: bodily infirmity that makes travel risky, a person in judicial/civil custody, and a Muslim pardanashin woman. No stamp duty applies since a Will is registered on plain paper.

⚠
Exam trap Registration is compulsory in exactly one situation: a bequest to a charitable or religious institution by a non-Hindu. That Will must be deposited with the Registrar within 6 months of execution, and the testator must survive 12 months from execution — otherwise the bequest fails. Miss either condition and the charitable gift is void, even though the rest of the Will stands.
5

Modifying & revoking a Will

Sec. 57, Sec. 62, Sec. 70, ISA 1925

A Will is always revocable until death — no deed can make it irrevocable — but the testator must be of sound mind at the moment of revocation. Merely intending to revoke at some future date doesn't count.

Fig 15.2 — Modes of revocation (Sec. 62, ISA 1925)
⚠
Exam trap — marriage & divorce For Hindus, marriage does not revoke a Will or codicil (Sec. 57) — this is the opposite of the English common-law default many candidates expect. Divorce also does not revoke a Will. And simply losing the physical document is not revocation either — only destruction carried out (or approved) by the testator, with intent, counts.
6

Codicils

Sec. 2(b), ISA 1925

A codicil is a small supplementary document that alters or adds to an existing Will without rewriting it. It must be executed and attested exactly like a Will, and it isn't independent — it's legally treated as part of the Will it modifies.

If the changes are substantial, the better route is a fresh Will that expressly revokes the earlier one — a codicil is for minor edits (e.g., swapping out one executor for another), not a rewrite.

7

Succession Certificate

Sec. 370 & Sec. 371, ISA 1925

Issued by a District Court to establish a legal heir's authority to collect debts and securities of someone who died — but only when there's no Will. If a valid Will exists, the property already vests with the executor, so a succession certificate cannot be granted.

It's narrow in scope: provident fund, insurance, bank deposits, shares, and government securities. It does not establish ownership/title — it only authorises the holder to collect and discharge those specific debts.

8

Role of the Executor

Sec. 2(c) & Sec. 222, ISA 1925

The executor is the person the testator names to carry out the Will — collate assets, pay off liabilities, distribute the legacy. Only an executor named in the Will can receive probate (Sec. 222).

The job in sequence: pay funeral/burial costs and obtain the death certificate → locate and value every asset → pay off debts, liabilities, and taxes (lenders get paid before heirs) → distribute what's left as the Will directs. If a trust is created by the Will, the executor also has to work through the trust deed.

  • Almost anyone can be an executor — family, friends, even a corporate body (Sec. 223) or a beneficiary can be named
  • A minor can be named executor, but probate can't be granted until they turn 18 — a legal guardian applies for letters of administration meanwhile
  • A person of unsound mind cannot be appointed
  • No cap on the number of executors a testator can appoint
  • An executor has a right of refusal before starting — but once accepted, the role is legally binding until a court releases them
  • An executor who is also a legatee cannot claim their legacy unless they show clear intent to actually act as executor (Sec. 141)

If no executor is named — or the named executor is incapable, refuses, or dies mid-administration — the court appoints an Administrator instead, via Letters of Administration.

15.3

Probate

Probate is the court's stamp of approval on a Will — proof, under the court's seal, that this really is the deceased's genuine last Will.

1

What probate actually certifies

Sec. 2(f), ISA 1925

Probate is a copy of the Will, certified under the seal of a competent court. It establishes both the legal character of the executor and the validity of every act the executor takes under the Will.

When there's no valid probate route available (no executor named, the named executor is incapable/refuses/died mid-way), the court issues Letters of Administration to the legatee instead — functionally similar, but for situations without a workable executor.

📍
When is probate mandatory? Only when the Will (or codicil) was made in — or the immovable property sits in — the three Presidency towns: Kolkata, and the municipal limits of Chennai and Mumbai. Everywhere else in India, probate is optional, though it's still often demanded by asset holders (banks, registrars) as practical proof.
⚠
Exam trap — probate ≠ succession certificate They look similar but sit on opposite sides of one fact: does a Will exist? Probate certifies a Will (issued when there IS one). A succession certificate is issued by a civil court precisely because there is no Will. Never use the terms interchangeably.
2

The process

The executor (or, if none, any competent person) files an application with the appropriate court, attaching the Will and details of the testator's death, assets, and executor. At least one attesting witness must verify the application. The court then issues notice to all legal heirs and publishes a public notice in a national newspaper, inviting objections — only once those are cleared does the court grant probate. Court fees are typically a fixed amount or a percentage of asset value, and vary by state since Wills are a state subject.

15.4

Gifts, Joint Holding & Nomination

Three everyday tools that move assets to family members quickly and quietly — without any of the ceremony a Will requires.

Gifts Joint Holding Nomination
1

Gifts

Sec. 15.4.1

A gift is a voluntary transfer without consideration — the donor gives, the donee receives. Anyone competent to contract can make one. A gift is generally irrevocable, unless the donee agrees to revoke it.

Tax treatment: gifts are taxable as "income from other sources" — except gifts from defined relatives (spouse, siblings of self/spouse, parents, grandparents, children, grandchildren, etc.), gifts received on marriage, or property inherited under a Will, all of which are exempt. Once transferred though, any income the gift subsequently generates is taxed in the donee's hands.

2

Joint Holding

Sec. 15.4.2

Property held by more than one person, accessible per the chosen mode of operation: jointly (all holders must approve every transaction), either or survivor, or anyone or survivor. The first holder is usually the "registered" holder for information and benefit purposes.

⚠
Exam trap Joint holding makes access simple, not ownership certain — if heirs contest it legally, the joint holder's practical claim can still be overridden by the laws of succession.
3

Nomination

Sec. 15.4.3

Nomination lets an asset holder name who should receive the monies on their death — but a nominee is legally reduced to a trustee of the bequest if a dispute arises; final ownership still follows succession law.

  • Only an individual can nominate. Corporates, partnership firms, trusts, HUF Kartas, and POA holders cannot nominate.
  • A minor can be a nominee (with a named guardian), and multiple nominees are allowed with defined percentage shares.
  • A Will overrides a nomination — but the institution can still validly pay the nominee first; the onus to prove a better claim then falls on whoever contests it.
⚠
The one big exception Under the Insurance Act, 1938 and the EPF Act, "beneficial nominees" — immediate family (spouse, parents, children) — actually are entitled to the money outright; other legal heirs get no claim. This is the opposite of the general rule, and a favourite exam trick. Outside these two Acts, courts have generally upheld legal heirs' rights over the nominee's.

Recent updates worth knowing: SEBI (Jan 2025) raised the maximum number of nominees allowed on securities accounts to 10. Banking law amendments are introducing "one after another" successive nomination — an alternate nominee steps in automatically if the first is unavailable.

15.5

Family Settlement & Business

The biggest section in the chapter — how families move property and businesses between generations, and how they protect what they've built along the way.

Family settlement Forms of transfer Family business Valuation Wealth transfer tools Joint tenancy Asset protection Creditor protection / IBC
1

Family Settlement

An instrument used to resolve disputes or rival property claims within a family — entered voluntarily, in good faith, purely to restore harmony. It's not a formal "transfer" in the tax sense, which gives it three real advantages:

  • No capital gains tax — family arrangements are not treated as a transfer
  • Not treated as a gift
  • Clubbing provisions do not apply

It can be oral or written, and stamped/registered only if the parties choose to.

2

Forms of intra-family property transfer

Property moves within a family for love, care, or planning purposes — each route carries a different tax and process footprint:

RouteWhen title changesTax / cost angle
Joint ownership On death of one owner, their share passes to the survivor No tax event on death; capital gains may apply if transferred during one's lifetime
Gifting Immediate No tax within blood relatives, but stamp duty is mandatory (immovable property gifts must be registered); several states offer concessional rates for spouse/children
Will / Inheritance Only after the testator's death; revocable until then No tax on the beneficiary on receipt; registration not required (only mutation); capital gains apply only if the inheritor later sells — using the previous owner's cost of acquisition
3

Estate planning for family businesses

Studies cited in the chapter note only about 70% of family businesses survive into the next generation — succession is one of the biggest risks a family enterprise faces. Three broad strategies help:

  • Rise to the challenge — transfer ownership early via a limited partnership, ESOP, or non-voting stock to heirs not active in the business, while operators keep management control
  • Partial sale — sell part of the business for liquidity to fund the older generation, while the younger generation grows from the residual business's cash flow
  • Create a trust — generates a fixed income stream, keeps control within the family during the trust term, and can pass the business on after a defined period
4

Five ownership models for a family business

Model 01

Owner-Operator

Simplest: control stays with the founder (or a couple). Works well across generations — if the family can agree on a fair successor.

Model 02

Partnership

Only active leaders own and benefit. Fragile when the next generation isn't equal in number or involvement — a classic exam story: four brothers succeed equally, but the third generation includes a daughter excluded as "not a partner," and the business ends up sold.

Model 03

Distributed

Ownership passes to all descendants, working in the business or not. Most popular model — but friction arises between contributors and non-contributors over pay and distributions.

Model 04

Nested

Family branches jointly own some assets, separately own others — small ownership groups "nested" inside a larger one that funds each branch's own portfolio. Risk: the core business gets underfunded.

Model 05

Public

Shares partly publicly traded, business still privately controlled; professional managers run day-to-day operations. Chosen when outside capital is needed or owners want distance from daily operations.

5

Valuing a family business

Method 1

Capitalizing Earnings

Values the business on cash flow available to owners/investors, factoring in current and future capital needs.

Method 2

Projected Earnings (DCF)

Discounted cash flow — based on anticipated future earnings. A 5-year+ horizon makes accuracy harder to pin down.

Method 3

Market Approach

Benchmarks against what the market pays for similar businesses — a directional range, not a precise figure.

Method 4

Net Asset Value

Sums the real estate, machinery, and other hard assets built up over decades — can exceed the operating value alone.

6

Inter-generational wealth transfer — four tools

  • Succession laws — the default when nothing else is planned: Hindu Succession Act 1956, Mohammedan Personal Law, and Indian Succession Act 1925 (Christians/Jews/Parsis)
  • Will — easy to write, freely revocable, but more open to challenge, which can delay transfer and erode value
  • Gift — immediate, tax-free within blood relatives, low litigation risk — but stamp duty applies and it's irrevocable once executed
  • Trust — the most sustainable structure for long-term wealth transfer across multiple generations
7

Joint Tenancy vs. Tenancy-in-Common

Both describe co-ownership by two or more people, but they behave completely differently at death.

Joint TenantsTenants-in-Common
On deathRight of survivorship — the deceased's interest passes automatically to the surviving joint tenant(s)No survivorship — the deceased's share passes per their Will, or succession law if none
Acquiring interestMust be acquired by all tenants at the same time, from the same transaction, with identical interestInterest can be acquired separately; shares can be unequal (equal, if unstated)
WillOnly the sole surviving joint tenant can bequeath the interest by WillFull freedom to deal with one's own share as they wish
8

Asset Protection

Strategies that shield personal or business assets from creditor claims. The right degree of protection depends on four factors: identity of the debtor (spouse's assets, guarantors), identity of the creditor (a government creditor has more seizing power than a private one), nature of the claim (dischargeable claims need less protection), and nature of the asset (PPF, for instance, is protected from creditor claims by law).

  • Corporations / LLPs — the entity's debt doesn't attach to the individual owner, except in cases of fraudulent transfer
  • Asset Protection Trusts — assets held in trust aren't legally "owned" by the settlor, so creditors can't reach them; trade-off: it's irrevocable, and the settlor gives up control
  • Transferring property rights — gifting legal ownership to a spouse or trusted party; risky if that relationship later sours, and still exposed if the transfer itself was fraudulent
9

Creditor Protection Period

Before 2016, defaulting companies could vanish with limited consequence — creditors had only slow civil courts as recourse. A sequence of laws built up creditor protection over three decades:

Fig 15.3 — Evolution of creditor-protection law (Sec. 15.5.9)

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016 runs on strict timelines: a financial creditor files with the adjudicating authority → default is ascertained within 14 days → if confirmed, admission is communicated within 7 days → corporate insolvency resolution process begins.

15.6

Trusts — Characteristics & Regulations

The most powerful — and most tested — tool in this chapter. A trust separates legal ownership from benefit, which is precisely what makes it so useful for privacy, control, and tax planning.

Anatomy of a trust Essential elements Advantages / disadvantages Classification Parties & hybrid Cancellation & revocation Taxation Perpetuities & pass-through Stamp duty & capital gains Offshore trusts DNI — worked example
1

What a trust actually is

Sec. 3, Indian Trust Act 1882

The Indian Trust Act, 1882 defines a trust as: "an obligation annexed to the ownership of property, arising out of a confidence reposed in and accepted by the owner … for the benefit of another, or of another and the owner."

Fig 15.4 — The three parties to a trust (Sec. 3, ITA 1882)
2

Five essential elements

Sec. 6, ITA 1882

A trust is validly created only when the author indicates, with reasonable certainty: (a) an intention to create a trust, (b) the purpose, (c) the beneficiary, (d) the trust property, and (e) — unless the trust is by Will, or the author is themself trustee — actually transfers the property to the trustee.

Private/family trusts fall under the Indian Trust Act, 1882 itself. Public charitable/religious trusts follow a mix of religious law and state legislation (e.g. the Bombay Public Trust Act, 1950).

3

Advantages vs. disadvantages

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Provides for family members/relatives dependent on the settlorStructurally complex
Reduces the probability of litigationCan be expensive to establish and maintain
Lets the settlor's charitable wishes be administered systematicallyTransferring immovable property in is costly (stamp duty varies by state)

4

Classifying a trust — four independent axes

A single trust gets classified along several axes at once — these aren't mutually exclusive categories. A trust can simultaneously be private, irrevocable, and discretionary, for instance.

Axis: WHO benefits

Public vs. Private

Public — for the general public or a section of it (temples, schools, hospitals); permanent, indefinite character. Private — for specific, ascertained individuals/families.

Axis: CAN it be undone

Revocable vs. Irrevocable

Revocable — settlor keeps control, can modify/end it; taxed in the settlor's hands (treated like an alternative to a Will). Irrevocable — locked in until its purpose completes; Sec. 77/78 ITA requires an express power-of-revocation clause, or it's irrevocable by default.

Axis: TRUSTEE discretion

Discretionary vs. Determinate

Discretionary — trustees decide how much income each beneficiary gets; taxed at Maximum Marginal Rate (30%), with exceptions. Determinate — each beneficiary's share is fixed/ascertainable in the deed; taxed via the trustee as representative assessee, at the beneficiary's own slab rate.

Axis: HOW created

Oral / Written, Implied / Express

Movable property can be settled orally; immovable property needs a written deed. An implied trust arises from circumstances alone; an express trust has a formal deed spelling out terms.

⚠
Exam trap — discretionary trust taxation Discretionary trusts are taxed at the flat Maximum Marginal Rate (30%) under Sec. 164(1) — except when: (a) no beneficiary is also a beneficiary of any other trust or has income otherwise taxed above MMR, or (b) the trust is the sole trust created under a Will for the support and maintenance of dependent relatives. Miss the exception and you'll answer 30% when slab rate actually applies.

A Hybrid Trust blends both: the trustee pays a fixed, determinate amount to certain beneficiaries per the deed, and retains full discretion over how the remaining trust property is distributed.

5

Parties to a trust deed

  • Settlor / Author — sets up the trust; in a living trust, retains the right to amend/revoke it (if more than one settlor, generally all must agree to change it)
  • Trustee — accepts the confidence; holds legal title, and the power to buy, sell, borrow against, or transfer trust assets, subject to the deed
  • Beneficiary — entitled to the benefit; can be a primary beneficiary (first in line) or a residual/secondary beneficiary (entitled only after the primary beneficiaries). In a private trust, settlor and beneficiary can be the same person.
6

Cancellation & revocation of a private trust

The Indian Trust Act has no formal dissolution mechanism — a trust simply becomes extinct when its purpose is fulfilled, becomes unlawful, or becomes impossible (destruction of trust property), or when it is revoked. Revocation can happen when:

  • Beneficiaries, competent to contract, consent that the current structure no longer serves them
  • The trust was created specifically to pay off the settlor's debts to creditors
  • A trust created by a non-testamentary document (or verbally) expressly reserved a power of revocation to the settlor
7

How a trust actually gets taxed

Sec. 60–64, Sec. 160–164, Income Tax Act

Public trusts get exemptions under Chapter III (Sec. 11, 12, 12A, 12AA, 12AB, 13) for services rendered to the nation. Private trusts are taxed at slab rate or Maximum Marginal Rate depending on their structure (Sec. 160–164).

Sec. 60

Income transferred, asset not

If income is transferred (revocable or not) without transferring the underlying asset, that income is taxed in the transferor's hands only.

Sec. 61

Revocable transfer of asset

Income from a revocable transfer of the asset itself is taxed in the transferor's hands.

Sec. 62

The carve-out

Sec. 61 does not apply if the trust isn't revocable during the beneficiary's lifetime, and the transferor gets no direct/indirect benefit from the income.

Sec. 161(1A)

Business income

If any part of trust income is business profit, the entire trust income is taxed at MMR — unless it's the sole trust under a Will for a dependent relative's support & maintenance, in which case slab rate applies.

Sec. 64

Minor's income clubbing

A minor's income (including from a private trust for the minor) is clubbed with the parent/guardian's income — except income of a minor with a disability specified under Sec. 80U.

Sec. 164(1)

Discretionary trust default

Where beneficiaries' shares are indeterminate/unknown, income is taxed at MMR (30%), subject to the two exceptions noted above.

A trust can even be created for a deity — in CIT v. Sri Jagannath Jew [1977] 107 ITR 9 (SC), the Supreme Court held the deity was the legal owner of the estate and assessable as such, even though the managers also handled secular duties like family members' residence and transport.

8

Perpetuities & the pass-through concept

No trust can run forever. Property can't be tied up beyond the lifetime of a living person plus the minority (until 18) of someone born by the end of that period. Income accumulation is similarly capped — generally the transferor's lifetime, or 18 years from the transfer, whichever is later. Exception: transfers for public benefit (religion, knowledge, commerce, health, safety) escape the rule.

A private trust has no separate legal identity — it's a pass-through (flow-through) entity. Income "flows through" to beneficiaries as if it were their own income; the trustee is only liable to tax in a representative capacity, not as the actual owner.

9

Stamp duty & capital gains on transfer into a trust

Art. 58, Indian Stamp Act 1899 · Sec. 47(iii), Income Tax Act
  • Stamp duty is levied on the trust deed itself (Article 58) — not on later distributions to beneficiaries
  • Settling immovable property via a registered document during the settlor's lifetime attracts stamp duty; settling property via a Will does not
  • Sec. 47(iii) exempts the transferor from capital gains tax when transferring capital assets into an irrevocable trust — but transfers into a revocable trust remain fully subject to capital gains
⚠
Two traps stacked together (1) Stamp duty applies to a lifetime transfer into a trust, but NOT to a testamentary (Will-based) settlement. (2) Capital gains exemption under Sec. 47(iii) applies only to irrevocable trusts — revocable trust transfers are fully taxable. Read the question carefully for both "revocable/irrevocable" AND "lifetime/testamentary."
10

Offshore trusts

Trusts formed outside India, under a foreign trust law — used for cross-border families, offshore business, or NRI succession planning. India places no restriction on non-resident trustees or beneficiaries.

  • Revocable offshore trust — income is treated as the settlor's own, taxed directly in their hands
  • Irrevocable, all-Indian-resident beneficiaries — trustee taxed in a representative capacity
  • Irrevocable, mixed resident/non-resident beneficiaries — only the Indian beneficiary is taxed on distributions received; trustee has no representative role in India
  • Partial Indian control — can be taxed as an Association of Persons

Indian resident beneficiaries must report their interest in an offshore trust in the information schedule of their income tax return.

11

Distributable Net Income (DNI)

DNI is the slice of trust income that's actually allotted to beneficiaries — it becomes their income, once distributed, and exists mainly to prevent double taxation.

DNI = Taxable Income − Capital Gains + Tax Exemptions
(swap in Capital Loss as an addition, if that's what occurred)

15.7

Guardianship

Who legally steps in when someone — a minor, an elderly parent, a person with special needs — cannot manage their own affairs.

Two kinds of responsibility

A guardian's duties split into two distinct roles, which can sit with the same person or be split between two people:

  • Guardian of the Person — responsible for custody and day-to-day care
  • Guardian of the Estate — responsible for managing financial affairs
Type 01

Natural Guardian

No legal process needed — it falls by birth. Parents are natural guardians until the child turns 18.

Type 02

Court-Appointed Guardian

For individuals with no living parents, incapacitated by birth, accident, or other cause — the court appoints someone to decide on their behalf.

Type 03

Testamentary Guardian

Appointed via a Will — most common for minor children, managing the child's affairs within the limits the Will sets.

A legal guardian's financial authority typically covers: managing bank accounts, making and managing investments (mutual funds, shares, bonds), and availing loans or government concessions on behalf of the person under guardianship.

15.8

Power of Attorney

A POA lets someone act on your behalf right now — unlike a Will, which only ever speaks after death.

Definition & parties

Sec. 1A, POA Act 1882 · Sec. 2(21), Indian Stamp Act

A POA is a formal instrument by which the donor (principal) empowers the donee (agent/attorney) to act on their behalf, for a specific act or generally. Both parties must ordinarily be 18+ and of sound mind — with two notable exceptions: a married woman can execute a POA even as a minor, and under Sec. 184 of the Contract Act, even a minor can be a donee. A person of unsound mind cannot be appointed attorney under any circumstance.

Type 01

General POA

Broad authorization — the agent can act generally, across more than one transaction.

Type 02

Special POA

Narrow — restricted to one specific matter/transaction. Authority expires once that transaction completes.

Type 03

Special POA for Registration

Authorizes the attorney specifically to present an already-executed but unregistered document for registration.

Revocation

Sec. 201, Indian Contract Act 1872

A POA ends when: the principal revokes it · either party is declared insolvent · the donee renounces the power · the underlying business is completed · or either party dies. If the agent has their own interest in the agency (Sec. 202), the POA cannot be revoked without the agent's consent — and Sec. 206 requires sufficient notice of revocation to avoid liability for resulting damages.

⚠
Exam trap — registration A POA generally does NOT require compulsory registration under Sec. 17(1)(b) of the Registration Act — because it only authorises the donee to act; it doesn't itself create, assign, or extinguish any title in immovable property. It becomes compulsorily registrable only if it creates an actual interest (a charge) in immovable property in favour of the donee.

Authentication (Sec. 85, Indian Evidence Act) can be done by a Notary Public, any court/judge/magistrate, an Indian Consul/Vice-Consul, or a representative of the Central Government. Stamp duty is a state matter (Article 48, Indian Stamp Act) — but stamping alone doesn't define the POA's legal nature; its content does. An agent's control ends automatically on the principal's death, and NRIs can execute a POA from abroad, provided it's authenticated by the Indian Embassy/Consulate where it was executed.

Case 1 — Probate objections

Concept check

Mr. PQR, a Hindu, died leaving a Will. Unmarried, no immediate family. He has 2 living siblings, and 2 deceased siblings who left behind spouses and children. His Will gives 1/5 of the estate to a living brother, and 1/5 each to a nephew and niece of a surviving sister.

Q: Who can contest the Will during probate?

Living brother
Living sister
Spouse/children of deceased siblings
✓ All of the above

When the Will is filed for probate, the court issues notice to all legal heirs — including spouses and children of deceased siblings — inviting objections. Anyone who doesn't respond is presumed to have no objection. A Will can only be challenged on specific legal grounds, which the court then evaluates.
