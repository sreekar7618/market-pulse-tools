Risk, and the four things you can do about it

Chapter 1.1–1.2

Pure risk (loss or no loss — fire, death, illness) is insurable. Speculative risk (loss or gain — equity, business) is not.

TechniqueUse whenExample
AvoidHigh frequency, high severityDon't take up the dangerous hobby
Reduce / controlHigh frequency, low severitySmoke alarms, health check-ups, helmets
RetainLow frequency, low severityDeductible, contingency fund, self-insure the phone
TransferLow frequency, high severityInsurance — the only quadrant insurance is efficient for
Insurance sits in the low-frequency / high-severity corner. Anything that happens often is cheaper to budget for than to insure — that is why extended warranties and phone insurance are bad economics, and term cover is superb economics.
Insurance transfers risk; it does not reduce it. Fitting a sprinkler reduces risk. Buying a fire policy transfers the financial consequence. Questions phrase this as "which technique did X use".

Life insurance needs analysis — HLV vs Needs approach

Chapter 2.2 · caselet arithmetic

Human Life Value (income replacement) HLV = PV of (future annual income − self-maintenance − taxes − premiums), discounted at a real rate over the remaining working years Needs approach (expense method) Cover = Final expenses + Outstanding liabilities + PV of family's living expenses + Goal corpus (education, marriage) − Existing assets − Existing cover
HLV answers "what is this earner worth?" The Needs approach answers "what does this family need?" They give different numbers and the exam expects you to know which was used. HLV ignores liabilities and existing assets; Needs approach nets them off. When a question gives you loans and existing corpus, it wants the Needs approach.
Rule-of-thumb cross-check the exam also uses: 10–15× annual income. If your worked answer lands wildly outside that band, re-read the question.

In a money-back policy the death benefit is the full sum assured, regardless of survival benefits already paid out. Candidates lose this mark by netting them off.

MWP Act, 1874 — the estate-planning tool hiding in Module 7

Chapter 2.5 · overlaps Module 10

  • Section 6 of the Married Women's Property Act lets a married man (or a married woman, in practice) buy a life policy expressed to be for the benefit of wife and/or children.
  • The policy proceeds form a separate trust. They are beyond the reach of creditors, and outside the estate for succession purposes.
  • Must be elected at the time of proposal — cannot be added later. Cannot be revoked, assigned, or used as loan collateral.
  • Beneficiaries must be named. A trustee may be appointed; otherwise the beneficiaries hold jointly.
MWP is the answer to any caselet where a businessman with personal guarantees wants his family protected from a business failure. It is the single cleanest creditor-protection device an Indian resident can buy off the shelf.
MWP protection is not retrospective and cannot be bolted onto an existing policy. If the caselet says the client "already holds" the policy, MWP is not available for it — a fresh policy is required.

Multiple policies — contribution, and where it doesn't apply

Chapter 3.2 & 2.6

  • Life insurance: no contribution, no indemnity. Hold ten policies, every one pays in full. The only constraint is underwriting — total cover must be justified by HLV and income, and all existing cover must be disclosed.
  • Indemnity health/general: since 2013 IRDAI rules, the policyholder chooses which insurer to claim from (settlement up to SA of that policy), and may then claim the balance from the other. The insurers settle contribution among themselves.
  • Benefit policies: claim from all, in full.
Non-disclosure of existing life cover at proposal stage is a breach of utmost good faith, and gives the insurer grounds to repudiate — but only within the Section 45 window.
Section 45, Insurance Act: no life policy can be called in question on any ground after 3 years from issue/revival/rider date. Within 3 years, repudiation is allowed only for fraud or misstatement, and only with written reasons. Three years is absolute — even proven fraud cannot reopen the policy after it.

Regulation, grievance and global coverage

Chapter 1.7–1.8, 2.8, 3.4

  • Regulator: IRDAI, under the IRDAI Act 1999 and Insurance Act 1938.
  • Distributors: agent represents the insurer (one life + one general + one health); broker represents the client and can place with any insurer; corporate agent may tie up with up to 3 life, 3 general, 3 health insurers; IMF (Insurance Marketing Firm) area-restricted.
  • Grievance ladder: Insurer's Grievance Redressal Officer → Bima Bharosa (IRDAI's integrated portal) → Insurance Ombudsman → Consumer Commission / Civil Court.
  • Ombudsman: complaint within 1 year of insurer's rejection; award limit ₹50 lakh; award binding on the insurer, not on the complainant.
  • Claim timelines (2024 general insurance circular): surveyor report in 15 days; claim settled within 7 days of report. Life death claims: 15 days if no investigation; 45 days if investigated.
  • Global coverage: term/whole life generally pay worldwide (subject to travel/residence disclosure and sanctioned-country exclusions). Health insurance is territorial — an Indian mediclaim does not pay for treatment abroad unless a global cover rider is bought. Travel insurance is the product for overseas medical events.
GST on insurance is now zero on all individual life and health insurance policies (including family floaters, term, endowment and ULIPs) with effect from 22 September 2025, down from 18%. Group policies — employer-sponsored group health or group term life — remain at 18%. The date of premium payment decides the rate. Surrender/claim payouts also attract no GST from that date.
If the workbook or an MCQ still shows 18% GST on a term plan premium, that is the pre-September-2025 position. Read the question: if it names a group policy, 18% is still correct.

Module 8 Retirement Planning 30 marks · Ch 4–6 · second-largest block

Why retirement planning is different from every other goal

Chapter 4.1–4.3

  • No loan exists for it. You can borrow for a house, a car, education. Nobody lends for retirement — so it cannot be deferred behind "more urgent" goals.
  • Longevity risk. The risk of outliving the corpus. It is the one financial risk that gets worse the better things go.
  • Sequence-of-returns risk. Poor returns in the first few years of withdrawal permanently damage the corpus, even if the average return over the full period is fine. Accumulation is immune to sequence; distribution is not.
  • Inflation over a long horizon. 6% inflation over 25 years multiplies expenses by 4.3×. Medical inflation runs higher still.
  • Shrinking joint family and rising life expectancy — the two structural drivers the workbook lists for India specifically.

Replacement ratio — the share of pre-retirement income needed post-retirement, typically 70–80%. It is below 100% because commuting costs, EMIs, children's expenses and retirement contributions all stop. It creeps back up because healthcare replaces them.
Do not plan retirement expenses as a percentage of current income. Plan them from current expenses, adjusted for what stops and what starts. A high saver's replacement ratio on income can be 40%; a paycheck-to-paycheck client's can be 90%.

Estimating the retirement corpus — the full arithmeticcaselet certainty

Chapter 4.4 & 6.2

Step 1 Expenses at retirement =FV(inflation, years to retire, 0, −current expenses) Step 2 Real rate for the withdrawal phase r* = [(1 + post-retirement return) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1 Step 3 Corpus needed at retirement =PV(r*, retirement years, −Step 1, 0, 1) the final 1 is the type argument = annuity due Step 4 FV of the existing corpus =FV(pre-retirement return, n, 0, −existing corpus) Step 5 Gap = Step 3 − Step 4 Step 6 Monthly SIP =PMT(rate/12, months, 0, −Gap)
Three places this goes wrong, every attempt:
  • Annuity due, not ordinary. Retirees spend at the start of the year. If the question says "expenses at the beginning of each year", multiply the ordinary annuity by (1 + r*).
  • Use the real rate in the withdrawal phase, not the nominal post-retirement return. Using 7% instead of 0.9434% understates the corpus by roughly two-thirds.
  • Retirement years = life expectancy − retirement age, not minus current age. Suresh needs 25 years of income, not 45.
If the real rate comes out negative (post-retirement return below inflation), the annuity formula still works — the factor simply exceeds the number of years. Don't "fix" it by flipping the sign.

Distribution products and reverse mortgage

Chapter 5.3 & 12.9

  • SWP from mutual funds — flexible, reversible, and tax-efficient because each redemption is part capital, part gain. The residual corpus keeps compounding and is inheritable. Downside: no longevity guarantee.
  • SCSS + POMIS ladder — guaranteed, quarterly/monthly, but capped (₹30 L and ₹9/15 L) and fully taxable.
  • Bucket strategy — Bucket 1: 2–3 years of expenses in liquid/ultra-short. Bucket 2: 5–7 years in short/medium debt. Bucket 3: the rest in equity. Refill upward annually. This is the standard structural answer to sequence-of-returns risk.
  • Reverse mortgage — age 60+, self-occupied and self-acquired residential property, clear title. Loan tenure typically up to 20 years. Borrower keeps possession for life; loan settled from sale after the death of the borrower and surviving spouse, and heirs may redeem it.
  • Reverse Mortgage Loan enabled Annuity (RMLeA) — the bank pays the lump sum to an insurer, which pays a lifetime annuity. Solves the "what if I outlive the 20-year tenure" problem of a plain reverse mortgage.
Reverse mortgage receipts are not income. Sec 10(43) exempts them, and Sec 47(xvi) says the mortgage itself is not a "transfer", so no capital gain arises at that point. Capital gains arise only when the property is finally sold. But in RMLeA, the annuity portion is taxable — the loan is exempt, the annuity built from it is not.
Reverse mortgage converts an illiquid use-asset into an income stream without losing possession. Its real-world problem in India is not tax — it is that heirs object, and lenders have almost stopped offering it.

The dual-track problem

The Income-tax Act, 2025 came into force on 1 April 2026, repealing the Income-tax Act, 1961. Three consequences you need to hold in your head simultaneously:
  • "Previous Year" and "Assessment Year" are gone. A single Tax Year (1 April – 31 March) replaces both. Income earned in Tax Year 2026-27 is assessed for Tax Year 2026-27. Full stop.
  • Section numbers have changed — 800+ sections became 536. Sec 87A → clause 156; the new regime (115BAC) → Sec 202; Chapter VI-A deductions consolidated into Sec 123 with Schedule XV; 80TTB → Sec 130; TDS on salary (192) → Sec 392, all other TDS (194-series) → Sec 393; TCS → Sec 394; CII → Sec 72(8)(a).
  • The substance is almost entirely unchanged. Rates, slabs, holding periods, exemptions and computation logic carry over. It is a re-presentation, not a rewrite of policy.
For the exam: answer with the 1961 Act sections and the PY/AY vocabulary your workbook uses. NISM marks against the workbook, and the X-B workbook's Annexure 1 is built on FY 2024-25. Use the new numbering only in professional work.
Do not "correct" a question. If an MCQ asks which section governs the rebate and offers 87A, answer 87A. If a caselet says "for AY 2025-26", stay in AY 2025-26. The dual track is a professional asset and an exam liability — keep them separated.

Framework and key concepts

Chapter 7.1–7.3

  • Previous Year (PY) — the year income is earned. Assessment Year (AY) — the following year, in which it is taxed. PY 2024-25 → AY 2025-26.
  • Person under Sec 2(31) — seven categories: Individual, HUF, Company, Firm, AOP/BOI, Local authority, Artificial Juridical Person.
  • Assessee — anyone liable to pay tax or any other sum. Includes a representative assessee (trustee, guardian, agent of a non-resident) and a deemed assessee.
  • Income — inclusive definition, Sec 2(24). Includes profits, dividends, capital gains, winnings, voluntary contributions to a trust, and gifts caught by Sec 56(2)(x).
  • Exceptions to the PY rule (taxed in the same year): shipping business of non-residents, persons leaving India, AOP/BOI formed for a short purpose, persons transferring assets to avoid tax, discontinued business.
A firm and a company are separate "persons" from their partners and shareholders. An HUF is a person distinct from its karta and coparceners — which is precisely why HUF is an estate-planning and tax-splitting tool, and why clubbing provisions exist to limit the game.

Residential status — three categories, two tests

Chapter 7.4 · very high yield

Every taxpayer is one of three: ROR (resident and ordinarily resident), RNOR (resident but not ordinarily resident), or NR (non-resident). Two tests, applied in order, decide which.

TEST 1 — Resident, or Non-Resident? Sec 6(1). Satisfy either: (a) in India for 182 days or more this year, OR (b) in India for 60 days or more this year AND 365 days or more across the 4 preceding years Neither → NON-RESIDENT. Stop here. TEST 2 — Ordinarily resident, or not? Sec 6(6). Satisfy both: (i) resident in at least 2 of the 10 preceding years, AND (ii) in India for 730 days or more across the 7 preceding years Both → ROR. Fail either → RNOR.
RNOR is the category to understand — the other two are just its boundaries. RNOR is the transition status: you are in India enough to be a resident, but not long enough to be taxed on your worldwide income. It is where a returning NRI sits for the first two or three years back, and it is the single most valuable window in Indian personal tax planning.

Section 60 gives the transferor nothing. Assigning the rent of your flat to your brother while keeping the flat means the rent is still taxed in your hands. Clubbing follows the asset, and it follows the income the asset produces — but not the income the clubbed income produces. Gift ₹10 L to your wife, she earns ₹70,000 interest → clubbed with you. She reinvests that ₹70,000 and earns ₹5,000 → hers. Second-generation income escapes.

And a gift to a spouse is itself exempt under Sec 56 (relative), so the transfer is tax-free — it is only the income that gets clubbed. Two different questions, two different answers.

PROSPECT THEORY (Kahneman & Tversky) — three components 1. Reference dependence outcomes are judged as gains or losses from a reference point (usually the purchase price), not as levels of wealth 2. Loss aversion the pain of a loss is roughly 2 to 2.5× the pleasure of an equivalent gain 3. Probability weighting small probabilities are over-weighted (lottery tickets, tail-risk insurance); large ones are under-weighted The value function is S-shaped: CONCAVE for gains → risk-averse when ahead → sell winners too early CONVEX for losses → risk-SEEKING when behind → hold losers too long STEEPER for losses → the kink at the origin is loss aversion itself
The S-curve explains the disposition effect in one picture. Concave above the origin, convex below it, steeper below than above. If you can draw that curve you can answer almost any Prospect Theory question.
Loss aversion ≠ risk aversion. Risk aversion is dislike of uncertainty at any wealth level. Loss aversion is asymmetry around a reference point — and it makes people risk-seeking when they are down. "Averaging down on a falling stock to get back to breakeven" is loss aversion producing risk-seeking behaviour, not risk aversion.
Bounded rationality (Herbert Simon): people have limited information, limited cognitive capacity and limited time, so they satisfice — pick the first option that is good enough — rather than optimise. Heuristics are the shortcuts this produces; biases are the systematic errors those shortcuts cause.

Two mnemonics that hold up under pressure:
  • Belief perseverance = "C.C.R.I.H." — Conservatism, Confirmation, Representativeness, Illusion of control, Hindsight.
  • Information processing = "A.M.F.A." — Anchoring, Mental accounting, Framing, Availability.
  • Emotional = "LOSSER" — Loss aversion, Overconfidence, Status quo, Self-control, Endowment, Regret. (Affinity is the seventh, listed separately in some texts.)
The three that get mis-sorted every single time:
  • Loss aversion is EMOTIONAL, even though it looks like a reasoning error. It is a feeling about pain, not a mistake about facts.
  • Mental accounting is COGNITIVE, even though it feels emotional. It is a data-processing failure — money is fungible and the investor is failing to treat it so.
  • Overconfidence is EMOTIONAL. Illusion of control, which sounds identical, is cognitive (belief perseverance). Overconfidence is about your ability; illusion of control is about your influence on outcomes.

Behavioural finance in practice — emotions, nudges, and the adviser's role

Chapter 17

  • Emotions in goal setting: goals framed emotionally ("my daughter graduating from a good college") are pursued far more persistently than goals framed numerically ("₹60 lakh in 12 years"). The adviser's job is to attach the number to the picture, not to replace the picture with the number.
  • Goals-based / behavioural portfolio theory: build a pyramid of layers — a safety layer (protection, contingency), an aspiration layer (goals), and a speculative apex. Each layer is its own mental account with its own risk tolerance. It is technically sub-optimal on a mean-variance frontier, and it is followed, which the optimal portfolio is not.

A nudge preserves choice. If it removes an option, forbids something, or imposes a cost, it is a mandate or an incentive — not a nudge. That definitional line is examinable.
The adviser's role in a crash is not to forecast. It is to reframe (horizon, goal), re-anchor to the plan document, ration information (stop the client checking daily), and offer a small controlled action — a partial rebalance — so the client feels agency without abandoning the plan. "Do nothing" is the right advice and the wrong thing to say.
Correct the cognitive, accommodate the emotional — but there is a third move: build the bias into the plan. A client with severe loss aversion is better served by a slightly lower-equity portfolio they will actually hold through a drawdown than by the theoretically correct portfolio they will sell at the bottom.

Risk profiling — ability, willingness, and need

Chapter 18.1–18.2

THREE DIMENSIONS — and they are not the same thing Risk capacity / ability objective. How much loss the client can absorb without derailing a goal. Measurable. Risk tolerance / willingness subjective. How much loss the client can stomach. Psychometric. Risk required / need the return the plan needs, and therefore the risk that must be taken to reach the goals. The recommendation is driven by the LOWER of ability and willingness. If risk required exceeds that, the GOAL must change — not the risk taken.

Age is not risk tolerance. A 28-year-old sole earner supporting parents with no emergency fund has low capacity; a 62-year-old with a pension covering all expenses and a ₹6 crore corpus has high capacity. Any answer option that reads "recommend equity because the client is young" without checking dependants, liabilities and cover is the distractor.
Willingness above ability → advise down, and document it. The client's enthusiasm does not create capacity. Ability above willingness → educate, then go at the client's pace. Never solve a shortfall by overriding a stated tolerance — under SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations that is a suitability failure, and it is the marked wrong answer even when the maths would have worked out.
Risk profiling is a regulatory obligation, not a sales aid: the adviser must assess risk, document it, obtain the client's confirmation, communicate the profile, and review it periodically. The tool must be validated and free of leading questions.
