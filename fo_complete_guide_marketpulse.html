01 — Foundation

What is a Derivative?

The word "derive" means to get something from something else. A derivative is a financial contract whose value comes from — or is derived from — an underlying asset.

🧩
The core idea
A derivative is a contract. You don't own the underlying asset (the stock, gold, currency etc.) — you own a contract based on its price. The contract's value moves up and down as the asset's price moves. Derivatives can be based on stocks, indices (Nifty, Sensex), gold, silver, crude oil, currencies like USD/INR, and even interest rates.
Real-life analogy
Imagine you agree with a tomato farmer: "I'll buy 1 kg of tomatoes from you next month for ₹40/kg — no matter what the market price is that day." This agreement itself is a derivative. Its value depends on the tomato price. If tomatoes go to ₹70/kg in the market, your contract is very valuable. If they fall to ₹20/kg, the farmer is happy but you're stuck paying ₹40.

02 — Key Metric

Open Interest (OI)

Open Interest is the pulse of the derivatives market. It tells you how many contracts are currently active — not yet closed, not yet expired.

What OI measures
OI is the total number of outstanding derivative contracts. If 1,000 people buy Nifty futures and 1,000 people sell them, OI = 1,000 (not 2,000, because every contract has one buyer and one seller). OI rises when new contracts are created and falls when contracts are closed or expired.
OI signals to watch
Rising OI + Rising Price → Strong bullish trend. New bulls entering.

Falling OI + Rising Price → Short covering. Bears exiting, not fresh bulls.

Rising OI + Falling Price → Strong bearish trend. New bears entering.

Falling OI + Falling Price → Long unwinding. Bulls exiting.
Where to find it: NSE publishes OI data for every contract daily at nseindia.com. It is updated throughout the trading session. Most trading platforms (Zerodha, Groww, Angel One) show OI alongside price in their F&O sections.
03 — Options Basics

Call Options & Put Options

Options give you a right — not an obligation. There are two types: Call (right to buy) and Put (right to sell). You pay a premium to get this right.

Call Option
Right to BUY at a fixed price
A Call option gives the buyer the right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price before the expiry date. If the market price rises above the strike, the Call becomes profitable. If it doesn't, you simply lose the premium.
Example — Reliance Call
Reliance is at ₹2,800. You buy a Call at Strike ₹2,900, paying ₹50 premium.

If Reliance rises to ₹3,100:
Profit = ₹3,100 − ₹2,900 − ₹50 = ₹150 per share

If Reliance stays at ₹2,800:
Loss = only ₹50 premium. Nothing more.
Put Option
Right to SELL at a fixed price
A Put option gives the buyer the right to sell the underlying asset at the strike price before the expiry date. If the market price falls below the strike, the Put becomes profitable. It works like insurance — protecting you from a fall.
Example — Reliance Put (as insurance)
You hold Reliance shares at ₹2,800. Worried about a crash, you buy a Put at Strike ₹2,700 paying ₹40.

If Reliance crashes to ₹2,400:
Put profit = ₹2,700 − ₹2,400 − ₹40 = ₹260 per share
This offsets your portfolio loss.
Key rule: The "limited loss = only the premium" protection belongs exclusively to the option BUYER. The option SELLER collects the premium but faces potentially unlimited loss if the market moves against them. Never confuse the two roles.
04 — Contract Type

Futures Contracts

Unlike options, futures are obligations. Both parties must settle. You don't pay the full value upfront — only a margin deposit. But losses can exceed that margin.

⏩
How a Futures contract works
A Futures contract is a binding agreement to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on a specific future date. It is standardised — fixed lot size, fixed expiry date (last Tuesday of every month on NSE, effective September 2025). Settlement happens daily through Mark-to-Market (MTM) — your account is credited or debited every evening based on that day's price movement.

Both profit when the price rises. So why are they different? The mechanism — and more importantly, the risk — is completely different.

🔴
Same stock, same view — two completely different outcomes
Reliance is at ₹2,800. Both Ravi and Suresh are bullish — they expect it to rise to ₹3,100 next month.

Ravi buys a Reliance Call Option — Strike ₹2,900, Premium ₹50 per share. He pays ₹50 and waits.
Suresh buys Reliance Futures — at ₹2,800, depositing ₹30,000 margin.

Scenario: Reliance falls sharply to ₹2,500 instead.

Ravi (Call buyer): Call expires worthless. Loss = ₹50 premium only. He goes home, no margin call, no stress.
Suresh (Futures buyer): Loss = (2,800 − 2,500) = ₹300 per share × lot size. Loss of ₹30,000+ — exceeds margin. Broker calls him at 9 AM asking for more funds or they'll auto-close his position.

Same stock. Same direction. Completely different risk. That is the core difference between buying options and trading futures.
Beginner takeaway: If Nifty falls from 22,500 to 22,000 — a Futures buyer loses (500 × 75) = ₹37,500. This comes out of their margin but can exceed it if the fall is large. A Call Option buyer on the same view loses only their premium — say ₹26,250 — no matter how far Nifty falls. For a beginner, always start by buying options, never selling them.
06 — OTC Contracts

Forwards — The Original Derivative

Futures were invented to fix the problems of Forwards. Understanding Forwards helps you appreciate why exchanges exist.

🤝
What is a Forward contract?
A Forward is a private, customised agreement between two parties to buy/sell an asset at a price agreed today, for delivery at a future date. No exchange involved — no third party. Just a signed contract between two parties.
Classic example
A wheat farmer and a flour mill. April: farmer expects to harvest wheat in October; mill needs wheat then. Both worried about price swings. They agree today: "Farmer sells, mill buys — 10 tonnes at ₹2,000/quintal in October." Private deal. No NSE, no regulator. This agreement is a Forward contract.

✕
Counterparty risk: If the other party goes bankrupt, you have no protection. You must sue in court. No exchange backs you.
✕
No liquidity: You cannot sell your forward contract to someone else. You are stuck with the original party until expiry.
✕
No price discovery: No public price exists. You don't know if you got a fair deal compared to the market.
✕
No daily settlement: Losses accumulate silently until expiry — you discover the full damage only at the end.
✕
Hard to regulate: No exchange means no regulator oversight. The 2008 global financial crisis happened partly because of trillions in unregulated forward-like contracts (credit default swaps) going wrong simultaneously.
07 — Trading Language

Long & Short — What Traders Actually Mean

These two words appear in every F&O conversation. Once you understand them, market discussions start making complete sense.

Long Position
You BOUGHT — you want price to rise
Going "Long" means you have bought a contract or asset. You own it. You profit when the price rises. You are betting the market will go UP. "I'm long Nifty" = I bought Nifty futures and I need it to rise to profit.
Short Position
You SOLD first — you want price to fall
Going "Short" means you sold something you don't own yet — betting the price will fall. You borrow and sell now, plan to buy back cheaper later. The profit is the difference. "I'm short Gold" = I sold Gold futures, expecting a fall.
🏏
Short selling in everyday language
Your friend has a cricket bat worth ₹5,000. You believe its price will fall. You borrow the bat, sell it for ₹5,000 today. Next month, the price falls to ₹3,000. You buy one back, return it to your friend.

Profit = ₹5,000 − ₹3,000 = ₹2,000. You never owned the bat — you sold first, bought later. This is short selling.

But if the price rises to ₹8,000, you still must return it → Loss = ₹3,000. Short selling losses are unlimited because prices can rise indefinitely.
Memory trick: Long = you OWN it and want price to go UP. Short = you OWE it and want price to go DOWN. In any F&O conversation, if someone says "long Reliance" they bought it. "Short Gold" means they sold Gold futures expecting a fall.
08 — The Four Positions

Long Call · Short Call · Long Put · Short Put

There are two separate decisions every options trader makes: (1) Do I buy or sell the contract? (2) Is the contract a Call or a Put? Combined, they create four distinct positions — each with a completely different risk profile.

The confusion cleared up: Call = right to BUY the underlying asset. Put = right to SELL the underlying asset. These describe what the CONTRACT does. Long/Short describes what YOU do with the contract — buy it (Long) or sell it (Short). They are independent choices that combine to create your position.

09 — Mechanics

Margin — The Security Deposit

You don't pay the full value of a futures contract. You pay a fraction — the margin — as collateral. But margin is not your maximum loss.

What margin is
Margin is the deposit the exchange requires from futures and option sellers to ensure they can honour their obligations. It is calculated using SPAN (Standard Portfolio Analysis of Risk) + Exposure Margin. It gets collected from your account the moment you enter a futures or option-selling position.
What happens when it runs out
If daily MTM losses erode your margin below the maintenance level, your broker issues a Margin Call — demanding you top up within hours. If you don't, they auto-close your position at the market price, locking in whatever loss exists at that moment.

10 — Risk Management

Hedging with a Put Option

Hedging means taking an opposite position in derivatives to protect an existing investment. A Long Put is the most common hedging tool for equity portfolios.

🛡️
The setup
You own a portfolio worth ₹10 lakhs. Worried about a market crash (election results, budget announcement, RBI policy), you buy a Long Put option with strike ₹11.5 lakhs, paying a premium. This gives you the right to sell your portfolio at ₹11.5L — no matter how far the market falls. Three scenarios can play out at expiry.
📊 Scenario 1 — Market stays flat at ₹10L

Your portfolio has no gain, no loss. But your Put strike (₹11.5L) is above the market price (₹10L) — so the Put is "in the money." You exercise it and earn the difference.

Portfolio P&L₹0 (no change)
Put exercised? (11.5L > 10L)Yes — in the money
Put profit (11.5L − 10L)+₹1,50,000
Less: premium paid−Premium
Net result₹11.5L − premium (better than doing nothing)
📈 Scenario 2 — Market rises to ₹15L

Your portfolio gained ₹5L. The Put strike (₹11.5L) is now below the market (₹15L). You would never sell at ₹11.5L when the market offers ₹15L. The Put expires unused.

Portfolio profit (15L − 10L)+₹5,00,000
Put exercised?No — out of the money, expires worthless
Less: premium paid (sunk cost)−Premium
Net result₹15L − premium (full upside captured!)
📉 Scenario 3 — Market crashes to ₹5L

Without a hedge, you'd lose ₹5L. But your Put lets you sell at ₹11.5L when the market is only ₹5L. The Put profit more than covers your portfolio loss.

Portfolio loss (10L − 5L)−₹5,00,000

The beautiful asymmetry: In all three scenarios, your effective floor is ₹11.5L minus the premium. When the market rises above ₹11.5L, you benefit fully — unlimited upside. When it crashes, the Put provides proportionally more protection. The worse the crash, the more the Put pays. That's why fund managers buy Long Puts before high-uncertainty events.
11 — Exchange Safety

What Happens When the Option Seller Exits?

This is the most insightful beginner question: if the Put seller panics and squares off at a stop loss, does your hedge break? No — and here's exactly why.

You — Put Buyer
Your position is with the EXCHANGE, not the seller
When you bought the Put, the exchange registered it. Your contract is with NSE/MCX — not with any individual. The original seller can exit, go bankrupt, or disappear. Your Put remains perfectly intact.
The Put Seller
They can only exit by buying back the Put
The seller cannot "cancel" your contract or "execute a sell" on it. Their only way out is to buy back the same Put contract in the market — from a new Put seller who takes over the obligation. Your position is unaffected throughout.
1
Exchange as central counterparty: NSE/MCX stands between every buyer and seller. When you buy a Put, your counterparty is the exchange — not the person who sold it.
2
Margin collected upfront from sellers: Before the seller can write a Put, the exchange collects large margin (₹2–5 lakh for a single lot). This ensures they can pay if the trade goes against them.
3
Daily MTM settlement: Every day, losses are deducted from the seller's margin automatically. No silent accumulation — losses are settled daily.
4
Auto square-off: If the seller's margin runs out, the broker forcibly closes their position. A new seller steps in seamlessly. Your Put is unaffected.
5
Investor Protection Fund: NSE/BSE maintain an Investor Protection Fund as the final safety net even if a broker defaults. Your rights as an option buyer are institutionally protected.
🏃
The relay race analogy
Your Put contract is the baton. You hold it in your hand. The runners (sellers) behind you can keep changing — one drops out, another picks up. The baton never leaves your hand. You don't care who is running behind you, because the exchange is the race director ensuring the baton is always passed correctly.
Practical insight: Most Indian retail option traders never actually exercise their option at expiry. Instead, they sell the option back in the market when its premium has risen to their target profit. If you bought a Put for ₹50K and the market falls sharply, your Put's market premium might rise to ₹2L — you simply sell it back and pocket ₹1.5L profit without any formal exercise.
12 — Strategy

Speculation in F&O

Speculators take positions purely to profit from expected price movements — with no underlying asset to protect. They provide crucial liquidity to the market.

🎲
How speculation works
A speculator has no Reliance shares, no gold jewellery to protect — they simply have a view. "Nifty will fall 2,000 points before election results." They express this view through F&O, taking on calculated risk for the potential of large returns. Without speculators, hedgers would have no one to trade with — speculators provide the liquidity that makes the market function.
Speculation example — Nifty Put
You expect Nifty to fall from 24,000 to 22,000 before month end. You buy 10 Nifty Put options, Strike 23,500, paying ₹200 premium each (lot size 75).

Total cost = ₹200 × 75 × 10 = ₹1,50,000

If Nifty falls to 22,500:
Put profit = (23,500 − 22,500 − 200) × 75 × 10 = ₹6,00,000

Return on ₹1.5L investment = 4x in one month. That's the allure — and the danger — of speculation.
Warning: SEBI data consistently shows that over 90% of retail F&O traders lose money. Leverage amplifies both gains and losses equally. Never speculate with money you cannot afford to lose entirely.
13 — Asset Classes

Currency & Commodity Derivatives

F&O isn't limited to stocks and indices. Entire markets exist for currency pairs and physical commodities.

Currency Derivatives
Traded on NSE/BSE
Currency derivatives let you trade exchange rate movements between currency pairs like USD/INR, EUR/INR, GBP/INR, and JPY/INR. Lot size for USD/INR = $1,000. Used heavily by Indian exporters and importers to hedge against currency risk.
Exporter hedging example
An IT company will receive $10L in 3 months. They fear USD will weaken from ₹82 to ₹79. They sell USD/INR futures at ₹82. If USD falls to ₹79, the futures profit (~₹30L) offsets the revenue loss. Currency risk eliminated.
Commodity Derivatives
Traded on MCX
Commodity derivatives are based on physical goods — Gold, Silver, Crude Oil, Natural Gas, Cotton, Aluminium. MCX (Multi Commodity Exchange) is India's primary commodity exchange. Used by producers, manufacturers, and traders to lock in prices.
Gold futures hedge
A jeweller needs 1 kg gold in 2 months. Price today = ₹72,000/10g. Worried about a rise, they buy MCX Gold Futures at ₹72,000. If gold rises to ₹78,000, their futures profit offsets the higher purchase price. Cost locked in.
