1. What "estate" actually means

An estate isn't just "the house and the bank account" — it's everything you own or control, in whatever form (sole name, joint, in a trust), plus anything that gets created on your death, like a life insurance payout. The syllabus splits it three ways:

Gross estate

Real estate, bank accounts, investments, businesses, retirement accounts (NPS), life insurance — anything that determines your net worth.

Residue estate

Personal items not named in a will/trust — car, jewellery, furniture, clothes — plus any outstanding payments owed to you at death.

Estate debt

What you owe: home/car loans, credit card dues, business liabilities, unpaid taxes, pending legal claims.

One more distinction worth keeping straight: estate planning is about your personal assets and applies to everyone. Succession planning is specifically about handing over a family business while you're alive — different question, different timeline.

2. Dying without a will (intestate)

Indian Succession Act, 1925, Section 30: you're "intestate" for any asset you haven't validly disposed of by will. When that happens, your legal heirs must apply to a civil court for a succession certificate, and the law that decides who gets what depends entirely on your religion — this is the single most important branching point in the whole chapter.

ReligionTestamentary (with a will)Intestate (no will)
Hindus, Jains, Sikhs, BuddhistsIndian Succession Act, 1925Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (as amended 2005)

Muslims are the outlier — no single Indian statute governs them either way; it's entirely religious-text-based, and it differs between Shias and Sunnis.

3. The 5 building blocks of an estate plan

ElementWhat it does
WillSays who inherits what, and names a guardian for minor children or dependants with special needs.
TrustA separate legal arrangement holding assets for a beneficiary. Can be created via a will (testamentary trust) — but then it still goes through probate.
Power of AttorneyLets someone manage your finances if you're incapacitated. Commonly used by NRIs to manage Indian assets remotely.
Living willAn advance directive to physicians for end-of-life situations (terminal illness, coma, advanced dementia) when you can't communicate decisions yourself.
Nomination & beneficiary designationTechnically not a formal "component," but essential in practice. A nominee is only a custodian until assets reach the real legal heirs — except under the new Insurance Act, where a parent/spouse/child nominee automatically becomes a beneficial nominee and can actually keep the money.

4. Hindu Succession Act, 1956 — male dying intestate

Only 4 people inherit directly: widow, son, daughter, mother. Everyone else on the Class I list is standing in for a son or daughter who died first — read every long entry as "this person, because their parent predeceased."

How the property actually splits (Section 10)

Not a headcount — it's shares: Widow (all widows together) = 1 share · each surviving son/daughter/mother = 1 share each · the entire branch of a pre-deceased child = 1 share, split again inside that branch.

Example: widow + 2 surviving sons + 1 surviving daughter + mother + one pre-deceased son (survived by his widow and 2 children) = 6 shares (1/6 each). The pre-deceased son's 1/6 is then split three ways inside his branch → his widow, son and daughter each get 1/18 of the whole estate.

Priority order (Section 8) — when do you even reach Class II?

You only move right if zero heirs survive in the box before it — Class I and II take everything before an agnate or cognate ever sees a rupee.

Class II — 9 entries, tried strictly in order

Class II isn't a representation tree like Class I — nobody "steps into" anyone's shoes here. It's a strict priority ladder (Section 9): entry I is tried first, and you only drop to entry II if entry I is completely empty, and so on.

Direct ascendant — father alone Your own siblings & their descendants Extended paternal family Extended maternal family

Read the shape of the ladder, not just the boxes: your own siblings and their children (teal) are exhausted before the law even looks at your father's parents or siblings (blue) — and your mother's side of the family (pink) is the very last resort in the whole of Class II. Entry VI is the odd one out, pairing a paternal in-law (father's widow, i.e. a stepmother) with a sibling's widow in the same entry.

Caselet from the chapter: unmarried Hindu man dies intestate, survived by 1 brother and 2 sisters. No widow, no children, no mother → zero Class I heirs → falls to Class II. Brother and sisters are all in Entry II, so all three inherit equally.

5. Hindu Succession Act — female dying intestate (Section 15)

Before 1956, a woman only held property as a "limited owner." Section 14 fixed that — anything she possesses is her absolute property. Section 15 then lays out her own succession order, which runs through her husband's side before her own parents':

Notice the shape: her own children and husband come first (same instinct as the male's Class I), but if none survive, the estate goes to her husband's family before it ever reaches her own parents.

6. Muslim personal law

No single Indian statute — succession follows religious sources (the Quran, the Prophet's practice, scholarly consensus, and analogical reasoning), and the rules differ between Shias and Sunnis. There's no concept of inheriting a fixed "birthright" while the ancestor is alive — rights crystallise only on the date of death, and only for heirs who are themselves still living on that date.

The share sizes that get tested:
  • Wife: 1/4 if no lineal descendants, otherwise 1/8
  • Husband: 1/2 if no lineal descendants, otherwise 1/4
  • Sole daughter: 1/2. Two or more daughters together: 2/3
  • If both sons and daughters survive, daughters stop being sharers and become residuary — the leftover is split so each son gets exactly double what each daughter gets.

Two ways the estate gets carved up

MethodUsed byHow it works
Per capitaSunni lawEstate divided equally among heirs — share depends only on headcount.

Unlike Hindu law, Muslim law makes no distinction between self-acquired and ancestral property — everything you hold, however you got it, passes to your heirs the same way.

Women, widows, and a few edge cases

Rights of daughtersBoth sons and daughters inherit — but a female heir's share is always half of a male heir's in the same position.
Widow — childless1/4 of the estate (after funeral costs, debts).
Widow — with children/grandchildren1/8 of the estate.
Deathbed marriageIf a man marries during his final illness and dies of it without recovering or consummating the marriage, the widow has no inheritance right. But if he divorces her during that illness and then dies, her right continues until she remarries.
Child in the wombEntitled to a share only if born alive; if stillborn, treated as if that share never existed.
No legal heir at allEscheat — the government inherits.
The will (Wasiyat)Anyone 15+ and of sound mind can make one — written, oral, or even by gesture, no formalities required. But it can only dispose of up to 1/3 of net assets; the remaining 2/3 must go to the heirs. Freely revocable anytime, expressly or by implication (e.g. later selling the same property).

7. Married Women's Property Act, 1874

Purpose: ring-fence a married woman's own property from her husband's creditors and tax troubles. Applies to any woman who was Hindu, Muslim, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain at marriage (or whose husband was).

What counts as her separate propertyHer own wages/earnings from employment or trade she runs herself (not her husband's business); income from her own literary, artistic or scientific work; and all savings and investments made from that income.
Her own insurance policyShe can insure her own life independently of her husband — fully valid, and the proceeds are her separate property, same as if she were unmarried.
His policy, for her benefitIf a husband takes a policy on his own life "for the benefit of his wife (and/or children)," it's treated as a trust for them. It stays outside his estate and is protected from his creditors, so long as the trust's purpose still exists.
Her legal standingShe can sue in her own name to protect her separate property, with the same civil and criminal remedies as an unmarried woman.
Her post-nuptial debtsIf she contracts a debt after marriage, the creditor can sue her and recover it out of her separate property, as if she were unmarried.
His liability for her ante-nuptial (pre-marriage) debtsMarriage alone doesn't make the husband liable — she remains liable to the extent of her separate property.
His liability if she's a trusteeHe isn't liable for her breach of trust or mismanagement as an executrix/trustee — unless he personally interferes in the administration.

8. Mutation

Mutation is simply updating the revenue records to show a new name against a property — done at the state level, and it exists mainly so the tax department knows who to bill.

The exam trap: mutation is not proof that you own the property — the proceedings aren't judicial. Title comes from the underlying deed/inheritance, not from the revenue record. The one thing mutation does do: where there are co-owners, it stops any single one of them from claiming sole ownership based on the record.

9. Worked examples

Class I — direct heirs only, no representation

Suresh dies intestate. Survived by his widow Lakshmi, his mother Kamala, and two children, Arun and Meena — nobody predeceased him.

4 people, 4 equal units: Lakshmi 1/4 · Kamala 1/4 · Arun 1/4 · Meena 1/4.

Class I — representation, one branch predeceased

Same family, except Suresh's third child, Vijay, died two years before him, survived by his widow Sunita and two children, Rohan and Priya.

Now 5 units: Lakshmi 1/5 · Kamala 1/5 · Arun 1/5 · Meena 1/5 · Vijay's branch 1/5, and that last 1/5 splits three ways — Sunita, Rohan and Priya each get roughly 1/15.

Class I — the gap the Schedule doesn't cover

Vijay's daughter Radha (item 6) herself predeceases Suresh, leaving a son Karan and a daughter Anjali.

Anjali qualifies under item 16 and takes a share. Karan gets nothing under Class I — there's no Schedule entry for "son of a pre-deceased daughter of a pre-deceased son." He'd only be checked under Class II, Entry II(1), and only if Class I turns out completely empty.

Class II — one entry takes everything

Suresh has zero Class I heirs, survived by his father's brother (Entry VII) and his mother's brother (Entry IX).

Entry VII is checked first and isn't empty, so the father's brother takes the entire estate. The mother's brother gets nothing — entries never blend, and closeness in plain English doesn't matter, only entry order.

Class II — equal split within one entry

Suresh has zero Class I heirs, survived by one brother, one sister, and a son's-daughter's-son — all three sit in Entry II.

They split equally: 1/3 each, even though the siblings feel closer than a grandnephew.

Married Women's Property Act — one couple, all seven provisions

Priya runs a boutique and writes a cookbook. Her husband Ravi runs a separate business that later goes bankrupt.
  • Separate property: boutique income + cookbook royalties + savings from both are hers alone — Ravi's creditors can't touch them.
  • Her own policy: Priya insures her own life independently — the payout is entirely hers.
  • His policy, for her benefit: Ravi takes a policy on his own life "for the benefit of his wife and children" — it's treated as a trust for them, stays outside his estate, and his creditors can't touch it.
  • Her legal standing: a customer owes the boutique money — Priya can sue in her own name, no need for Ravi to be involved.
  • Her post-nuptial debt: Priya takes a loan after marriage to expand the boutique — the lender can only recover from her separate property, never Ravi's.
  • His liability for her ante-nuptial debt: Priya had an old college loan before marrying Ravi — marriage doesn't make Ravi liable for it.
  • His liability as her trustee: Priya is later appointed executrix of her aunt's estate and mismanages it — Ravi isn't liable, unless he personally steps in and interferes.

Mutation — one flat, start to finish

Your father dies and leaves you his flat. You clear the pending property tax, and the revenue authority updates its register to show your name — that's mutation, done.

Your sibling later produces a different Will claiming joint ownership. Your name in the mutation register doesn't protect you — a court decides true ownership from the actual Will/succession certificate/deed, regardless of what the revenue register says. Mental shortcut: mutation = who gets the tax bill; title = who actually owns it.
