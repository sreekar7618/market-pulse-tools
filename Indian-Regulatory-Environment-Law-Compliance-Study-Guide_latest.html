Regulatory System and Environment

RBI, SEBI, and the erstwhile FMC

India splits financial regulation by product, not by institution: whoever takes your deposit answers to RBI, whoever sells you a security answers to SEBI, whoever sells you insurance answers to IRDAI, and your pension answers to PFRDA.

RegulatorDomain
RBICredit and deposits (banks, NBFCs, UCBs)
SEBISecurities and capital markets
IRDAIInsurance
PFRDAThe National Pension System (NPS)

Each is a statutory body accountable to Parliament through the Ministry of Finance, but funded by fees from the industry it regulates — which is what gives it independence from government day to day. RBI doesn't regulate every credit entity personally either: it regulates NABARD, and NABARD in turn regulates Regional Rural Banks and co-operative banks; RBI regulates the National Housing Bank, which supervises housing finance companies. Think of it as a chain of command, not a flat list.

Reserve Bank of India

Core idea

RBI is the country's central bank: banker to the government, banker to every scheduled bank, issuer of currency, and the body that manages the money supply to keep prices stable while supporting growth.

Example

The 1991 balance-of-payments crisis is the textbook's own illustration of why RBI matters: fiscal deficits through the 1980s, then a Gulf War oil-import shock, pushed India to the edge. RBI's response — liberalisation, fiscal correction, and building up the securities and forex markets — is the reform package still shaping today's financial system.

Securities and Exchange Board of India

Core idea

A securities market exists so that people with capital can meet people who need it. SEBI's job is to keep that market fair enough that ordinary savers are willing to participate in it.

India's stock market traces back to informal cotton-share trading in 1860s Bombay; brokers formalised themselves into what became the BSE in 1875. But for most of the 20th century, capital issues were tightly controlled by the government itself (the Capital Issues Control Act, 1947). SEBI was set up in 1988 and got real teeth with the SEBI Act, 1992, passed alongside the repeal of that old Act — this is the real starting point of the modern, SEBI-run market.

FunctionWhat it means
ProtectiveStop price rigging, insider trading, fraud
DevelopmentalGrow the market — train intermediaries, push technology
RegulatoryLicense and police brokers, AMCs, advisers, takeovers, buybacks

Memory hook

SEBI's market today: ~80% of GDP in market cap, 41 million demat accounts — up from a market that barely existed before 1992. That's the scale of what the SEBI Act changed.

The Forward Markets Commission — now part of SEBI

FMC regulated commodity futures (think MCX, NCDEX) until it was merged into SEBI on September 28, 2015. Commodity futures had a chequered run in India — banned for decades, eased back in commodity by commodity from the 1980s, fully reopened in 2003. Today MCX alone carries about 92% of all commodity derivatives trading volume.

Exam traps

  • NABARD regulates RRBs/co-op banks; NHB supervises HFCs — RBI doesn't do this directly.
  • SEBI existed from 1988, but the SEBI Act (1992) is what gave it statutory power.
  • FMC merged into SEBI on September 28, 2015 — a date worth knowing cold.

Role of Regulators

Ten functions, boiled down to three jobs and a toolkit

Every Indian regulator wears three hats — lawmaker, police officer, judge — and separately runs a before/during/after playbook for firm failure, plus (for RBI specifically) the actual machinery of managing the rupee.

Three hats: rule-maker, police, judge

Core idea

A regulator writes the rules (regulation-making), enforces them (executive functions — licensing, inspection, investigation), and then also judges breaches and hands out penalties (administrative law/quasi-judicial functions). Appeals against SEBI, IRDA and PFRDA orders all go to one place: the Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT).

Worth flagging

These executive powers in India don't have standardised statutory checks — which is why the course stresses two safeguards regulators should hold themselves to: use less discretion than they could, and always explain the reasoning behind an action.

Consumer protection: caveat emptor, with training wheels

Because India regulates by sector, the same kind of product can be treated differently depending on who sells it — "regulatory arbitrage." And caveat emptor (buyer beware) still largely governs Indian finance: consumers are expected to decipher products beyond the basic disclosures. That's why every sector has built its own ombudsman or complaint system (Chapter 4).

Before, during, and after a firm fails

Core idea

The FSLRC frames it as a sequence: consumer protection governs a firm's behaviour while healthy; micro-prudential regulation reduces (never eliminates) the chance it fails; resolution — the IBC, in India's case — is what happens once it fails anyway.

Micro-prudential regulation looks at one firm at a time (regulating its entry, its risk-taking, its capital buffers, its governance, and supervising it on an ongoing basis). Systemic risk regulation is the same idea applied to the whole financial system — "the forest, not the trees," as the FSLRC puts it. Deposit insurance is the safety net underneath all of this: DICGC covers up to Rs. 500,000 per depositor per bank, across all their accounts and branches.

Money mechanics: capital controls, public debt, and monetary policy

Core idea

India's current account (trade, remittances) is freely convertible; the capital account (investment flows) is not. The Central Government makes the rules for current account transactions (with RBI's input); RBI makes the regulations for capital account transactions (with the government's input) — the reverse of what people usually assume.

RBI also runs the government's borrowing programme, aiming to fund the deficit cheaply without flooding the market — and, contrary to popular belief, this doesn't conflict with monetary policy: two specific 1997 and 2003 rules (no more ad-hoc T-bills to RBI; RBI barred from primary bond auctions) keep the government from simply printing its way out of debt.

Monetary policy itself is set by the six-member Monetary Policy Committee (3 RBI + 3 government-nominated, Governor has the casting vote), which meets at least 4 times a year to fix the benchmark rate.

Example: how the levers connect

Say RBI wants to fight inflation. It raises the CRR (the cash-only reserve every bank must park with RBI, earning nothing) — banks now have less to lend, so lending rates drift up and borrowing slows. It can also raise the repo rate (what banks pay to borrow from RBI overnight), which pushes every other rate in the economy up with it. The reverse repo rate (what RBI pays banks to park surplus cash with it) normally sits about 50 bps below repo; the MSF rate, for emergency overnight borrowing, sits about 100 bps above it. Loosen the same three dials in the other direction, and you get the classic "rate cut to boost growth" story.

Exam traps

  • Micro-prudential = one firm. Systemic risk = the whole system. Don't swap them.
  • Current account rules: Central Govt. Capital account regulations: RBI. (Reversed from what most people guess.)
  • CRR = cash with RBI, earns nothing. SLR = the bank's own cash/gold/securities cushion. Different mechanisms, both reserve ratios.
  • DICGC: Rs. 500,000 per depositor per bank (not per account, not per branch).

Acts Relevant to Corporate Entities, Securities and External Trade

Twelve Acts, each reduced to: what it's for, one number worth remembering, one example

These Acts define the entities (companies, trusts, LLPs), instruments (securities, contracts, cheques) and processes (insolvency, money laundering, foreign trade) sitting underneath every piece of financial advice.

Companies Act, 2013

Core idea

Governs how every listed and unlisted Indian company is formed, run, and — if needed — wound up. It gave shareholders real teeth (class-action suits, mandatory independent directors, CSR spending) and created the NCLT/NCLAT for fast, specialised company disputes.

Number worth rememberingValue
CSR spend, once a company crosses the net worth/turnover/profit threshold2% of average net profit (last 3 years)
Independent directors on a listed company's boardAt least 1/3
Auditor rotationEvery 5 years
Shareholders allowed in a private companyUp to 200 (was 50 under the old Act)

Example

A listed company wants to merge with its wholly-owned subsidiary. If 90% of creditors (by value) agree, the Act lets it skip the usual creditors' meeting entirely — a fast-track route that didn't exist before this Act.

Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 (SCRA)

Core idea

Defines what counts as a "security" and sets the ownership rules for who can run a stock exchange.

The one number set worth memorising

A recognised stock exchange must keep at least 51% public holding. No single ordinary person can own more than 5%; a few special categories (another exchange, a depository, an approved institution) can go up to 15% — but all foreign holders together are capped at 49%.

SEBI Act, 1992

Gives SEBI the actual powers to act: it can investigate with the same authority as a civil court, freeze bank accounts, suspend trading, and impose penalties — all appealable to the Securities Appellate Tribunal, and from there to the Supreme Court within 60 days.

FEMA, 1999

Core idea

Splits foreign exchange transactions into two buckets and hands them to two different rule-makers.

Current account (trade, remittances)Capital account (investments)
Who sets the rulesCentral Government (with RBI's input)RBI (with the government's input)

Breaking FEMA can cost up to three times the amount involved as a penalty — and unpaid penalties can lead to civil imprisonment.

PMLA, 2002

Core idea

Money laundering is dressing up the proceeds of crime as clean money. PMLA makes this a specific criminal offence — and flips the usual burden of proof: once accused, you must prove your assets are legitimate, not the other way round.

Example

A client wants you to help "invest" a large, unexplained cash sum with no clear source. Under PMLA, entities like banks and financial intermediaries (reporting entities) must flag this to the Financial Intelligence Unit – India (FIU-IND). Punishment for laundering: rigorous imprisonment of 3–7 years, up to 10 years if drugs are involved (NDPS Act).

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC)

Core idea

India's first real playbook for what happens when a company can't pay its debts: a time-bound process to either revive it or liquidate it fairly.

A creditor triggers the process at the NCLT → an Interim Resolution Professional takes over management → a Committee of Creditors decides whether to revive the company or liquidate it. If liquidation happens, the money is paid out in a strict order:

OrderWho gets paid
1stResolution process fees and costs
2ndSecured creditors (via the resolution process)
3rdWorkmen's dues, then employee wages
4thUnsecured creditors
5thGovernment dues — after unsecured creditors
LastPreference, then ordinary, shareholders

Worth flagging

Government dues ranking below unsecured creditors specifically overrides the Companies Act, 2013 — a deliberate, testable exception.

Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

Covers promissory notes (an unconditional promise to pay), bills of exchange (an unconditional order to someone else to pay), and cheques. The famous part is Section 138: a bounced cheque, for insufficient funds, is a criminal offence — 30 days to notify the drawer, 15 days for them to pay up, 1 month after that to file a complaint. Punishment: up to 2 years, or a fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both.

The rest, briefly

ActWhat to remember
Indian Trusts Act, 1882A trust needs a lawful purpose. Once created for someone's benefit, it generally can't be revoked without every beneficiary's consent — except a trust made by Will, which the author can revoke, or one that expressly reserves that right
FCRA, 1952A forward contract delivers goods within 11 days; beyond that it isn't a simple "ready delivery" deal. This Act is the legal ancestor of today's MCX/NCDEX commodity futures
Indian Contract Act, 1872A contract needs offer + acceptance + consideration + free consent + competent parties + a lawful object. Indemnity: I cover your loss. Guarantee: I (the surety) pay if a third party (the debtor) defaults, to you (the creditor). Bailment: I hand you my goods for a purpose, you return them. Pledge: bailment as security for a debt
Indian Partnership Act, 1932Partners share profits equally by default, are jointly and severally liable for the firm's acts, and can't be expelled except by a process the partnership contract itself allows
LLP Act, 2008An LLP is its own legal person — partners aren't personally liable for each other's mistakes, only their own. If it falls to one partner, that person carries full personal liability after 6 months

Exam traps

  • SCRA numbers: 51% public / 5% ordinary cap / 15% special exception / 49% total foreign cap.
  • PMLA reverses the burden of proof — the accused proves innocence, not the prosecution proving guilt.
  • IBC: government dues rank below unsecured creditors — overrides the Companies Act.
  • Cheque bounce timeline: 30 / 15 / 1 month.
  • Guarantee: surety pays, creditor receives — don't mix up who's who.

Consumer Grievances Redressal

One ladder per sector: try the entity, escalate, go to court as a last resort

Banking has its Ombudsman, securities has SCORES, insurance has its own Ombudsman, and everything has the Consumer Protection Act as the final backstop.

MechanismSinceHow it works
Banking Ombudsman199521 RBI-run offices, free to the consumer; ~96.5% of complaints resolved, two-thirds by mediation
SEBI SCORES2011Online portal for grievances against listed companies and SEBI-registered intermediaries only — the entity gets 30 days to respond before it counts as unresolved
Insurance Ombudsman199817 ombudsmen; can only decide claims up to Rs. 3 million; insurer must honour an award within 3 months

Worth flagging

SCORES will not take a complaint against a bank, NBFC, insurer, or housing finance company — those go through RBI's or IRDAI's own systems. A common trap: assuming SCORES is a universal financial-grievance portal.

Consumer Protection Act, 2019: the three-tier ladder

Core idea

Where you file a consumer complaint depends purely on the rupee value of the goods or services involved.

ForumValue of goods/servicesAppeal window
District CommissionUp to Rs. 10 million45 days to State
State CommissionRs. 10–100 million30 days to National
National CommissionAbove Rs. 100 million30 days to Supreme Court

Example

A consumer disputes a Rs. 25 million service deficiency — that falls between Rs. 10 million and Rs. 100 million, so it starts at the State Commission, not the District Commission.

The 2019 Act also created the Central Consumer Protection Authority, which can act on its own (suo moto), recall products, and go after misleading endorsements — this is why celebrity endorsers now carry personal liability for the products they promote.

Exam traps

  • SCORES excludes banks, NBFCs, insurers, pension products — sector-specific mechanisms handle those.
  • Consumer Protection Act jurisdiction is purely about rupee value, not complaint type.
  • Non-compliance with a Commission order: 1 month to 3 years imprisonment, or Rs. 25,000–100,000 fine, or both.

Other Acts, Statutes and Regulations

RTI, and SEBI's Disclosure and Investor Protection Guidelines

One law gives citizens a right to information from the government; the other forces companies to disclose everything an investor needs before a new issue.

Right to Information Act, 2005

Core idea

Any citizen can demand records from a public authority, without giving a reason — the authority must respond or explain, in writing, why it's refusing and how to appeal.

Exceptions exist for national security, cabinet papers, trade secrets, and anything that would endanger someone's safety or an ongoing investigation. But even then, the authority must release the non-exempt part of the record rather than refusing the whole thing.

SEBI (Disclosure and Investor Protection) Guidelines, 2000

Core idea

SEBI doesn't fix the price of a new share issue — but it forces the issuer to disclose everything (via a lead manager, a red herring prospectus, and a disclosed price band) so the market can arrive at a fair price itself.

Example

A company doing a rights issue can't touch the money it raises until the exchange confirms at least 90% subscription — protecting investors from a company that raised only a fraction of what it asked for.

Regulation of Market Intermediaries

Three SEBI rulebooks: for intermediaries generally, for advisers specifically, and for self-regulating bodies

There's no single licence that lets you sell insurance, give investment advice, and manage pensions — each SEBI-regulated role has its own rulebook, and they deliberately keep advice separate from distribution.

RegulationWho it coversCore rule
Intermediaries Regulations, 2008Brokers, bankers to an issue, custodians, AMCs, and moreRedress investor grievances within 45 days; no price rigging, front-running, self-dealing
Investment Advisers Regulations, 2013Anyone giving investment advice for a feeFiduciary duty; paid only by the client, never by a product provider; Financial Planning counts as investment advice
SRO Regulations, 2004Self-regulatory bodies for a market segment (not stock exchanges)Net worth ≥ INR 10 million; Board must be majority independent directors

Worth flagging — the direction of reform

Mutual fund distribution was originally treated as separate from "advice." SEBI's later proposals push distributors to choose one role or the other — you can't earn commission and call yourself a fiduciary adviser to the same client.

Self-test

Eight questions, with the wrong answers explained too

1. Who regulates Regional Rural Banks?Ch 1

A. RBI directly

B. NABARD

C. SEBI

D. NHB

Answer: B

NABARD regulates RRBs and co-op banks; NABARD itself sits under RBI. NHB's job is housing finance companies, not RRBs.

2. FMC merged into SEBI on which date?Ch 1

A. April 4, 1992

B. September 5, 2013

C. September 28, 2015

D. June 27, 2016

Answer: C

A is when the SEBI Act was promulgated; B is when FMC's administrative control shifted to the Ministry of Finance (an earlier, separate event); D is when the Monetary Policy Committee was formed.

3. Under FEMA, who regulates capital account transactions?Ch 2/3

A. Central Government alone

B. RBI, in consultation with the Central Government

C. SEBI

D. IRDAI

Answer: B

It's reversed from what people expect: RBI handles capital account regulations, while the Central Government handles current account rules. SEBI and IRDAI play no role here.

4. Under the IBC, where do government dues rank in the payout order?Ch 3

A. Above secured creditors

B. Between secured and unsecured creditors

C. Below unsecured creditors

D. Equal with employee wages

Answer: C

This specifically overrides the Companies Act, 2013 — a deliberately testable exception. All other options rank government dues too high.

5. Under the SCRA, what's the maximum combined foreign holding in a recognised stock exchange?Ch 3

A. 5%

B. 15%

C. 49%

D. 51%

Answer: C

5% is the ordinary individual cap; 15% is the special-category exception (another exchange, a depository, an approved institution); 51% is the minimum public holding requirement — the opposite kind of number.

6. A grievance against a housing finance company — where does it go?Ch 4

A. SEBI's SCORES

B. RBI/NHB's own mechanisms

C. The Insurance Ombudsman

D. IRDAI directly

Answer: B

SCORES explicitly excludes housing finance companies — they fall under RBI/NHB oversight, not SEBI. C and D relate to insurance, not housing finance.

7. A complaint involves Rs. 50 million in goods/services. Which forum hears it first?Ch 4

A. District Commission

B. State Commission

C. National Commission

D. Supreme Court

Answer: B

Rs. 50 million sits between the District cap (Rs. 10 million) and the State cap (Rs. 100 million), so it starts at the State Commission.

8. Under the Investment Advisers Regulations, 2013, who may pay the adviser?Ch 6

A. The client only

B. The product manufacturer only

C. Either the client or the manufacturer

D. A mix, as long as it's disclosed

Answer: A

The fiduciary requirement is that remuneration comes directly from the client and no other source — this is what separates an RIA from a commission-based distributor.
