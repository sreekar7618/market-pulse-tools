Estate planning is more than saving tax

The course opens with a quote from Roy Diliberto: imagine asking a dying man how he wants to be remembered and hearing, "As a person who had an estate plan that saved taxes." Nobody says that.

Point Textbook position
What estate planning looks at How a person wants to address the end of his or her life
Main focus Postmortem (after death) distribution of property / wealth — but not the only target
When the work happens Much of the planning and execution happens long before the end of life
Role of tax Important in many scenarios, but it does not define estate planning

Hook: Tax is the tool, distribution is the goal. "Nobody wants Saved Taxes on their tombstone."

What is an estate?

All the assets a person owns make up his or her estate — securities, real estate, business interests, physical possessions, cash, titles, intellectual property and anything else owned. Financial planning mostly targets accumulation and management; estate planning focuses on managed distribution.

Items that can sit in an estate (textbook list):

Category Examples
Homes Primary residence; secondary, vacation or other dwellings
Household Home furnishings, furniture, appliances, other household items, clothing
Valuables and equipment Jewellery; cameras, computers, sound equipment; sports and recreational equipment
Vehicles Cars and other vehicles
Financial Cash, bank and securities accounts, notes and structured payments
Insurance and retirement Life insurance policies (sometimes other policy types); vested pensions and other retirement plan assets
Intellectual property Authored works, copyrights, trademarks
Income streams Royalties and other income streams

The estate includes everything a person owns or has a contractual right to receive. Implication: even people who think they have little or no estate probably have more than they think.

Hook: Estate = "Own it or are owed it." If you own it, or someone must pay it to you, it is in the estate.

Titling, marital property and moving territories

Some property gets included in an estate depending on how it is titled, and titling rules vary a lot by territory. Marital / matrimonial property shows this well:

Approach How it works Effect on gifts / estate
Separate property (some territories) Husband's property belongs to the husband; wife's to the wife Neither spouse's property is in the other's estate. Each can give his or her own property freely (wife to her daughter from a prior marriage; husband to a charity)
Shared marital property (more common) Each spouse owns marital assets jointly in some fashion; division may be even or by formula In some cases neither spouse can distribute marital property alone — the wife needs the husband's express permission to give to her daughter; the husband needs the wife's approval for a large charitable donation
Pre-marriage property May be treated differently from property bought during the marriage —

Cautions from the textbook:

  • The course cannot assume how property is owned, titled and distributed — every planner must learn the laws of his or her own territory (and jurisdiction within it).
  • If an individual, married couple or unmarried partners move to another territory, the ownership and distribution laws may change, especially for jointly held property. Often property owned before the move follows the old territory's laws, but not always.
  • For cohabiting partners (unmarried, living together), rules vary substantially between territories.
  • Footnote: for ease of writing, the course treats civil partnerships as following the same rules as marriage, but that is often not true. Territories also treat separation, divorce and remarriage differently.

Example: Anita (living in a shared-marital-property territory) wants to gift a flat bought during her marriage to her daughter from her first marriage. Under shared rules she may need her current husband's written consent. Had the flat been bought before the marriage, it might be treated as her separate property.

1.1 Why terminology matters — and its limit

Every planning area has its own vocabulary (investments: standard deviation, MPT; insurance: mortality risk, law of large numbers). Estate planning has unique terms and also uses common words in specific ways.

Caution (textbook) What it means for you
The course cannot cover all terms used in all territories, or how each locale applies them Learn your own territory's usage
Most financial planners will not be estate-planning legal experts Estate planning often needs a legal background and licence
Avoid the unauthorised practice of law Partner with a competent estate-planning professional (e.g., lawyer); know when to refer

Hook: "Know it, don't draft it." A planner understands the law well enough to spot issues — the lawyer writes the documents.

1.2 Estate and the three types of property

Estate (for this course) = all rights, titles and interests a person has in any property, plus all debts and liabilities. The person may be living or dead; the term covers all owned property before death and before its distribution.

Property type Meaning (textbook) Examples
Real Land and permanent (immovable) structures on it. Also called real estate or immovable property (technically different terms; course uses "real estate / real property") House, land, building
Personal — tangible Items other than real property with a physical presence and value Car, jewellery, furniture
Personal — intangible Other than real or tangible; has value from legal, contractual rights Securities, bank notes, intellectual property (sometimes its own category); securities sometimes their own category too
Community Several states and a few territories (e.g., France, Brazil, U.S.) — property acquired and held as part of the matrimonial estate; some property may be excluded or exempt Salary savings and a flat bought during marriage

Hook: "Real, Personal, Community = Land, Things & Rights, Marriage." Personal splits into Touch (tangible) and Paper (intangible).

1.3 Estate planning, inheritance and types of interest

Estate planning = developing strategies to achieve goals about management and distribution of property, so as to efficiently accomplish tax and non-tax goals.

  • Focuses on distribution at death and plans and distribution while the owner is living.
  • Also integrates decisions on health care and incapacity.
  • Should be done long before death so distribution happens as the decedent (deceased property owner) wanted.

Inheritance = receipt of property from an estate, often as a result of estate planning. It can mean taking physical ownership of real and tangible property, or receiving legal and/or beneficial ownership of intangible property — all rights, interests and titles.

Ownership type Meaning Who has it
Legal ownership Right to manage property, including transferring it Trustee; outright owner
Beneficial (equitable) ownership Right to use and enjoy property Trust beneficiary; outright owner
Both — Heirs (those who inherit as outright owners) have both
Interest Meaning Textbook examples
Present interest Legal or beneficial ownership that allows immediate use Outright current owner; trust beneficiary receiving current income or able to demand payments
Future interest Ownership comes eventually; cannot use now Beneficiaries under a will (wait for testator's death); trust beneficiaries who get assets after income ends
Both — Trust beneficiary who gets mandatory income (or can demand it) and ultimately the corpus

Textbook example (Estelle): Her mother retitled a car in Estelle's name — full ownership now, so present interest. Her father named her heir to 50% of his estate in his will — she must wait until his death, so future interest.

Hook: Legal = Lever (you control it). Beneficial = Benefit (you enjoy it). Present = Now, Future = Later.

Exam trap: A will beneficiary has only a future interest — even if the testator is 95 and gravely ill. Nothing passes until death.

1.4 Taxation, territory and jurisdiction

Taxation is one of the major aspects of estate planning.

  • Territories may tax the estate, the heir (inheritor), or both.
  • They differ in tax structures, exemptions, inclusions, ownership and distribution requirements. Some also tax wealth as part of asset distribution.
  • The course says "taxation of the estate" to mean all these options, unless the difference matters.
  • Part of estate planning is trying to limit those taxes.
  • Wealth-transfer tax, especially on real estate, may be called stamp tax / duty; the course treats stamp duty as part of overall estate taxes.
Term (footnote) Meaning Example
Territory A nation Belgium
Jurisdiction A subdivision or state within a territory, often with different rules; also means having legal authority in an area Brussels capital region, Flemish region, Walloon region

Hook: Territory = The country; Jurisdiction = the Jigsaw pieces inside it.

1.6 Legal documents and distribution methods

Probate and the probate estate

Probate = the process of settling a decedent's estate.

  • Every estate goes through probate except assets that pass by law or by will substitute / contract.
  • It is a legal proceeding in which a court (e.g., probate court) with authority over settlement reviews the will, determines its legality and arranges whoever and whatever is needed to carry out its terms.
  • No will, or the will doesn't apply (e.g., mandatory succession rules) → estate settled by intestate succession rules.
  • Life insurance and other contracts with a named (nominated) beneficiary generally pass outside probate.
  • Probate incurs expenses and helps identify taxable property, but the probate estate is not the taxable estate. Probate is the settlement process, not the tax determination.
  • Not every territory requires a formal probate process (most do).

Probate estate = all items included in probate, i.e., that pass to heirs via the decedent's will or the intestacy statutes.

Wills

A will specifies how the owner wants the estate distributed at death (a document, except a valid oral / nuncupative will). It can:

  • name someone to administer the estate (executor or personal representative),
  • name a guardian / administrator for minor children.

It normally cannot override applicable laws or will-substitute contracts.

Form of will Textbook conditions
Legal / formal Written per applicable regulations, properly witnessed, notarised when required, approved by an authorised person or entity
Handwritten (holographic) Where allowed; must be complete, signed and dated; generally delivered to an authorised person (notary, justice of the peace)
Oral (nuncupative) Seldom allowed; must be heard by witnesses (usually at least two)
Will type When it operates
Testamentary will Comes into effect on the death of the maker (testator). Someone who dies without a valid will dies intestate
Inter vivos / living will (advance directive) Operates during the maker's life — not at death

Testamentary will details:

  • Can be simple ("all to my spouse") — but such a will is invalid where succession laws require division among children.
  • Most valid wills say how debts are handled, the percentages to each heir, may send assets into a trust or to charities, and may follow laws that limit taxes or ease distribution.
  • Must contain a statement of compliance with relevant laws.
  • Must be in legal form for the territory of domicile — a valid will may need rewriting after a move (even between states).
  • Must be current — a will written 30 years ago with no children and few assets needs revision.

Living wills, advance directives and powers of attorney

  • A living will falls within advance directives: powers of attorney, health care proxies, health care directives, advance health directives.
  • Legal status varies: full legal status in some territories, only guidance in others, not recognised in some.
  • Main function: specify the course of treatment health care providers should follow — most useful when the maker is incapacitated.
  • A power of attorney gives an authorised representative power to act for the individual. Variants: general, durable / enduring, springing, health care, physician's directive. The variety exists because a typical POA terminates when the person becomes mentally incapacitated.
  • HCCH Convention on the International Protection of Adults (January 2000): broader than living wills (includes business representation), but covers giving another person authority if one becomes incapacitated. Signatories as of 13 March 2017 (footnote): Cyprus, Czech Republic, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Luxembourg, Monaco, Netherlands, Poland, Switzerland, UK — signing does not guarantee it is in force.

Textbook illustration (partial sample — Medical Durable Power of Attorney): The maker appoints an agent to make health care decisions if and when he lacks capacity — consent to, withhold or stop treatment, talk to health personnel, see medical records and sign forms — and names Alternative One and Alternative Two in order if the agent can't act. It takes effect on incapacity and continues during it.

Will substitutes

Will substitutes = contracts that pass assets directly to a named beneficiary.

  • The most common is the life insurance contract; most territories treat it as a separate category. Proceeds often are not part of the estate and pass outside probate and succession laws.
  • No named beneficiary → proceeds probably pulled into the estate and possibly probate.
  • Some territories don't apply estate / inheritance tax or income tax to proceeds; but non-spousal beneficiaries or corporations may be taxed; calculations can depend on the beneficiary's relationship to the decedent.
  • In some territories (e.g., France) life insurance can bypass civil succession rules, letting assets pass to a wider group.
  • Other contracts may or may not pass outside probate; tax treaties matter.

Hook: Probate = Paperwork through court. Will substitutes skip the queue — the insurer pays the named person directly.

Exam trap: "A will is used to change the beneficiary of a life policy" — wrong in the textbook's logic. A will normally cannot override a will-substitute contract; change the nomination with the insurer.

1.7 Property ownership forms

Property can be owned by an individual, spouses, several non-spouses, organisations, trusts, partnerships, outright, with a mortgage, or with a life interest. How it is owned and titled affects estate handling, especially for real property like the family home.

Individual ownership

  • Simplest form. Full and complete ownership = fee simple (sometimes freehold). One person, no one else.
  • At death it needs direction from a will or a mandate by the territory:
Mandate How it works
Succession rules / forced heirship Spouse may get full ownership, or children, or a combination. Sometimes children get legal ownership while the spouse can use and enjoy it for life
Rules of intestacy Apply when there is no valid will; territory distributes by preset rules — very similar to succession rules. Spouse often first, sometimes children first or shared. If no immediate family, a line of succession (siblings, cousins, parents etc.)

Footnote: many territories prohibit foreign ownership of real estate and allow only a leasehold for years (which may pass to an heir and is part of the estate). In some territories no one may own land in fee simple — land belongs to the people and is administered by the state; citizens get leaseholds.

Joint ownership (co-ownership, shared or concurrent ownership)

Feature Joint tenancy (with right of survivorship) Tenants by the entirety Tenants in common
Who can use it Anyone (often spouses) Spouses only Anyone
Shares Equal; no one owns an individual share — each owns all Equal right to the entire property Unequal (proportionate) shares allowed
On death Share passes automatically to surviving owner(s), outside probate Passes automatically to surviving spouse, outside probate Share goes into the decedent's estate and passes by will (or intestacy)
Can a will override it? No — it is a will substitute No Yes — owner distributes his share as he likes
Selling own share — — Can sell without other owners' agreement
  • "Joint tenants with rights of survivorship" is added only to make the survivorship intent clear and distinguish it from tenants in common.
  • Strata (a subset in some territories): title to part of a property plus shared ownership of the common property — like a condominium / apartment. For estate planning, the owner can pass only his own unit's share, not the common property.

Textbook example (Ted, Ahmad, Jonas, Fredrick): Holiday home costs $200,000; they invest $50,000 / $30,000 / $80,000 / $40,000 and title it as tenants in common, so they own 25% / 15% / 40% / 20%, and each can later sell his share to the others or to an outsider.

Community (marital) property

  • Where recognised: all property acquired and held as part of the matrimonial estate, including debts.
  • Includes all property acquired after marriage, unless declared separate. Inherited property and property each brings into the marriage are not automatically community property.
  • While living: a debt incurred by one becomes owed by both; property bought by one is owned by both. Tax treatment varies.
  • At death: some community-property places treat it like tenants by the entirety (all to surviving spouse) — but not always; children may take priority and the spouse gets only a portion, unless legal arrangements were made before death.
  • Footnote: in South Africa, the standard marriage is "in community of property", covering everything owned or owed by either partner before and during the marriage (can be amended).

Worked example (textbook Example 1 — Susan, Jessica, Violet, Helen): House $400,000; investments $100,000 / $100,000 / $125,000 / $75,000; titled tenants in common. Shares: Susan 100 ÷ 400 = 25%, Jessica 25%, Violet 125 ÷ 400 = 31.25%, Helen 75 ÷ 400 = 18.75% (total 100%). If Susan dies, the others have no automatic right to her share — it passes to her estate. Correct answer: B.

Hook: Joint tenancy = "Last man standing takes all." Tenancy in common = "My slice goes to my will." Entirety = "Entire couple, entirely survivorship."

Exam trap: Unequal contributions are a strong clue for tenants in common — joint tenants own equally.

1.8 Laws of succession and forced heirship

Many territories — especially civil-law ones in Europe, Japan, Latin America, Russia and many Islamic territories (which may follow Sharia) — follow succession rules / forced heirship:

  • Owners are not completely free to distribute their estates.
  • There are rules against disinheriting certain kin — mainly spouse, children, grandchildren, possibly grandparents and others.
  • Rules usually apply to the whole estate, but some assets may be excluded and distributed as the decedent wishes.
  • In most situations, laws of the decedent's domicile at death take precedence for distribution.
Territory example Textbook position
Spain Children typically entitled to two-thirds; some discretion over one-third. Spouse is not entitled to the whole estate — at best the remaining one-third
France Estate divided into a reserve (forced) portion and a disposable (elective) portion
Others Some allow a portion to the surviving spouse instead of a large share to children; most allow some portion by will

Ways around forced heirship (Chapter 1 list): trusts (where recognised), a foreign corporation owning the property, and in some territories holding property jointly and electing community property so it passes to the spouse.

Real estate follows its own territory: even a person living where there is testamentary freedom (e.g., England) must have a house in a forced-heirship territory (e.g., France) pass under French rules.

Brussels IV — EU regulation on succession (Regulation 650/2012), applicable from 17 August 2015 throughout the EU except Denmark, Ireland and the UK. It lets people with cross-border estates choose whether the law of their last domicile or their nationality applies. Courts may still be asked to decide; it is new, somewhat untested and not a panacea.

Variations: not all civil-law territories apply forced heirship (e.g., China, Mexico), and some common-law territories apply at least some of it (e.g., Cyprus, India, Jersey, Guernsey).

Hook: Forced heirship = "The law writes part of your will." France splits it into Reserved (law's share) and Disposable (your share).

Example: A Spanish-domiciled widower with two children leaves a €900,000 estate. Typically two-thirds (€600,000) must go to the children; he has some discretion over one-third (€300,000).

Exam trap: A UK resident who owns a villa in France cannot simply will the villa to a friend — French forced heirship governs French real estate.

1.9 Incapacity

Incapacity = an individual is unable to function normally for his or her own benefit; lacks capacity.

  • Can happen at any age. Here it refers to a legal disqualification — the person is judged, mainly for medical, emotional or psychological reasons, incapable of entering into or carrying out certain agreements or activities.
  • Examples: a person in a medical coma, or with cognitive impairment (dementia).
  • Unless prior arrangements were made, once incapacitated the person cannot express wishes about ongoing treatment, life-extending measures, etc. (Chapter 5 covers planning.)
1.10 Gross, probate and taxable estate
Estate concept Meaning (textbook) Typical contents
Gross estate Everything a person owns (including debts, per footnote) All assets
Probate estate Assets that pass by will or intestacy — must go through probate Solely owned assets without a beneficiary
Taxable estate Any amount subject to tax on transfer or distribution — may or may not be everything Depends on territory
  • Transfer to heirs may be wholly, partly or not at all taxable, depending on territory.
  • The three terms do not refer to the same assets: some of the gross estate passes through probate, some doesn't; some may be taxable, some not.
  • Clients care how the gross estate is distributed, want to limit taxes and probate, and want efficiency. Planners should learn what the client wants, then make it happen efficiently with minimal expense.

Hook: G-P-T = Got · Probated · Taxed. "Everything you Got, the part the court Probates, the part the government Taxes."

1.11 Gifts

Gifting = distributing property before death; a key part of many estate plans. A gift is made when the donor during his or her lifetime transfers ownership to another individual or entity.

A gift must be complete — the owner has relinquished all control.

Scenario (textbook) Completed gift? Why
Income-producing asset placed in a trust, income paid to someone else for a period, then asset reverts to the original owner No Owner directed the income and reserved the right to retake the asset
Grantor gifts property but keeps income for a period, after which the grantee owns it Not until ownership vests in the grantee with no strings attached Control retained during the period

Why give a gift?

  1. Provide a current benefit to family, others or organisations — and see them enjoy it.
  2. Reduce the eventual estate by the gift's value (a $500,000 gift reduces the estate by $500,000), probably cutting estate taxes.
  3. Remove future growth of the asset from the donor's estate.

But some territories tax gifts, especially large ones. Rules differ; a supposed gift can be disallowed on a technicality, and lookback periods can pull gifts back into the estate.

Textbook illustration (UK): A donor made a lifetime non-exempt gift of £200,000 five years ago and dies today. HMRC would likely add the gift back to the estate because death was within seven years; in some circumstances the lookback can stretch to 14 years. The gift then bears inheritance tax (IHT).

Hook: A gift is complete only when the donor can say "Gone, gone, gone" — gone legally, gone beneficially, gone forever.

Example: Ravi gives his son shares worth $500,000 that grow to $800,000 by Ravi's death. If the gift is complete and outside any lookback, Ravi's estate is reduced by $800,000 (the gift plus the $300,000 growth), not just $500,000.

2.1 The real goal of an estate plan

Learning outcomes: 2-1 distinguish between estate planning goals; 2-2 determine constraints to meeting them.

  • Clients almost always want to save taxes, and that is one goal — but rarely the primary one.
  • At its heart, an estate plan lets a person, at death, distribute assets to the people and organisations they choose, in the manner they want.
  • "The manner they want" usually means efficiently and with as little reduction of the estate as possible, so heirs get the largest inheritance — that is where minimising tax fits.
  • Saving taxes is a tool supporting the primary goal: distributing assets to heirs efficiently and effectively.
2.2 Discovering client goals

Asking "Tell me your estate planning goals" often gets a shrug — clients have ideas but can't articulate them. The planner must discover goals and the motivations behind them — what they say they want, what they really want, and why.

Key skill: be curious about the client. If a discussion is going nowhere, some unspoken factor is often influencing the client.

Textbook case (Marcee Yager — Joan and Dick): Both were in second marriages with children from earlier marriages. Joan had inherited a family trust and was wealthy; Dick had little left after his divorce. Dick first wanted everything in community property (he believed in the marriage partnership), then worried his kids would get nothing, then refused to pay life insurance premiums to fix that. He rejected every plan. Marcee stopped suggesting solutions and focused on his issues: "You are torn between two conflicting goals, and I need clarity about what is troubling you." After several meetings he admitted guilt and anger about his divorce, anxiety about money, estrangement from his children, and discomfort at being supported by a rich wife. The final plan addressed these feelings — taxes had no bearing on them.

Three legacy questions clients can ask themselves (Diliberto):

  1. What was the meaning of my life?
  2. Did I make a difference in the world?
  3. What is my legacy to the world?

Hook: Legacy questions = "Meaning, Difference, Legacy" → M-D-L ("My Dear Legacy").

Exam trap: When a client keeps rejecting technically sound plans, the textbook's answer is not "offer a better product" — it is to stop proposing and explore the underlying concern.

2.3 Common estate planning goals

Common objectives of married couples (also apply to unmarried partners and others) — Saret (2014):

Goal Meaning
Provide for loved ones Financial security when one dies or becomes incapacitated — among the most important goals
Minimise taxes More passes to heirs, less to the government — common, not primary
Protect assets passing to spouse / heirs Keep assets safe from creditors and unscrupulous people
Make it simple and inexpensive Easy for heirs to follow, without undue cost — but not at the expense of overall objectives
Keep things private Maintain privacy as far as possible
Have control over assets Keep maximum control
Plan for potential incapacity Arrangements for many possible needs if one becomes incapacitated
Manage assets Systems for good financial management when one or both can no longer manage

Added by the textbook: avoid disputes (family, business partners, third parties), provide for charities, provide liquidity to settle the estate, transfer business ownership to heirs.

Two technical questions to ask (the planner asks; the client answers):

  1. Do any heirs need protection, and from whom? (Elderly mentally incompetent person; a minor likely to overspend; creditors; even siblings and former spouses.)
  2. Who will act for the individual when he or she no longer can?

Few goals focus on reducing transfer taxes; three areas take precedence: family, children and grandchildren, philanthropy.

Hook: Eight goals = "Please Make People Sincerely Keep Control, Plan, Manage" → Provide, Minimise tax, Protect, Simple, Keep private, Control, Plan for incapacity, Manage.

2.4 Providing for loved ones

The most commonly expressed goal: financial security for loved ones at death or incapacity.

  • Parents often want important family assets to stay in the family across generations.
  • More immediately: give the family enough money to keep their lifestyle. Illiquid and unmarketable assets are far less useful than cash and liquidity.
  • Liquidity can be harder in some territories, especially forced heirship regimes. Life insurance is often used.
  • To give a surviving spouse adequate liquidity and cash flow: a valid will, perhaps a trust, and life insurance (or alternative) are likely needed — partly because under mandatory succession the spouse may have to share with the children.

Textbook example: Estate $1 million; wife (cannot work) and six children; succession law divides it seven ways. Wife gets about $143,000 (1,000,000 ÷ 7 = $142,857), and so does each child. But she must support herself and all six children from her share, while the children's shares stay untouched. Her money runs out fast; she might even have to leave the family home.

  • Many territories therefore let the surviving spouse receive a larger portion. Stating distribution goals clearly in a well-constructed will plus funding vehicles usually improves the chance that wishes are carried out.
  • Existing incapacity of survivors also needs planning: a spouse who can't care for himself / herself, children needing ongoing special care, parents needing extended care at home or in a facility. Funds and arrangements must be provided.

Hook: "Equal is not always fair." Equal split ≠ the spouse who carries the family's costs.

2.5 Children and grandchildren

Providing for children is a common goal: funds while they are dependent, plus assets they can use on reaching majority. This usually needs an administrator or trustee; a large estate needs thorough planning and clear instructions on how to distribute over time.

Three common needs for minor children:

# Need Why
1 Sufficient funds for living expenses (food, clothing, care; maybe higher education) Minors can't earn
2 A guardian or administrator to oversee the funds Minors cannot (and usually aren't allowed to) manage money
3 Someone to care for and guide the children to independence Family member, trusted friend or neighbour, or even an agency
  • Parents must choose and get agreement from those chosen. Without arrangements, the state / courts decide who cares for the children — seldom what parents would want. Address this early — shortly after birth is not too soon.
  • Most jurisdictions require a guardian or administrator for children's funds, usually under state supervision with regular reporting.
  • Where recognised, a trust can hold assets for children: terms say how income and assets are distributed and when, often allow whatever is needed for welfare, and say how remaining assets go once the children reach majority (or a later age).
  • Education funding may be a separate arrangement: prefunded tuition, life insurance proceeds directed to education (efficient and cost-effective), paid into a trust, directly to the institution, or to the administrator. Paying funds directly to children is seldom wise and may not be legal.
  • All of this can apply to grandchildren too.

Hook: Minor-child plan = "Money, Manager, Mentor."

2.6 Children of high net worth individuals (HNWI)

Legacy planning for HNWI children is more complex: heirs often aren't prepared for large sums, the family may live under scrutiny, and parents may not have prepared them.

Start early — teach the value of money and what money is not. Six money myths children may believe (Mellon & Christie, 2014):

Myth Myth
Money = Happiness Money = Freedom
Money = Love Money = Self-worth
Money = Power Money = Security

Truth: money is a tool — it can help achieve these things but doesn't produce them by itself. Children in HNW homes may have more reason to believe the myths, especially if their parents do.

Affluenza = mainly a false sense of entitlement (Furnham, 2014).

Textbook illustration (BBC, Dec 2015): Ethan Couch, at 16 in June 2013, drove drunk and speeding, crashed into a stationary car, killed four people and injured others. His lawyers argued his wealthy parents failed to instil responsibility — "affluenza". He pleaded guilty to four counts of intoxication manslaughter and two of intoxication assault; he and his mother were later detained in Puerto Vallarta, Mexico after he failed to report to his probation officer.

How HNW parents counter it — teach giving:

  • Donate clothing, assemble food baskets, help elderly / disabled neighbours (raking leaves, shopping, picking up trash), set aside part of an allowance for a charity the child chooses.
  • Service projects together; trips to help people in impoverished places; include children in family giving decisions; a donor-advised fund for the child's own money.
  • Why philanthropy? Attitude — money is not a right or entitlement; that perspective helps when a large inheritance arrives.
  • Also teach how money works — enlist a mentor or financial planner.
  • Practically, set up a trust, guardian or administrator to put parameters around use of the inheritance at first.

Hook: Six myths = "HL-PF-SS" → Happiness, Love, Power, Freedom, Self-worth, Security ("How Long People Fool Selves Silly").

2.7 Providing for organisations and others
  • Leaving a meaningful legacy is common among HNWI.
  • "Charity begins at home" — for most people, caring for loved ones comes first; but supporting a cause matters to many with means.
  • Rules vary by territory and type of organisation. Recognised / government-approved organisations make transfers easier.
  • Some territories make testamentary or inter vivos charitable gifts fully or partly tax-free; others don't; the tax-free amount may be limited.
  • Strict succession laws can make gifts to non-family beneficiaries nearly impossible.
  • Where allowed, trusts can hold assets for charities. Universities run endowment-type plans: small gifts go into general finances; large ones may fund scholarships, faculty salaries, research, libraries or buildings.
2.8 Small business owners and buy-sell agreements

Business owners worry about keeping the business going, letting the family benefit, and distributing ownership to achieve both. They often have most of their time, effort and money in the business, draw current income from it, count on it for retirement, and expect it to provide for the family at death.

Top estate-related concerns (UK study, Family Business United 2017):

# Concern
1 Continuing to develop and remain profitable
2 Management succession planning
3 Planning for later life
4 Engaging and developing the next generation
5 Ownership succession and developing responsible future owners
6 Identifying and maintaining family values
7 Extracting value from the business
8 Taxation

Most concerns are about business succession. Start planning early — earlier than the owner thinks.

Points to consider in a succession plan (SBDC, 2016): the successor (family, partner, other); succession type (partial / full); timeframe; key personnel changes and skill retention; restrictions; legal (buy-sell agreement, reference to a will); risk management; communication strategy; financial (retirement income, sale price, tax).

Buy-sell agreement (must be drafted by a licensed attorney) — three consistent elements:

  1. Who can buy the departing owner's share
  2. The circumstances allowing the sale (retirement, death, disability, leaving)
  3. The price to be paid

If the business can't be sold before death, the family should plan to suspend operations or hire a manager / firm — useful when the market is poor but expected to improve. A running business sells more easily and for more than a failing one.

With more than one owner:

  • Corporation — usually the business itself buys the outstanding shares.
  • Partnership — usually each remaining owner buys the decedent's share.

Common objectives after an owner's death: keep the business going (employees want jobs; remaining owners want income and growth in value); the family wants to recover the decedent's investment and replace the lost income stream. The family usually can't or won't run the business and wants money; remaining owners prefer the family stay out — a funded buy-sell satisfies both.

Feature Entity (redemption) purchase Cross-purchase (cross-option)
Who owns the policy The business, on each owner Each owner, on each other owner
Who pays premiums Business Each owner
Who receives proceeds Business Surviving owner(s)
Who buys the decedent's share Business buys from the estate Survivors buy from the estate
Agreement between Business and owners Owner A and Owner B

Funding options: life insurance (most common), sinking fund, instalment payments from the business to the family (delays income but guarantees provision), borrowing, or a third-party buyer. The goal is the same; only the funding differs.

Worked example (textbook Example 1): "In an entity purchase, the business pays the premiums" — correct (C). In a cross-purchase, owner A pays premiums on B, and the surviving owner receives proceeds.

Example (policy count — my addition): With n owners, an entity plan needs n policies; a cross-purchase needs n × (n − 1). Three partners: entity = 3 policies; cross-purchase = 3 × 2 = 6 policies.

Hook: Buy-sell elements = "Who, When, How much." Entity = Enterprise pays and receives. Cross = Colleagues pay and receive.

Exam trap: In a cross-purchase, the business is not the beneficiary — the surviving owner is.

3.1 Overview

Learning outcomes: 3-1 develop steps in the estate planning process; 3-2 determine estate value at death; 3-3 evaluate ways to reduce taxes and expenses at death.

  • The process varies by territory. At one extreme the state controls distribution (tax, succession laws, mandates); at the other, owners distribute freely. Most territories sit in between.
  • Some taxation is common even without a general transfer tax; most territories have intestacy and/or mandatory succession rules.
  • This chapter focuses on the monetary side: net worth, expenses and taxes, ways to reduce them, and liquidity.
3.2 Six steps in the estate planning process

Every client is unique, but most plans include these steps (Northwestern Mutual, 2014):

Step What it involves (textbook)
1. Create an inventory of what is owned and owed Account numbers, contact information, medical personnel, online logins (username, password, URL), all real and personal property, investments, anything of value; current debts (amounts, to whom, payments); contacts of financial and other advisors. Keep it in a secure but accessible place; give a summary to those who need it. Banks may demand a lot of proof of access rights, causing delays
2. Develop a contingency plan For incapacity or another unplanned event — how will expenses be paid and dependents provided for?
3. Provide for children and dependents Short-term liquidity and ongoing support; maybe funds to start a business, buy a home, pay university. Guardian and administrator for minors until majority or later; children from a prior marriage; special needs dependents
4. Protect assets Minimise expenses and taxes; keep assets from creditors and others without real claim; include all assets; transfer special assets (business, investment property) appropriately
5. Document wishes A current, valid will (often the first step); nominate beneficiaries on life insurance, annuities, retirement accounts; plan retitling of solely owned assets (vehicles, real estate); living will, medical POA, enduring / springing POA; establish any trust
6. Appoint fiduciaries / legal representatives Executor to settle the estate, trustees, guardians and administrators for children, personal representative or POA for incapacity. Representatives should be fiduciaries where the territory has a fiduciary standard, or otherwise legally authorised. The planner may be part of the team

Hook: Six steps = "I Can Provide Protection, Document, Appoint" → Inventory, Contingency, Provide for children, Protect, Document, Appoint (I-C-P-P-D-A).

3.3 Creating and reviewing a will

Dying without a will, or with an invalid one, is dying intestate; intestacy laws usually won't reflect the decedent's wishes. One of the first goals is a valid, current will.

  • Drafting a will is the lawyer's job. A planner who drafts without legal authority almost certainly breaches the code of ethics and law — usually a criminal offence. The planner can guide what the will should cover.

Four basic functions of a will:

# Function
1 Specify how the estate is to be distributed at death
2 Name an executor
3 Name a guardian / administrator for minor children
4 Revoke any previous wills

A simple will ("all property, after debts, to be distributed as follows…") seldom works except for basic estates — it doesn't handle unmarried partners, specific items to certain children, unequal shares, children from current and former marriages, succession mandates, tax-saving arrangements or protection of assets and heirs.

Property that passes outside a will: life insurance, retirement arrangements (annuities etc.), ownership titling (joint tenancy, tenancy by the entirety — note tenants in common does pass by will), accounts with named beneficiaries or postmortem provisions (e.g., tontine), and some mandatory real-property rules (property in one territory may not pass to an heir from another).

  • In many territories you cannot disinherit a spouse and/or children — law overrides the will; a safety net.
  • Probate property passes by will (or intestacy) and makes up the probate estate; non-probate property doesn't. Non-probate is easier (no lengthy, costly probate), but a will gives more control and catches forgotten or late-arriving property (a dividend, final pay, a car).
  • Residuary clause = catch-all: everything left after debts and specific gifts passes to a named person or organisation, so nothing is left for the court to decide.

Will review checklist (Parish, 2010) — review is needed when:

Area Triggers
Legal No lawyer review in past 12 months; execution requirements not met; drafted too long ago and laws changed; significant tax law changes; precatory language (wishes that aren't legally binding) a court may not enforce
Beneficiaries Add / delete beneficiaries; change amounts or type of property; change marital property or life income to spouse; change residuary clause; cancel debts by will
Family Change in marital status; change in health of client or family; birth or adoption of children / grandchildren
Assets Will doesn't address abatement or ademption (specific property no longer in the estate); significant change in estate value; assets appreciated greatly; potential liquidity problem; new or changed life insurance, pension or retirement benefits
Business Significant business change; business liquidity or continuity problems
Fiduciaries Change guardian, executor or trustee; add a successor; change apportionment of debts or taxes; change in a fiduciary's status (divorce, health, disability, relocation)
Ownership and place Change in form of ownership or deeds; change of jurisdiction of residency; property acquired in another jurisdiction; other reasons

Cross-border and expatriate clients: a will drafted in the home territory likely won't be fully valid elsewhere, especially where forced heirship is recognised in one territory and not the other. First step: have it reviewed by a competent lawyer in the territory of domicile. Clients with assets in several territories should have a valid will in each, with no conflicts between them.

Why a will matters — making wishes known:

  • Unmarried partner: intestacy rules mostly don't recognise the relationship. Even with a will, territorial rules may override; without one, the partner almost certainly gets nothing.
  • Without a will, gifts to charities, friends, universities won't happen (unless via a will substitute).
  • No valid will may mean frozen bank and investment accounts, cancelled visas, notification to the home territory, distribution to the extended family (spouse, parents, children, cousins), children from a previous marriage disinherited, and higher expenses.

Hook: Will = "D-E-G-R" → Distribute, Executor, Guardian, Revoke. Residuary clause = the "sweeper".

Exam trap: "Ademption" = the specifically willed property no longer exists in the estate (e.g., the car was sold). "Abatement" = gifts are reduced because the estate is too small.

3.4 Trusts

Not every territory recognises trusts — notably civil-law territories that haven't signed the Hague Convention (several European, Asian, South American and other territories).

Trust = a legal arrangement by which one person (or entity) legally owns property of another person for the benefit of someone else.

Party Role
Grantor / settlor Creates and supplies the trust assets
Trustee Holds legal ownership; administers the corpus for the beneficiary
Beneficiary Holds beneficial ownership
Remainderman Receives the trust assets after the trust terminates

Textbook illustration (Mrs B and Timmy): Mrs B's will leaves money to trustee John Doe, who may pay "so much, or none" of the income, at his discretion, for Timmy's health, education, maintenance and support, and must hand Timmy the whole balance at age 25. Timmy has a present interest (income) and a future interest (corpus).

Trust type Meaning
Testamentary Created under the will; doesn't exist until the grantor dies; funded from the estate
Living (inter vivos) Created while the grantor is alive; can be funded immediately; may be revocable or irrevocable
Revocable Can be changed or terminated
Irrevocable Cannot be modified or terminated without the beneficiary's express written agreement — use extreme care
Grantor trust Grantor keeps some control; may be trustee and even beneficiary
Non-grantor trust Grantor gives up all right, title and interest; trust owns the assets; taxed to the trust

Uses of trusts (textbook):

  • A trustee for assets left to a minor, or to an adult (child, parent, sibling, elderly person) who can't manage assets; disabled beneficiaries — some territories allow special needs trusts with tax or other incentives.
  • Beneficiaries with drug, alcohol or gambling problems — limits access by the beneficiary and creditors.
  • In a second marriage, ensure children from the first marriage are not disinherited.
  • A living trust lets the grantor see the benefits being used; may reduce current and future tax; often revocable. Full tax savings may require an irrevocable trust.

Pourover will: a will provision sending all assets (or those not otherwise distributed) into a trust. Assets still pass through probate because they go via the will.

Revocable living trust for incapacity: the grantor is trustee while alive; on incapacity a named successor trustee takes over. The trust document can define when the grantor is considered incapacitated.

Worked example (textbook Example 1 — Mary and Susan, 12): Mary wants financial guardianship for Susan if she dies, but is hesitant to set up structures during life. Best answer C: a testamentary trust in her will with Susan as beneficiary and Wendy (advisor) and Rhonda (accountant) as joint trustees. Why not A: Susan as her own trustee — a minor controlling assets. Not B: leaving it outright with written wishes gives no guardianship. Not D: an inter vivos trust works but must be funded now, which Mary doesn't want.

Hook: Testamentary = Tomorrow (after death). Living = Live now. Pourover still goes through probate — "pour through the court funnel first".

Exam trap: A minor beneficiary should not be the trustee of her own trust.

3.5 Estate expenses at death

At death, the estate must be administered — calculating expenses, taxes and net worth. Five broad categories of final expenses (Parish, 2010):

Category Textbook points
Administrative Every estate has them; reduced by planning, never zero — probate court costs, death certificates, appraisers, lawyers, accountants. Rise with cross-border complexity and estate size
Death taxes Some territories none; others high — a few top rates 50% or more, one reaching 80% (EY, 2017). Usually progressive. Some tax only real-estate transfers (stamp / transfer tax); states may tax when the territory doesn't; sometimes the heir is taxed, sometimes gifts, or both. Another territory may tax assets located there (e.g., real estate); double-tax treaties usually help; usually the territory of final domicile is primary
Debts and claims Wills usually say debts and legitimate claims are paid before distribution. Fraudulent claims (esp. rich or famous estates) delay settlement and add cost. Life insurance to a spouse or administrative beneficiary gives liquidity, usually free of creditor claims and probate delay
Cash needs Survivors' living costs continue; funeral; debts; medical bills after a long illness; fees for appraisers, lawyers, accountants who won't wait. Forced asset sales bring lower prices (esp. a sole proprietor's or partnership business — another reason for a buy-sell; and real estate). Assets can be sold or gifted before death for cash, but assets passing at death sometimes get better tax status than cash
Cash bequests Less urgent but raise liquidity needs; consider limiting or eliminating cash (vs property) bequests

Hook: Five expenses = "A Dead Duck Can't Cash" → Administrative, Death taxes, Debts & claims, Cash needs, Cash bequests.

3.6 Determining estate value: gross, probate and taxable
Estate What it includes (textbook)
Gross estate Everything owned: current assets, bank and investment accounts, at least some portion of jointly held assets, amounts owed but unpaid, ongoing royalties, real estate rents (commercial property may be valued by gross rent multiplier or NOI capitalisation), retirement accounts and income streams, life insurance payable to the estate, policies where the decedent kept incidents of ownership (even if paid to a named beneficiary), revocable living trust assets, trust assets where the decedent was beneficiary, real property, business interests
Probate estate Only assets passing by will or intestacy. Excludes will substitutes (life insurance to a named beneficiary), irrevocable trust assets, certain ownership titles and assets passing by law. Often much smaller than gross
Taxable estate Varies by territory: some assets taxed at higher rates (notably real estate), some excluded by law or by how they are titled; cross-border estates may need separate accounting per territory

Joint assets in the gross estate:

Joint holding Amount included
Jointly owned with spouse May be half the current market value
Other joint ownership The decedent's percentage contribution (60% contributed → 60% included)

Incidents of ownership (footnote) = owning or controlling an asset, even if placed in trust or given as a gift. For life policies: the right to change beneficiaries, take policy loans or otherwise affect the policy.

Gross estate − amounts carved out by law (e.g., to spouse) − debts and expenses = Net estate
Net estate − non-included assets − exemptions = Taxable estate

Debts and expenses include funeral costs, excess medical bills, administration costs (including appraisals), outstanding mortgage and other debts. Some territories exempt assets going to a spouse or children, or tax them at a different rate.

Jurisdiction question: who taxes an ongoing income stream? Often the territory where it is generated — possibly in addition to the territory of last domicile.

Worked example (my numbers): Decedent owns: house in sole name $600,000; bank account (sole, no nominee) $50,000; flat held as joint tenants with his spouse, worth $400,000; life policy $300,000 to a named daughter, but he kept the right to change the beneficiary; irrevocable trust he funded years ago $200,000. Debts and expenses $70,000. Assume a $100,000 legislative spousal carve-out and a $250,000 exemption. Gross estate = 600,000 + 50,000 + (½ × 400,000 = 200,000) + 300,000 (incidents of ownership) = $1,150,000 (irrevocable trust excluded). Probate estate = house + bank = $650,000 (joint flat passes by survivorship; policy has a named beneficiary). Net estate = 1,150,000 − 100,000 − 70,000 = $980,000. Taxable estate = 980,000 − 250,000 = $730,000.

Exam trap: A life policy paid to a named beneficiary is outside the probate estate but can still be in the gross estate if the decedent kept incidents of ownership.

3.7 Ways to reduce taxes and expenses at death

Expenses reduce the gross (and taxable) estate, but the aim is that assets pass as wished, not that money goes on unnecessary costs. Three areas raise administrative expenses:

Area Textbook points How to reduce
Probate Court reviews will validity, appoints personal representative (or administrator if no valid will), mandates paying taxes and liabilities. Costs: court, lawyers, research, notifying heirs, meetings. The more ambiguous the documents, the more it costs. Not needed for will substitutes or assets passing by law. (Common-law territories may call it a grant of representation) A well-written, current, valid will naming all beneficiaries and assets / percentages, compliant with succession laws; a valid will for each territory
Appraisal and valuation Needed for gross and taxable estate and inheritance taxes; court-appointed or approved professional; complex property (collectibles, commercial real estate) needs specialists, sometimes more than one; check qualification and licence Variations rectified by more than two appraisals — costs more but usually worth it
Litigation Unhappy relatives (missed assets, disinherited children from a former marriage, adopted children, people promised something) go to court. Intellectual property royalties are especially hard (several creators, licences in many places). Guardianship disputes with children of different parents. Famous / wealthy estates can drag on for years; the planner may need his own lawyer Clarity in the will helps but won't eliminate all disputes

Debt, tax and other financial settlement:

  • Not all debts must be settled at death. A jointly held mortgage can continue with the surviving debtor. Credit debts held solely by the decedent may not be payable by survivors (but the estate may be liable) — the PR or court should make this clear. Settlements may be negotiated to reduce amounts or extend terms.
  • Taxes due are what they are, but valuation may sometimes be fixed at a date after death when it's more favourable — in a falling market a later date lowers the taxable estate without lowering the long-term value of the inherited portfolio. Check double-tax treaties, credits and exclusions.
  • Remove assets before death through completed gifts — outright or via trust; charities may bring special exemptions. Gifts must be complete (legal and beneficial ownership released).
  • Charitable trust with income: in some territories the grantor gives an asset to a charitable trust, the charity eventually owns it, and the grantor receives income for a period — the gift is complete and out of the estate.
  • House gifted with right to live: a parent gifts a house to a child but keeps the right to live there for a set period; then full ownership passes.

Hook: Admin cost drivers = "P-A-L" → Probate, Appraisal, Litigation. The cure for all three = a clear, current will.

4.2 Lifetime transfers and gift taxation

Why give during life? To remove assets from the estate — and, often equally, to see the recipient use the gift: a scholarship or research chair at an alma mater, a building on campus; help a family member buy a home, start a business, pay debt, start a portfolio, support children, pay medical bills; fund a charity's projects.

Who pays tax on a gift (where no money is received in return): usually the donor, occasionally the donee.

Gift category (textbook) Treatment
Exempt e.g., gifts to a recognised charity in some territories; gifts to a spouse / civil partner may be fully or partly exempt; very small gifts
Potentially exempt Exempt if the donor survives a set period
Chargeable lifetime transfer (CLT) Fully taxable
  • Lookback period: gifts within e.g. 3 years (or 7, or other) of death may be recalled into the estate valuation.
  • Usually a certain amount is exempt regardless of recipient.

Unified gift and estate / inheritance tax — one tax on the estate at death and certain lifetime gifts:

  • Some value is exempt. Textbook example: a $5 million estate reduced by a $3 million exemption → $2 million taxable.
  • In a unified system, lifetime gifts use up the exemption, so large lifetime gifting can leave no exemption at death and the whole taxable estate is taxed.
  • Many territories have abolished gift and estate taxes, but some levy a stamp or income tax instead.

Steps to value a taxable gift:

  1. Is the gift taxable at all? (Charity, immediate family / spouse, very small gifts may be excluded.)
  2. Apply any reduction in value (appraisal, depreciation, other).
  3. Apply exclusion / exemption amounts.
  4. Report taxable gifts on the right forms and track them so exemptions apply correctly.

Taxes may apply only within a territory or worldwide; citizens and non-citizens may face different rates and exemptions.

Worked example (unified system — my numbers): Exemption $3 million. Mr Rao makes taxable lifetime gifts of $1.2 million, which use part of the exemption. At death his estate is $4 million. Remaining exemption = 3.0 − 1.2 = $1.8 million. Taxable estate at death = 4.0 − 1.8 = $2.2 million (versus $1.0 million if he had made no lifetime gifts). The total taxed base is the same $5.2m − $3m; lifetime gifts don't create a second exemption.

Hook: Unified tax = "One bucket of exemption — fill it in life, and there's less left at death."

Exam trap: A gift inside the lookback window is treated as if it never left the estate for tax purposes.

4.3 Small business owners — transferring during life

Selling a sole proprietorship or a two-partner business after death often means a deep discount — buyers know the family must sell for liquidity (to pay inheritance tax or meet cash needs). A funded buy-sell helps, but a sole owner usually has no one to take the buy side.

Options during life:

Option Textbook points
Sell as a going concern or liquidate before death Transfer for value — price = appraised fair market value
Gift the business (usually to family) Subject to normal gifting rules and tax
Discounted sale to family The difference between discounted price and FMV is likely deemed a gift; same for advantageous terms (cheap loan, favourable instalments)
Outright sale to a third party Better if the family member isn't willing and capable of running it

Restructure to transfer gradually: reorganise as a partnership or a closely held corporation issuing stock with different classes:

Arrangement Owner keeps Family member gets Effect
Option 1 Common stock (voting) Preferred stock (normally no vote) Owner keeps control
Option 2 Preferred stock — guaranteed income (preferred dividends paid before common) A non-majority share of common stock Starts transfer, reduces estate exposure, owner still controls
  • Gift all at once or over years — useful if there is an annual gift exclusion; gifts can be sized to trigger no tax and not reduce exemptions.
  • Gift tax is based on pre-gift FMV minus the value the donor keeps. Private business value can be speculative — get a professional appraisal. For listed shares: price per share × shares outstanding.
  • Often the donee carries over the donor's basis.
  • Gradual gifting builds the donee's interest and ability to manage, giving the donor confidence the business will continue.

Example (discounted sale — my numbers): A business with appraised FMV of $1,000,000 is sold to a daughter for $600,000. Deemed gift = 1,000,000 − 600,000 = $400,000, subject to the territory's gift rules.

Hook: Common = Command (votes). Preferred = Paycheque first (dividends first).

4.4 Transfers at death and the personal representative

A dead person can't keep owning assets — they must go to someone. Best if the decedent addressed distribution in advance; worst case, the state decides (intestacy rules vary by territory and even region).

Personal representative (PR) — also executor / executrix or administrator. Needed with or without a will.

  • A good will names the PR, whom the court approves. If intestate, the court appoints a PR / administrator.
  • If the named person declines, the court seeks the next most competent person — perhaps the spouse or another family member; failing that, a local lawyer or bank.
  • The PR gets legal authority to act for the estate.

PR's duties (textbook order):

# Duty
1 Find the assets — notify banks and institutions of the death; become the legal point of contact
2 Collect money in accounts titled solely in the decedent's name
3 Protect property (house, use assets, cars) from theft or vandalism. Survivorship-titled property shifts to co-owners, who then protect and insure it
4 Collect amounts due — rents, royalties, debt repayments; notify income sources (social security, pensions, deferred pay); collect life insurance payable to the estate (not to named beneficiaries)
5 Act in the estate's interest; likely report to the court
6 Pay debts as agreed; notify creditors with instructions for filing claims
7 Determine and pay taxes — includes discovering prior gifts that affect exemptions; get valuations; an accountant usually prepares final forms
8 Administer assets until distributed — insurance, portfolios, real estate, business operation or sale
9 Distribute per the will or statutes (deeds, titles, assignments, cheques for cash bequests, account transfers), including forced heirship and spousal rights
10 Take part in any lawsuits

Assets passing by law or will substitute make the PR's job easier. But if a will tries to disinherit a spouse or children, the law seldom allows it and the court rules on final distribution.

Hook: PR duties = "Find, Collect, Protect, Pay, Distribute" → "Five Careful People Pay Dues."

4.5 The probate process — positives and negatives

Probate = the orderly process of estate distribution under a valid will or statute (e.g., intestacy), handled by a probate court (the name varies).

Positives Negatives
Fair and orderly — the court provides boundaries and structure Expensive — court costs, legal fees, assessors, accountants, PR fees, all paid before distribution
Disputes heard by a disinterested, unbiased third party Takes time — valuing assets and identifying heirs; delays frustrate heirs
Structured by laws and rules — keeps things moving efficiently Public — probate records are generally public; a problem for the famous and very wealthy
Gives heirs valid titles and change-of-ownership documents
Gives creditors a venue for valid claims; taxes get paid

Worked example (textbook Example 1): Which is least likely a positive of probate? C — "Probate is not expensive." Probate can be expensive; that is a negative.

Hook: Probate's three negatives = "Costly, Slow, Public" → C-S-P ("Can't Stay Private").

4.6 High net worth individuals

Estate planning for HNWI is not fundamentally different, but:

  • They want to minimise taxes, avoid probate and protect chosen heirs' rights; where estate taxes apply, their burden is higher than average, so they use every exemption and deduction.
  • Privacy: probate can make net worth and distributions public, which can be exploited — so HNWI more often use trusts (inter vivos and testamentary) and lifetime gifts.
  • Predators: fake creditors, "high-return, high-security" schemes; grieving heirs are temporarily vulnerable, and HNWI heirs are lucrative targets.
  • Protection tools: assets flow into a trust with a reliable, often professional trustee; foreign (offshore) corporations or domestic corporate entities give security and privacy — the heirs don't own the assets, the entity does, while documents ensure their access. Exact structures depend on what each territory recognises.
4.7 Forced heirship, community property and usufruct

A will seldom overrides territorial succession statutes. In some cases a will overrides some of them, rarely all. With no will, intestate succession follows forced heirship where it applies.

Feature Textbook position
Meaning Decedent not free to distribute as desired; strict rules on who inherits and in what percentages
Where Occasionally in common-law territories; often in civil-law ones — many Arab (Islamic) states, Europe, Japan, Latin America, Russia (CFA Institute, 2017)
Children Almost always provided for (protected heirs)
Spouse Rules vary; providing for children (and maybe grandchildren) may effectively disinherit the spouse or leave a nominal share — not in all regimes, but often enough to be a concern
Mandatory heirs May extend to parents, grandparents, siblings, cousins and other relatives
Structure Some regimes split the estate into a hereditary reserve (forced) and a disposable / discretionary / free portion
Order Often the first and largest share to the first-born (often male), then next in line; lower categories may get nothing (Skandia, 2008)
One benefit Lower costs, especially from legal challenges

Three main ways to circumvent forced heirship:

# Method Caveat
1 Lifetime (inter vivos) transfers A period (e.g., 10 years) must pass before death or the gift may be challenged and recalled
2 Trust (waqf in Islam) Not all territories recognise trusts; some specifically deny trusts the power to bypass heirship
3 Foreign (offshore) company owning the property Property in a foreign company is likely outside the territory's heirship rules

Spouse protection inside forced heirship:

  • Many systems give a significant part of the marital estate to the surviving spouse — often 50%, or at least the community property portion — though it may be reduced to provide for children.
  • Community property = marital assets considered jointly and severally owned by spouses; its purpose is broadly agreed — security for the surviving spouse (especially the wife) (Dué, 1964).

Usufruct (more common in civil-law than common-law systems):

  • The right to use another person's property without changing its character — land, buildings, movables; sometimes even money and consumable assets (the user is the usufructuary).
  • Not limited to spouses, but often used for them: live in the family home, use community assets.
  • Usually ends at the earlier of remarriage or death. Less beneficial than outright ownership, but gives security.

Hook: Escape forced heirship = "Give early, Trust it, or Offshore it" → G-T-O.

Hook: Usufruct = "Use the fruit, not the tree." You enjoy the house; you can't sell it.

Example: Under a regime where children own the family home but the widow holds a usufruct, Lakshmi can live in the house for life. If she remarries, her usufruct ends and the children take full possession.

5.1 What incapacity means and why people avoid planning

Learning outcomes: 5-1 describe incapacity; 5-2 analyse plans to address incapacity.

Incapacity = lack of capacity — a physical or mental disability that impedes the ability to think and act as desired; it can make a person legally ineligible to handle his or her own affairs, most often through lack of mental fitness (can't process information or understand consequences).

Without prior arrangements, once judged incompetent:

  • A guardian or administrator is often chosen — maybe not to the person's liking.
  • The state decides using predetermined guidelines; loss of personal freedom; perhaps a caregiver unknown to the family.
  • The person may lose the right to represent himself legally.
  • Life as known changes, perhaps permanently. Pre-planning prevents much of this.

Why people don't plan: they never think it could happen to them, and it's unpleasant to imagine losing control. Planners add value by educating clients — directly or about a dependent / family member. It's a difficult but necessary conversation, especially as populations age.

Footnote: the chapter focuses on mental incapacity (more relevant to estate planning) and on the elderly, though much applies to minors and other adults.

6.2 Planning for the surviving spouse
  • Spousal provision may or may not be written into law — don't leave it to chance. Most territories won't allow disinheriting a spouse anyway.
  • Don't plan around possible remarriage; plan for what the survivor (either gender) needs as a single person — caring for children and/or parents, deciding on a family business, health care, income, support network, a transition period.

Grief diminishes mental clarity; even getting through the day is hard. Emotions listed (NEFE, 2017): angry, annoyed, anxious, bitter, crushed, depressed, despairing, doubtful, fearful, guilty, helpless, hopeless, nervous, outraged, panicked, perplexed, rejected, scared, shocked, terrified.

6.3 Unmarried partners and ex-spouses

Unmarried partner

  • Most spousal considerations apply, but an unmarried partner may lack a spouse's rights and support network — needs at least as much, if not more, clarity and documentation.
  • Name the partner in the will, on life insurance, retirement income plans etc. Some territories don't recognise unmarried partnerships, so succession rules may invalidate even a will designation.
  • Spousal tax-free transfers usually don't extend to partners.

Textbook example (Maria): Her partner promised she'd be cared for under his will. But her territory has strict forced heirship and doesn't recognise unmarried partners. The probate court favours his children from a prior marriage, who divide the whole $2 million estate — Maria gets nothing. A trust, a life policy naming her, or lifetime gifts would have protected her.

Remedies for unmarried couples:

Tool Why

Ex-spouse

  • Many part badly; some remain friendly, share children / grandchildren, or laws require provision.
  • Law usually ended legal and financial arrangements at divorce, so any intended provision must be specifically documented — starting with an updated will.
  • Children of the former marriage are a likely focus: they may fall outside forced heirship and risk disinheritance. Both parents should name them by name (not just by class) in their wills, care for children of any current marriage too, consider a trust, and set proper beneficiary designations including an administrator / guardian.
  • Divorce decrees may require an irrevocable beneficiary designation for the children. Problem: when children reach majority, an irrevocable designation naming the ex-spouse as guardian / administrator may no longer suit — but it can't be changed without the ex-spouse's consent (and sometimes the court's). The planner can't intervene but should be aware.
  • Another option: lifetime gifts to children or grandchildren.

Hook: Partner protection kit = "Will, Wrapper, Wallet, Word" → will, trust wrapper, insurance wallet, POA word.

6.4 Lifetime (inter vivos) gifts and tax basis

Gifts can benefit spouses, partners, parents, friends, business associates, charities and more; options are almost limitless; above a certain value they may be taxable, but can still be given.

Aspect Textbook points
Simplest method Outright — shift beneficial and legal ownership; within legislative limits, unlikely to be taxable
Biggest downside Loss of control — a true gift requires giving up control (sometimes an interest such as income for a period may be kept). Decide before giving
Greatest advantage Satisfaction of seeing the recipient enjoy it — a charity using it, a grandchild at university, children running the family business, help with a new baby
Tax benefits Reduces the estate (taxes and fees); more private than testamentary transfers (probate is public); removes future growth of appreciating assets — the donee deals with the gain; a gift tax exemption may cut current tax (in a unified system this may raise tax at death)

The basis dilemma:

Tax basis is subtracted from value to find the potentially taxable gain. Textbook example: a $100,000 gift with a $25,000 basis has potential taxable gain of $75,000.

Option Basis for recipient Tax on later sale at $100,000
Carryover basis (lifetime gift) Donor's $25,000 Gain $75,000
Step-up in basis (testamentary transfer, in some territories) Value at transfer, $100,000 Gain nil

The client decides, with the planner or accountant evaluating both options.

Worked example (my numbers): Shares bought for $40,000, now worth $160,000, assume a 20% gains tax on sale. Gift now (carryover): gain on immediate sale = 160,000 − 40,000 = 120,000 → tax $24,000. Inherit at death worth $160,000 (step-up): basis 160,000 → gain nil → tax $0 on immediate sale. Trade-off: gifting removes future growth from the estate; waiting gets the step-up.

Hook: Gift = Grandpa's basis; Grave = Great new basis (step-up).

Exam trap: Step-up applies to testamentary transfers in some territories, not to lifetime gifts.

6.5 Special needs, children and grandchildren

Special needs

  • A dependent child or parent with mental, emotional or physical special needs requires specific care and extra funding; funding may be treated as a gift (taxable or exempt).
  • Special needs trust (or equivalent): holds cash or assets to pay for care, housing and other costs, and spending money. Placing assets in the trust may let the beneficiary keep government aid that direct gifts might disqualify. A trustee oversees payments (caregivers, medical, housing, education). Set it up carefully to get all tax and other benefits and avoid provisions that defeat its purpose. Parents can benefit too.

Children and grandchildren

  • Former or current marriage, adopted or stepchildren — parents want to provide, through lifetime gifts, testamentary transfers or both.
  • Under forced heirship, the state may require specific shares, especially for children of the current marriage; lifetime gifts can act as an inheritance substitute for children of a prior relationship.
  • Parents may want unequal shares — e.g., favour an impoverished child over a wealthy one; lifetime gifts help.

Exceptionally gifted children

  • Very high intelligence, prodigious music or maths ability, etc. — may be highly gifted in one area and less developed in others (school, social, home).
  • Can be underserved without special support. Consider a fund or trust for extra education and support (special institutions cost more), and a specially trained guardian / mentor in instructions for after the parents' death.

Grandchildren and generation skipping

  • Several territories levy an additional tax when assets skip a generation (grandparent → grandchild).
  • Skip person / direct skip = beneficiary more than one generation away from the decedent.
  • If no exemption applies, a generation-skipping trust may reduce or remove the tax: it divides title from usufruct — usufruct to the child, title held in trust for the grandchild; when the child dies, usufruct merges with title for the grandchild (EY, 2017).

Hook: Generation-skipping trust = "Child eats the fruit, grandchild gets the tree."

6.6 Intrafamily transfers and loans

An intrafamily transfer = a gift or transfer from a parent, grandparent or close relative within the family. Gift / inheritance rules still apply (taxes, re-registration, retained interests); special exemptions may apply for spouses and children.

Intrafamily loan — used when the parent wants to move money to family without it being classed as a gift (gifts may be taxable and use up exclusions).

Requirements to be a real loan: a legally recognised agreement, a stated interest rate (may be 0% in some territories) and a repayment period with minimum payments — basically the same as a commercial loan.

Feature Intrafamily loan Commercial loan
Interest Can be much lower (government may set a floor) Market rate
Repayment period Can be longer Standard
Minimum payments Can be lower Standard
Where repayments go Stay in the family — income for the parent To the lender

Benefits:

  • Keeps a business, house, farm or real property in the family.
  • Freezes value: a business transferred at $1 million that grows to $10 million stays at $1 million for the parent's estate; the child deals with the growth.

Cautions:

  • Valuation must be realistic: a $1 million business can't be "loaned" at $100,000 — the $900,000 difference is a gift.
  • Biggest pitfall: the loan becomes a gift. If the parent forgets it, or the child doesn't repay and the parent doesn't enforce, it is treated as a gift; the textbook adds that a forgotten loan remains part of the parent's estate and can be taxable.
  • Small (de minimis) loans may not register; large ones (home, business) attract notice — follow normal loan requirements and the schedule; get professional guidance.
  • Divorce of a married child may hand the property to the ex-spouse — add protective legal documentation at the start.

Textbook example (Phillip and Faye's bakery): After 40 years they want to hand the bakery to their three children, who can't pay the price. Their planner suggests an intrafamily loan: a lawyer drafts the transfer, a nominal down payment, interest at the low end of current rates, repaid monthly over 10 years partly from bakery profits. Payments plus some ongoing revenue give the parents income; they help during the transition; the bakery stays in the family.

Worked example (textbook Example 1): Which statement about intrafamily loans is most likely true? D — a loan needs a legally recognised agreement, a stated rate (possibly 0%) and a repayment period with minimum payments. A is wrong (it's gifts that may be taxable), B is wrong (a loan can become a gift), C is a rule, not an advantage.

Hook: Real loan = "Paper, Percent, Payments."

6.7 Disclaiming an inheritance

Disclaiming (renouncing) = a formal, legal rejection of inherited property.

Reason Textbook explanation
Associated debt Inheriting an asset often means inheriting its debt; the debt can outweigh the value. Debts can often be collected across borders (a South Korean debt payable by an heir in Canada)
Extreme inconvenience Mostly cross-border, but also within a territory — the business or farm is too far away to manage, or the heir's job and life don't allow it
  • There is usually a limited time to disclaim (e.g., three months) and specific legal steps — act quickly.
  • Alternative: request an inventory and official liquidation; debts are paid from proceeds and any excess goes to heirs.
  • Tax advantage: disclaiming usually beats accepting then giving away — accepting may be one taxable event, and later gifting or selling another; disclaiming lets it pass directly to the next heir.
  • Warning: relatives living near the asset may urge a distant heir to disclaim — perhaps innocently, perhaps to grab the asset themselves. Before disclaiming (especially cross-border), investigate the asset and its debts (Hayes, 2017).

Textbook example (Terri's polluted land): Terri inherits land she hopes to develop. An environmental report shows mining chemicals polluting the water and soil; clean-up costs at least $10 million, possibly more. With development costs, it's uneconomic, so on legal advice she disclaims.

Hook: Disclaim when it's a "Debt or a Drag."
