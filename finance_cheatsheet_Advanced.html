📊 Examples
Time Value of Money
Present Value (PV)
EXCEL
What a future cash flow is worth today
Math
PV = FV / (1 + r)ⁿ
Excel
=PV(rate, nper, pmt, [fv], [type])
💡 Result is negative in Excel (outflow logic). Use –PV(...) or enter pmt as negative to get a readable positive number.
Future Value (FV)
EXCEL
What today's money will grow to over time
Math
FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ
Excel
=FV(rate, nper, pmt, [pv], [type])
💡 FV of lump sum: set pmt=0. FV of recurring SIP: set pv=0.
PMT (Periodic Payment / EMI)
EXCEL
Monthly loan EMI or SIP amount
Math
PMT = PV × [r(1+r)ⁿ] / [(1+r)ⁿ – 1]
Excel (monthly)
=PMT(rate/12, nper*12, pv)
ALWAYS divide rate by 12 and multiply nper by 12 for monthly loans. Use /4 and *4 for quarterly. Forgetting this gives completely wrong EMI.
RATE
EXCEL
Interest rate per period
Excel
=RATE(nper, pmt, pv, [fv], [type])
RATE gives periodic rate. Multiply ×12 if monthly inputs were used to get annual rate. Also used to compute CAGR — see Growth tab.
NPER
EXCEL
Number of periods to reach a financial goal
Excel
=NPER(rate, pmt, pv, [fv], [type])
💡 If inputs are monthly, result is in months. Divide by 12 to get years.
PVIF / PVIFA
MATH
Discount factor for single cash flow or annuity series
PVIF (single)
PVIF = 1 / (1 + r)ⁿ
PVIFA (annuity)
PVIFA = [1 – (1+r)⁻ⁿ] / r
💡 No separate Excel function. Use PV() instead. PVIF tables are used in manual exam calculations.
Annuity — PV & FV
EXCEL
Series of equal periodic payments
Ordinary Annuity (payments at end)
PVA = PMT × [1 – (1+r)⁻ⁿ] / r
Annuity Due (payments at start)
PVA_due = PVA × (1 + r)
Perpetuity (infinite payments)
PV = PMT / r
Excel
=PV(rate, nper, pmt, 0, type) type: 0 = ordinary, 1 = annuity due
Effective Annual Rate (EAR)
MATH
True annual rate accounting for compounding frequency
Math
EAR = (1 + r/n)ⁿ – 1
💡 A 12% rate compounded monthly → EAR = 12.68%. Always compare EAR when evaluating FDs with different compounding schedules.
PPMT (Principal Payment)
EXCEL
Principal portion of a specific EMI in a given period
Excel
=PPMT(rate, per, nper, pv, [fv], [type])
Example — Month 1 of ₹10L loan at 10% for 10 years
=PPMT(10%/12, 1, 120, 1000000)
💡 PPMT is lowest in early EMIs and grows over time. Opposite pattern to IPMT. Together: IPMT + PPMT = Total EMI (PMT) for any given period.
IPMT (Interest Payment)
EXCEL
Interest portion of a specific EMI in a given period
Excel
=IPMT(rate, per, nper, pv, [fv], [type])
Example — Month 1 of ₹10L loan at 10% for 10 years
=IPMT(10%/12, 1, 120, 1000000)
ALWAYS use rate/12 and nper×12 for monthly. "per" is the period number you want — e.g. per=1 for month 1, per=24 for month 24.
💡 IPMT is highest in early EMIs and shrinks over time as principal reduces. In month 1 of above loan → interest = ₹8,333. By month 100 → much lower.
Cash Flow Analysis
NPV
EXCEL
Net Present Value — sum of discounted future cash flows minus initial outlay
Math
NPV = Σ [CFt / (1+r)ᵗ] – Initial Investment
Excel
=NPV(rate, cf1:cfn) + initial_investment
Excel NPV() does NOT include Year 0. Always add the initial investment (as a negative) manually outside the function: =NPV(10%,B2:B6)+B1
💡 NPV > 0 → accept project. NPV = 0 → breakeven exactly at hurdle rate.
XNPV
EXCEL
NPV with actual/irregular dates (not periodic)
Excel
=XNPV(rate, values, dates)
💡 Preferred over NPV for real-world analysis. Use for LIC policy valuation, chit fund analysis, or any investment with irregular cash flow dates.
IRR
EXCEL
Rate that makes NPV = 0; internal project return
Math
Solve for r: 0 = Σ [CFt / (1+r)ᵗ]
Excel
=IRR(values, [guess])
IRR assumes reinvestment at the IRR rate — unrealistic for high-return projects. Multiple sign changes in CFs → multiple IRRs → use MIRR instead.
💡 Include Year 0 outflow as negative in the values array.
XIRR
EXCEL
IRR with actual dates — best for SIPs and irregular investments
Excel
=XIRR(values, dates, [guess])
💡 Gold standard for calculating mutual fund SIP returns, LIC policy return, or chit fund return — wherever cash flows happen on specific real dates.
MIRR
EXCEL
Modified IRR — uses separate reinvestment and financing rates
Excel
=MIRR(values, finance_rate, reinvest_rate)
💡 finance_rate = cost of borrowing (loan rate). reinvest_rate = where you park positive CFs (e.g., FD at 7%). If self-funded, use opportunity cost as finance_rate.
Payback Period (PBP)
MATH
Time to recover initial investment from raw cash flows
Math
PBP = Full years before recovery + (Remaining amount / CF of recovery year)
💡 Simple to calculate but ignores time value of money. Use DPBP for accuracy. No Excel function — calculate cumulative CF manually.
Discounted PBP (DPBP)
MATH
Payback period using discounted (PV) cash flows
Math
Step 1: Discount each CF → PV = CF/(1+r)ᵗ Step 2: Find cumulative PV until recovered
💡 Always longer than PBP. Accounts for TVM — more realistic for project evaluation. No dedicated Excel function.
Growth & Returns
CAGR
EXCEL
Compound Annual Growth Rate — smoothed annual return
Math
CAGR = (End Value / Start Value)^(1/n) – 1
Excel — RATE method (preferred)
=RATE(n, 0, –Start_Value, End_Value)
Excel — Power method
=(End/Start)^(1/n) – 1
Use RATE() in Excel for CAGR: nper=years, pmt=0, pv=negative start, fv=end value. This is the standard professional & exam approach.
💡 CAGR is hypothetical — it shows "as-if steady" growth. Actual year-by-year returns will differ significantly.
Simple / Holding Period Return
MATH
Raw % gain or loss on investment
Math
HPR = (Ending – Beginning) / Beginning × 100
💡 Doesn't account for time. Use CAGR or XIRR for multi-period comparisons.
Absolute Return
MATH
Total gain in ₹ terms or as a percentage
Math
Absolute Return = (Current – Invested) / Invested × 100
💡 ₹1L → ₹1.5L in 3 yrs: absolute = 50%, CAGR ≈ 14.5%. Absolute looks better but hides the time factor.
Real Return
MATH
Return adjusted for inflation — actual purchasing power gain
Exact formula
Real Return = [(1 + Nominal) / (1 + Inflation)] – 1
Approximation
Real ≈ Nominal – Inflation
💡 FD at 7%, inflation 6% → real return ≈ 0.94%, not 1%. Always use exact formula for precision.
Compound Interest
MATH
Interest earned on interest — base of all investing math
Math
A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t) P=principal, r=rate, n=freq, t=years
💡 Bank FDs: quarterly compounding (n=4). Higher n → higher effective yield. Use EAR to compare across instruments.
Rule of 72
MATH
Quick mental math for doubling time
Math
Doubling Time (years) = 72 / Annual Rate (%)
💡 At 12% CAGR → doubles in 6 yrs. At 7% FD → doubles in ~10.3 yrs. At 4% savings → 18 yrs. Shockingly accurate shortcut.
Personal Finance Ratios
Net Worth
RATIO
Snapshot of your overall financial health
Math
Net Worth = Total Assets – Total Liabilities
✓ Good: Positive & growing YoY ✗ Bad: Negative or shrinking
💡 Track monthly. Include EPF, PPF, gold, MF, property as assets. Home loan, personal loan, credit card debt as liabilities.
Savings Ratio
RATIO
% of income being saved or invested
Math
Savings Ratio = (Savings / Gross Income) × 100
✓ Good: ≥ 20% ~ Okay: 10–20% ✗ Bad: < 10%
💡 Target: 20%+ (50/30/20 rule). SIP contributions count. 30–40% is considered strong for wealth building in India.
Debt-to-Income (DTI)
RATIO
% of income consumed by EMI repayments
Math
DTI = (Total Monthly EMIs / Monthly Gross Income) × 100
✓ Good: < 30% ~ Caution: 30–50% ✗ Bad: > 50%
💡 Banks prefer DTI ≤ 40–50%. Below 30% = healthy. Above 50% = danger zone for loan eligibility.
Liquidity Ratio
RATIO
Emergency fund coverage in months
Math
Liquidity Ratio = Liquid Assets / Monthly Expenses
✓ Good: 3–6 months ✗ Bad: < 3 months
💡 Target: 3–6 months. Count only savings account and liquid MFs — not FDs with lock-in or equity holdings.
Expense Ratio
RATIO
% of income or AUM consumed by expenses
Personal Finance
Expense Ratio = (Total Expenses / Gross Income) × 100
Mutual Fund
MF Expense Ratio = Annual Fund Costs / Avg AUM
✓ Good MF ER: < 0.5% (direct) ✗ Bad: > 1.5% (regular)
💡 For MFs: lower ER = better for investor. Direct plans always have lower ER than regular plans.
Debt-to-Equity Ratio
RATIO
Leverage level — personal or company balance sheet
Math
D/E = Total Liabilities / Net Worth (personal) D/E = Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity (company)
✓ Good: < 1 ✗ Bad: > 2 (high risk)
💡 Lower = more stable. A D/E > 1 means liabilities exceed net worth — risky for personal finance.
Insurance Coverage Ratio
RATIO
Adequacy of life insurance relative to income
Math
Coverage Ratio = Life Cover / Annual Income
✓ Good: 10–15× ✗ Bad: < 5× (under-insured)
💡 Thumb rule: 10–15× annual income as term cover. ₹10L income → target ₹1–1.5 Cr cover.
Financial Asset Ratio
RATIO
% of total assets held in financial form (cash, securities, receivables)
Math
Financial Asset Ratio = Financial Assets / Total Assets
✓ Good: Higher = more liquid & flexible ✗ Bad: Too low = assets tied up in illiquid form
💡 Financial Assets include cash, FDs, mutual funds, bonds, receivables. A manufacturing company will naturally score lower than a bank or NBFC — always compare within sector.
Liquid Assets to Net Worth Ratio
RATIO
% of your net worth that is immediately accessible
Math
Liquid Assets to Net Worth = (Liquid Assets / Net Worth) × 100
✓ Good: 10–20% ~ Caution: < 10% (over-invested, under-liquid) ✗ Bad: > 50% (too much idle, not growing)
💡 Tells you if your wealth is well-structured. Too low = everything locked in property/PPF/equity with no buffer. Too high = sitting on cash that isn't working. Sweet spot is 10–20% liquid, rest growing.
Leverage Ratio (Personal)
RATIO
How much of your assets are funded by debt
Math
Leverage Ratio = Total Liabilities / Total Assets
✓ Good: < 0.5 (50%) ~ Caution: 0.5–0.7 ✗ Bad: > 0.7 (debt-heavy, risky)
💡 If you have ₹1 Cr in assets and ₹60L in loans → ratio = 0.6. Unlike corporate leverage, personal leverage above 1 means you are technically insolvent — liabilities exceed everything you own.
Solvency Ratio (Personal)
RATIO
What % of your total assets are actually yours (debt-free)
Math
Solvency Ratio = Net Worth / Total Assets × 100
✓ Good: > 50% ~ Caution: 30–50% ✗ Bad: < 30% (most assets are debt-funded)
💡 Flip side of Leverage Ratio — they always add up to 100%. Solvency 40% = Leverage 60%. If your home is worth ₹80L and outstanding loan is ₹60L → net worth from this asset = ₹20L → solvency = 25%. You own very little of it yet.
Liquid Assets to Income Ratio
RATIO
How many months of income you hold in truly liquid form
Math
Liquid Assets to Income Ratio = Liquid Assets / Monthly Gross Income
✓ Good: ≥ 3× ~ Caution: 1.5× – 3× ✗ Bad: < 1.5×
💡 Not the same as Liquidity Ratio (which is benchmarked against expenses). This one is stricter because income is usually higher than expenses — it tells you if your cash buffer is keeping pace with your earning power, not just your spending.
Investment Assets to Net Worth Ratio
RATIO
% of your net worth that is actually working for growth
Math
Investment Assets to Net Worth = (Investment Assets / Net Worth) × 100
✓ Good: > 50% ~ Caution: 25–50% ✗ Bad: < 25% (wealth mostly idle or tangible)
💡 Investment Assets = equity, mutual funds, stocks, PPF, EPF, NPS, bonds — financial assets held for growth, excluding idle cash and use assets like your home or car. Most Indian households score low here because a house dominates net worth.
📋 Which asset is which? Liquid vs financial vs use assets
Total Assets = Financial Assets + Non-Financial (Use) Assets
Financial Assets = Liquid Assets + Investment Assets

🟢 Liquid assets — convert to cash almost instantly, with no loss of value and no penalty:
Cash in hand · Savings/current account balance · Liquid & money-market mutual funds · Short-term FDs with no lock-in

🔵 Financial assets — the broader bucket. Includes every liquid asset above, plus non-liquid financial instruments (paper/contractual claims that can't be encashed instantly without loss or penalty):
FDs with lock-in · Equity & debt mutual funds · Direct stocks/shares · Bonds & debentures · PPF · EPF · NPS · ULIP/insurance cash value · Money receivable or lent out

🟠 Non-financial / use / tangible assets — physical property, not a paper claim:
House/land/property · Car & vehicles · Gold jewellery · Other physical possessions

⚠️ Common exam trap: Gold jewellery is a use/tangible asset (physical metal), but Gold ETFs, Sovereign Gold Bonds, and digital gold are financial assets (a demat/paper claim on gold) — same metal, different classification depending on the form you hold it in.

Memory hook: Liquid = spendable today · Financial = a paper/contractual claim (may or may not be liquid) · Use = something you physically use, not a claim on anything else.
Solvency, Leverage & Asset Ratios
Current Ratio
RATIO
Can the company pay short-term obligations from short-term assets?
Math
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
✓ Good: 1.5 – 3 ~ Caution: 1 – 1.5 ✗ Bad: < 1 (can't cover ST debts)
💡 > 3 can also indicate idle assets not being put to work. Indian banks look for ≥ 1.33 for working capital loans.
Quick Ratio (Acid Test)
RATIO
Stricter liquidity — excludes inventory (which may not sell fast)
Math
Quick Ratio = (Current Assets – Inventory) / Current Liabilities or = (Cash + Marketable Securities + Receivables) / CL
✓ Good: ≥ 1 ✗ Bad: < 1 (liquidity risk)
💡 More conservative than Current Ratio. Preferred for manufacturing/retail businesses where inventory can be slow-moving.
Cash Ratio
RATIO
Most conservative liquidity — only cash vs current liabilities
Math
Cash Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents) / Current Liabilities
✓ Good: 0.5 – 1 ✗ Bad: < 0.2 (very tight)
💡 Rarely used alone, but useful during credit crises. > 1 means excess idle cash — may signal poor capital deployment.
Debt-to-Assets Ratio
RATIO
What % of total assets are funded by debt
Math
Debt-to-Assets = Total Debt / Total Assets
✓ Good: < 0.5 (50%) ~ Caution: 0.5 – 0.7 ✗ Bad: > 0.7 (heavily debt-funded)
💡 0.4 = 40% of assets are debt-financed. Higher ratio → more financial risk and interest burden.
Leverage Ratio (Financial Leverage)
RATIO
How much debt is used to amplify returns — or risk
Math (most common form)
Leverage Ratio = Total Assets / Shareholders' Equity or = Total Debt / EBITDA (for credit analysis)
✓ Good: < 2× (conservative) ~ Watch: 2× – 4× ✗ Bad: > 4× (high leverage)
Leverage > 1 is not automatically bad — it amplifies returns. But it ALSO amplifies losses. High leverage + falling revenues = bankruptcy risk. Infrastructure/utilities tolerate higher leverage; tech/startups should stay lower.
Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR)
RATIO
Can operating profit comfortably cover interest payments?
Math
ICR = EBIT / Interest Expense (EBIT = Earnings Before Interest & Tax)
✓ Good: > 3× ~ Caution: 1.5× – 3× ✗ Bad: < 1.5× (interest strain)
💡 ICR = 2 means EBIT is 2× the interest — a thin cushion. Indian banks want ICR ≥ 1.5 for loan approvals. ICR < 1 = interest not even being covered.
Solvency Ratio
RATIO
Long-term ability to meet all obligations — creditor's lens
Math
Solvency Ratio = (Net Income + Depreciation) / Total Liabilities or = Net Worth / Total Assets
✓ Good: > 20% ✗ Bad: < 10% (insolvency risk)
💡 Measures long-term financial health, not just short-term. Used heavily by insurance regulators (IRDAI requires solvency ratio ≥ 1.5 for insurers in India).
Asset Turnover Ratio
RATIO
How efficiently assets generate revenue
Math
Asset Turnover = Net Revenue / Average Total Assets
✓ Good: Higher is better (industry-relative) ✗ Bad: Declining over time
💡 Retail (Dmart): high turnover (~2–3×). Heavy industry (steel, infra): low turnover (<0.5×). Compare within same sector only.
Return on Assets (ROA)
RATIO
Profit generated per rupee of total assets
Math
ROA = Net Income / Average Total Assets × 100
✓ Good: > 5% (varies by sector) ✗ Bad: < 1% or negative
💡 Banks typically have low ROA (0.5–1.5%) because assets are huge. Non-financial companies target 5–15%.
Return on Equity (ROE)
RATIO
Profit generated per rupee of shareholders' equity
Math
ROE = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity × 100
✓ Good: > 15% ~ Okay: 10–15% ✗ Bad: < 10% or negative
💡 Warren Buffett's favourite metric. High ROE with low debt = excellent business. High ROE with very high debt may be misleading — check D/E alongside.
Debt Service Coverage (DSCR)
RATIO
Can cash flow cover all debt obligations (principal + interest)?
Math
DSCR = Net Operating Income / Total Debt Service (Debt Service = Principal + Interest repayments)
✓ Good: > 1.25× ✗ Bad: < 1× (cash flow insufficient)
💡 DSCR = 1.25 means NOI is 25% more than required debt payments — a buffer. Banks commonly require DSCR ≥ 1.25 for project finance in India.
Bonds & Fixed Income
Bond Price
EXCEL
PV of all future coupon payments + face value at maturity
Math
Price = Σ [C/(1+r)ᵗ] + [FV/(1+r)ⁿ] C=coupon, r=YTM, n=periods
Excel
=PV(ytm/freq, n*freq, –coupon, –face_value)
💡 Price & YTM move inversely. Rates rise → bond prices fall. Longer duration bonds are more sensitive to rate changes.
YTM (Yield to Maturity)
EXCEL
Total annualized return if held to maturity
Excel — RATE method
=RATE(n, coupon, –price, face_value) × freq
Excel — YIELD function
=YIELD(settlement, maturity, rate, pr, redemption, freq)
💡 YTM is effectively the IRR of all bond cash flows. Multiply RATE() by payment frequency to annualize.
Current Yield
MATH
Annual coupon as % of current market price
Math
Current Yield = Annual Coupon / Current Market Price
💡 Simpler than YTM — ignores capital gain/loss at maturity. Always use YTM for a complete picture.
Macaulay Duration
MATH
Weighted average time to receive all cash flows
Math
D = Σ [t × PV(CFt)] / Bond Price
💡 Measured in years. Zero-coupon bond duration = maturity. Higher coupon → shorter duration. Basis for interest rate sensitivity.
Modified Duration
MATH
% price change for 1% change in yield
Math
Mod Duration = Macaulay Duration / (1 + YTM/n)
Price change approximation
ΔPrice ≈ –Mod Duration × Δyield × Price
💡 If Mod Duration = 5 and yield rises 1% → price falls ~5%. Convexity corrects for larger yield moves.
Convexity
MATH
Curvature correction for price-yield relationship
Combined price change
ΔPrice ≈ –D*×Δy + ½×Convexity×(Δy)²
💡 Duration underestimates price rise and overestimates price fall. Convexity fixes this asymmetry. More convex = better for the bondholder.
Risk & Performance Metrics
Sharpe Ratio
PERF
Excess return per unit of total (overall) risk
Math
Sharpe = (Rp – Rf) / σp Rp=portfolio return, Rf=risk-free, σp=std dev
💡 >1 = good, >2 = excellent. Risk-free rate in India ≈ 6.5–7% (91-day T-bill). Higher = better risk-adjusted return.
Sortino Ratio
PERF
Like Sharpe, but only penalises downside volatility
Math
Sortino = (Rp – Rf) / Downside Std Dev
💡 Better than Sharpe for equity MFs — upside volatility shouldn't be punished. Preferred for comparing equity fund managers.
Alpha (Jensen's Alpha)
PERF
Excess return vs what market model predicts
Math
α = Rp – [Rf + β × (Rm – Rf)]
💡 Positive alpha = fund manager added value above market-predicted return. α = 0 → exactly matched CAPM prediction.
Beta
PERF
Sensitivity of stock/fund to Nifty / market movements
Math
β = Cov(Rp, Rm) / Var(Rm)
💡 β=1: moves with market. β>1: amplified (aggressive). β<1: dampened (defensive). β=0.8 → if Nifty falls 10%, stock falls ~8%.
Information Ratio (IR)
PERF
Consistency of outperformance vs tracking error
Math
IR = (Rp – Rb) / Tracking Error Tracking Error = StdDev(Rp – Rb)
💡 IR > 0.5 = good fund manager. Higher IR = more consistent alpha generation, not just lucky periods.
M² (Modigliani-Modigliani)
PERF
Sharpe ratio scaled to benchmark risk — gives a % figure
Math
M² = Sharpe × σm + Rf
💡 Expresses risk-adjusted return as %, directly comparable to benchmark return. M² > Rm → outperformed on risk-adjusted basis.
Standard Deviation
EXCEL
Measure of return volatility / total risk
Excel
=STDEV(returns_range) ← sample (use this) =STDEVP(returns_range) ← population
💡 Use STDEV for fund analysis. Higher SD = higher volatility = more risk. MF factsheets report annualized SD.
Max Drawdown
MATH
Largest peak-to-trough loss in value
Math
Max DD = (Trough Value – Peak Value) / Peak Value
💡 Critical for risk-averse investors. NAV ₹50 → ₹30 = –40% drawdown. No direct Excel function — calculate from cumulative return series.

Key Takeaway
Aditi's net worth is positive — but that single headline number hides real stress underneath. Her EMI burden, savings rate, and liquidity all come back "bad," and leverage, solvency, and financial-asset quality all sit in caution territory.
This is the classic "asset rich, cash-flow poor" trap: a big home loan taken early can make someone look financially healthy on a balance sheet while their monthly cash flow and emergency buffer are quietly under strain.
Never judge financial health from Net Worth alone — it's the ratio that mixed results the most easily.

Two fictional companies — one healthy, one stressed — to see every ratio in context.

Sunrise Retail Ltd — Healthy Company
A mid-size Indian retail chain. Revenue ₹50 Cr | EBIT ₹8 Cr | Net Income ₹5 Cr | Total Assets ₹40 Cr | Current Assets ₹18 Cr | Inventory ₹6 Cr | Current Liabilities ₹10 Cr | Total Debt ₹12 Cr | Equity ₹28 Cr | Interest ₹1.5 Cr | Cash ₹4 Cr
Current Ratio
18 Cr / 10 Cr
= 1.8 ✓
Can comfortably cover short-term dues. Within the ideal 1.5–3 range.
Quick Ratio
(18 – 6) Cr / 10 Cr
= 1.2 ✓
Even without selling inventory, can still pay current liabilities.
Cash Ratio
4 Cr / 10 Cr
= 0.4 ~
Can't pay all CL from cash alone — but that's normal. Acceptable range.
Debt-to-Equity
12 Cr / 28 Cr
= 0.43 ✓
Less than 1 — equity funds most of the business. Low financial risk.
Leverage Ratio
40 Cr / 28 Cr
= 1.43× ✓
Conservative. Assets are 1.43× equity — modest use of debt to grow.
Interest Coverage (ICR)
8 Cr / 1.5 Cr
= 5.3× ✓
EBIT is 5× interest — very comfortable. Lenders would be happy.
Debt-to-Assets
12 Cr / 40 Cr
= 30% ✓
Only 30% of assets are debt-funded. Strong asset base relative to debt.
ROE
5 Cr / 28 Cr × 100
= 17.8% ✓
Good. Generating ₹17.8 per ₹100 of shareholder equity. Buffett-worthy.
ROA
5 Cr / 40 Cr × 100
= 12.5% ✓
Excellent. Every ₹100 of assets generates ₹12.5 in net profit.
Asset Turnover
50 Cr / 40 Cr
= 1.25× ✓
Good for retail. Each rupee of asset generates ₹1.25 in revenue.
Solvency Ratio
28 Cr / 40 Cr × 100
= 70% ✓
Highly solvent. Equity covers 70% of total assets. Very strong long-term position.
Redstone Infrastructure Ltd — Stressed Company
An infra company with heavy borrowing. Revenue ₹30 Cr | EBIT ₹3 Cr | Net Income ₹0.5 Cr | Total Assets ₹60 Cr | Current Assets ₹8 Cr | Inventory ₹5 Cr | Current Liabilities ₹12 Cr | Total Debt ₹45 Cr | Equity ₹15 Cr | Interest ₹4 Cr | Cash ₹1 Cr
Current Ratio
8 Cr / 12 Cr
= 0.67 ✗
Cannot cover short-term liabilities from current assets. Liquidity risk.
Quick Ratio
(8 – 5) Cr / 12 Cr
= 0.25 ✗
Extremely tight. Even immediate receivables barely cover a quarter of CL.
Cash Ratio
1 Cr / 12 Cr
= 0.08 ✗
Almost no cash buffer. One bad month = potential default on short-term dues.
Debt-to-Equity
45 Cr / 15 Cr
= 3.0 ✗
Debt is 3× equity. For every ₹1 of equity, there are ₹3 of debt obligations.
Leverage Ratio
60 Cr / 15 Cr
= 4.0× ✗
High leverage. Assets are 4× equity — any revenue drop severely impacts solvency.
Interest Coverage (ICR)
3 Cr / 4 Cr
= 0.75× ✗
EBIT cannot even cover interest! Company is consuming capital. Red flag.
Debt-to-Assets
45 Cr / 60 Cr
= 75% ✗
75% of assets are debt-funded. If assets fall in value, equity is wiped out fast.
ROE
0.5 Cr / 15 Cr × 100
= 3.3% ✗
Very poor. Shareholders earning less than an FD rate on their investment.
ROA
0.5 Cr / 60 Cr × 100
= 0.8% ✗
Near zero. Massive asset base generating almost no profit. Efficiency problem.
Asset Turnover
30 Cr / 60 Cr
= 0.5× ~
Low but expected for infra. Only ₹0.50 revenue per ₹1 of assets. Sector-normal.
Solvency Ratio
15 Cr / 60 Cr × 100
= 25% ✗
Equity covers only 25% of assets. Long-term solvency depends on debt rollover.
Key Takeaway
Sunrise Retail is profitable, liquid, and conservatively financed — a business you'd confidently invest in.
Redstone Infra has high assets but is drowning in debt with insufficient earnings to cover even its interest. Its leverage is not "productive" — it's a liability.
Always read ratios together, not in isolation. And always compare within the same industry.
