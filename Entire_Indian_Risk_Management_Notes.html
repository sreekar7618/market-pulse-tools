1.1 Economic, commercial and social aspects of insurance

Insurance is a protection and risk management mechanism. It matters at four levels:

Level What insurance does (textbook)
Economy / Government A well-built insurance sector enhances risk-taking because it gives safety from unanticipated loss-causing events. Insurers hold long-term funds, so investing in long-term projects becomes easier, leading to rapid infrastructure development.
Individual / family Insurance money supports the family after loss due to death, immobility, work restraints, illness, etc.
Society Mitigates the effects of illness, accident, death and natural disasters. It manages, diversifies and absorbs risks of individuals and companies so they can recover from sudden misfortune — a precondition for economic development.
Commerce / trade Once external risks are insured, productive activity (buying a home, starting or expanding a business) is spurred. This fuels the demand chain, supply and trade, giving level consumption patterns and wider financial and social stability.

India's insurance journey has gone full circle: unregulated → completely regulated (nationalised) → partly deregulated (private companies allowed, FDI allowed up to 49% as of 2019).

India is one of the fastest-growing economies, so its insurance market is poised for strong growth. IRDAI is actively working on: retention ratio, claim settlement ratio, protecting policyholders' interests, grievance redressal, consumer financial education and insurance literacy, preventing unfair business practices, and spurious calls.

Hook: "Free → Fenced → Freed" — free market before 1956, fenced by nationalisation, freed again after 1999–2000. That is the "full circle".

1.2.2.2 Health insurance

Health insurance is an emerging sector, driven by India's huge population, rising healthcare costs and increasing longevity.

  • Standalone health insurers grew 36.56% in 2018-19.
  • Health premium has grown over 20% year-on-year for the last 4 years.
  • Three classes: Government-sponsored, group, and individual health insurance.
  • In 2018-19, general and health insurers issued about 2.07 crore policies covering 47.20 crore lives.

Exam trap: Lives covered (47.20 crore) are far more than policies (2.07 crore) because one group or Government policy covers many lives.

1.2.2.3 Re-insurance

  • After de-nationalisation in 2000, GIC Re became the only Indian reinsurer. Foreign reinsurers worked only as servicing / liaison offices for their parents.
  • Doors opened for foreign reinsurers in 2016, after the Insurance Act was amended. The Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015 allowed foreign reinsurers and the Society of Lloyd's to open branches in India — aim: make India a reinsurance hub.
  • GIC Re's net premium in 2018-19 rose 3.62% to Rs. 38,996 crore.
  • ITI Reinsurance Ltd was designed along with GIC Re to optimise retention within India, but it never commenced business and surrendered its registration.
  • Sec 101A, Insurance Act 1938: every insurer must reinsure with the Indian reinsurer such percentage of the sum insured on each general insurance policy as the Authority specifies (this is the "obligatory cession").

Hook: "101A = 1 Indian Reinsurer gets a slice of every general policy."

1.3 Laws governing insurance business in India

1.3.1 The Insurance Act, 1938

The primary legislation governing all types of insurance, giving strict state jurisdiction over insurance business. Amended by the Insurance (Amendment) Act, 2002. Applies to all states of India.

Part What it contains
Part I Definitions — "insurance company", "insurer", "insurance agent", "actuary", "intermediaries", etc.
Part II The main section — provisions for insurers: registration, policies and claims, furnishing reports, investments, assignment and transfer of policies, registration of intermediaries including agents, rural / social sector business, and other protections for the insured
Part V Penalties for default; gives the Authority (IRDA) power to make regulations

Hook: Part I = Identify (definitions). Part II = Instruct Insurers. Part V = Violations (penalties).

1.3.2 The Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015

Amended three Acts: the Insurance Act 1938, the General Insurance Business (Nationalisation) Act 1972, and the IRDA Act 1999. IRDAI got further authority to frame rules.

What changed:

  • Various sections amended, omitted, substituted or newly inserted.
  • Investment provisions strengthened, including the ban on investing funds outside India.
  • IRDAI's power of investigation and inspection increased.
  • Provisions on assignment and transfer, nomination, and prohibition of commission payments strengthened.
  • Revised limits on management expenses.
  • New provisions on appointment and records of agents.

The four headline points:

Point Rule (textbook)
FDI cap (Sec 7A) Raised to 49%
Mis-statement No life policy can be called in question on any ground whatsoever after 3 years from the policy date
Reinsurance Foreign reinsurers allowed to do business in India
Appeals Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT) made the appellate authority against IRDAI orders

Hook: "3 – 3 – 49 – SAT" → amended 3 Acts, 3-year incontestability, 49% FDI, appeals to SAT.

q

Example: Ravi buys a term policy in January 2020 and hides his diabetes. He dies in February 2024 — more than 3 years after the policy date. Per the textbook rule the insurer cannot question the policy on any ground. Had he died in 2021, the insurer could still investigate the non-disclosure. (This rule is popularly called "Section 45" — my addition, not in the textbook.)

1.3.3 Law relating to Agency — Indian Contract Act, 1872

Agency is a relationship in which one person (the agent) has power to act on behalf of another (the principal) and create legal relationships between the principal and third parties. A financial adviser must understand it because nearly all business is done through agent–principal relationships.

  • Chapter X of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 deals with agency.
  • Sec 182: an "agent" is a person employed to do any act for another, or to represent another in dealings with third persons. The person for whom the act is done, or who is represented, is the "principal".
  • It also covers appointment and authority of agents / sub-agents, the agent's duties to the principal, and the principal's duties to the agent.
  • Agency arises when one person delegates authority to another or appoints him for specific tasks; it creates rights and duties for both.
  • Examples: insurance agency, advertising agency, travel agency, brokers.

Hook: "182 — One acts, the other is bound." The agent signs; the principal is liable.

Enacted to protect consumers' interests and set up authorities for timely and effective settlement of disputes. Applies to all goods and services. It repeals the 1986 Act.

Hook: 2019 = "Consumer comes home" — the consumer can now file from where he lives or works.

1.3.5 Doctrines of Waiver and Equitable Estoppel

These doctrines link to an insurance agent's responsibilities and can work in the policyholder's favour — they can decide whether a policy stays in force or a claim is paid.

Waiver = the conscious, voluntary giving up of a known legal right. It is unilateral. The right may be constitutional, statutory or contractual. The key test: did the person give it up voluntarily?

Type How it arises Textbook example
Express waiver A clear statement (oral or written) that a right is given up Insurer notifies the policyholder that it has not lapsed the policy for non-payment, though it had the right to
Implied waiver Conduct that clearly shows the right will not be enforced Insurer accepts premium after the grace period — it impliedly waives the right to say the policy lapsed

Estoppel = a rule that prevents a person from going back on what he said or did earlier (Cambridge: prevents someone changing their mind in court about something they said was true; Investopedia: prevents arguing contrary to a prior claim or act). Often called a rule of fair play.

In insurance: the insurer waives a right → the policyholder relies and acts on it → the insurer is stopped from claiming that right later.

Textbook example: A proposer fails to disclose some information; the insurer does not ask for it and issues the policy (waiver). When a claim arises later, the insurer cannot question the contract for that non-disclosure (estoppel).

Point Waiver Estoppel
Nature Voluntary surrender of a known right Bar on going back on one's words or conduct
Who acts One party alone (unilateral) Arises from the other party's reliance
Timing Comes first Follows the waiver once reliance happens
Insurance effect Insurer gives up a right (e.g. lapse) Insurer cannot later enforce that right

Hook: Waive → Rely → Estop. Waiver: "I let it go." Estoppel: "Now I can't take it back."

q

Exam trap: If a question says the insurer accepted a late premium → think implied waiver. If it says the policyholder acted on that acceptance and the insurer now wants to deny the claim → answer estoppel.

2.1 Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI)
Feature Textbook position
Created by IRDA Act, 1999, on the Malhotra Committee's recommendations
Status Autonomous and statutory body, incorporated in April 2000
Purpose To regulate and develop the insurance industry
Composition 10 members — Chairman + 5 full-time + 4 part-time members, all appointed by the Government of India
Source of power to frame regulations The Insurance Act, 1938 (the principal Act)
Mission "To protect the interests of the policyholders, to regulate, promote and ensure orderly growth of the insurance industry and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto."

Hook: 1 + 5 + 4 = 10. One captain, five full-timers, four part-timers.

2.1.1 Duties, powers and functions (Sec 14, IRDA Act 1999)

  1. Registering and regulating insurance companies
  2. Protecting policyholders' interests
  3. Licensing and setting norms for insurance intermediaries
  4. Promoting professional organisations in insurance
  5. Regulating and supervising premium rates and terms of general insurance covers
  6. Specifying financial reporting norms for insurers
  7. Regulating investment of policyholders' funds by insurers
  8. Ensuring insurers maintain their solvency margin
  9. Safeguarding insurance cover in rural and weaker sections of society

Hook (in order): "Real Policyholders Love Proper Pricing From Insurers, Solvent & Rural" → Register, Protect, License, Promote, Premium rates, Financial reporting, Investment, Solvency, Rural.

2.1.2 Licensing and governance of insurers and intermediaries

The regulator ensures insurers follow good governance with emphasis on overall risk management and financial stability.

  • 2009: IRDAI issued comprehensive Corporate Governance guidelines.
  • 2016: Revised because of the Companies Act, 2013, after consulting industry and stakeholders — called the Corporate Governance Guidelines for Insurers in India – 2016.
  • Objective: The Board and management must recognise the expectations of all stakeholders and the Regulator, and be able to address non-compliance quickly.

Elements covered by the 2016 Guidelines:

# Element
1 Governance structure
2 Board of Directors
3 Key management functions
4 Role of appointed actuaries
5 External audit — appointment of statutory auditors
6 Disclosures
7 Relationships with stakeholders
8 Whistle-blower policy

2.1.3 Apex insurance regulator and industry watch-dog

  • IRDAI is the apex body; every insurer is registered with it and it sets all rules for the industry.
  • It draws power from two Acts — the IRDA Act 1999 and the Insurance Act 1938. Its functions under Sec 14 are far-reaching.
  • It is the watch-dog for both the insurance and reinsurance industries.
  • Role: protect policyholders, oversee industry growth, help economic growth.
  • Insurers and associates — agents, TPAs, brokers, surveyors, repositories — work under IRDAI's rules, with stiff reporting guidelines.

Hook: IRDAI has two parents — the 1999 Act gave birth, the 1938 Act gave power.

2.1.4 Supervision of the Tariff Advisory Committee (TAC)

Feature Textbook position
Created by Insurance (Amendment) Act, 1968 — a statutory body
Purpose "To control and regulate the rates, advantages, terms and conditions that may be offered by insurers in respect of general insurance business"
Managed under Insurance Act 1938, IRDA Act 1999 and IRDAI regulations
Chairman IRDAI Chairman
Vice-Chairman A senior IRDAI officer (nominated)
Members Ten elected members from the Indian market

Duties under Sec 14(2), IRDA Act 1999:

  1. Drawing up and updating tariffs
  2. Clarifying insurers' queries
  3. Ensuring implementation of tariffs
  4. Collecting and analysing data
  5. Publishing tariffs and other regulations
  6. Inspecting risks

The IRDA Act left TAC's role as regulator of products and prices unchanged, but IRDAI supervises TAC. Their work areas are complementary but mutually exclusive. TAC decisions within its jurisdiction (following prescribed procedure) are final and binding on all non-life insurers and the insured.

Hook: TAC = Tariffs And Conditions — general insurance only, never life. (See the Discrepancy Box for TAC's current status.)

2.1.5 Power to issue guidelines and directions

  • Sec 14, Chapter IV of the IRDA Act empowers IRDAI to act in policyholders' best interest and to regulate, promote and ensure orderly growth of insurance and reinsurance business.
  • IRDAI can delegate its powers to the Chairperson or any member, and form committees of members by general or special order.
  • It can set up an Insurance Advisory Committee and, in consultation with it, make regulations consistent with the Act.
  • Every rule and regulation must be laid before each House of Parliament while in session.

Link to Chapter 4: Sec 25 of the IRDA Act sets up the Insurance Advisory Committee. Draft regulations go to it first → approved by IRDAI → notified in the Gazette of India.

2.2 Insurance Councils and the General Insurance Council

2.2.1 Constitution and powers

Council Role (textbook)
Life Insurance Council Forum linking all stakeholders; coordinates discussion between Government, regulator and public; works through sub-committees; includes all life insurers
General Insurance Council Key link between IRDAI and the non-life industry; plays its role under the Insurance Act and facilitates overall industry growth

Both were constituted under Sec 64C of the Insurance Act, 1938 in 2001. They work through sub-committees and include all insurance companies in India.

Mission of the Insurance Councils:

  • Act as an effective forum to assist and advise insurers in maintaining high standards of conduct and service to policyholders
  • Interact with Government and other bodies on policy matters
  • Spread insurance awareness in India
  • Develop education and research in insurance

2.2.2 Self-regulatory mechanism

  • Powers come from the Insurance Act 1938 and the IRDA Act 1999.
  • They work as a self-regulatory organisation (SRO) — codes of conduct for members, compliance programmes, representing the collective interest of insurers, guiding members.
  • They build a positive image of the industry and help insurers serve policyholders efficiently.
  • They link the Indian industry with global markets and other financial-sector bodies.
  • The General Insurance Council also has a Code of Good Insurance Practices — general provisions, application of law and regulations, claims handling and grievance redressal.

Hook: 64C = Councils. Remember "C for Council". Don't confuse with 1957 — that was the original General Insurance Council that framed a code of conduct (Chapter 1).

2.3 Insurance Information Bureau of India (IIB)

Why it exists: Insurance is data-driven, including pricing. After private entry, IRDAI needed credible data support, but no agency pooled industry-level data.

Point Detail
Constituted 2009 by IRDAI, with an advisory body chaired by IRDAI
Registered as independent society November 2012
Verticals Life, Health, Motor, Property, Fire, Engineering — supported by IT and analytics

Main functions:

  1. Sole point for all insurance industry data
  2. Make data accessible to market players, researchers, policyholders and the public for real-time decisions
  3. Provide benchmark rates for the industry
  4. Publish reports to help IRDAI regulate and insurers decide
  5. Publish reports for the benefit of the whole industry
  6. Provide mandated inputs for policy analysis and industry development
  7. Lead fraud detection and identification of uninsured vehicles

How it works:

  • Insurers submit transaction-level data on policies and claims (demographic, policy and product attributes) at set intervals → IIB produces annual, thematic and customised reports and research.
  • IIB provides data to IRDAI for setting third-party motor premium rates.
  • V-Seva: a call-centre, SMS and web service for the public, police, transport departments and insurers — insurance status of a vehicle, stolen vehicles, recovered vehicles, accident records.
  • Central Index Server: nodal point between any two insurance repositories; helps de-duplicate e-insurance accounts.
  • Repository of insurance salespersons: de-duplication using key identifiers to confirm an applicant is not already engaged with another insurer or intermediary.

Hook: IIB = India's Insurance Brain (it remembers all data). V-Seva = Vehicle Seva. Third-party motor rates → IIB supplies the data, IRDAI sets the rate.

2.4 Insurance Ombudsman

2.4.1 Establishment and objectives

  • Created by a Government of India notification in 1998.
  • Purpose: quick disposal of policyholder grievances in an efficient, economical and impartial manner.
  • The insurer is the first point of contact. Only if the reply is unsatisfactory, or there is no reply, does one approach the Ombudsman.
  • The Executive Council of Insurers (earlier the Governing Body of Insurance Council, GBIC) was set up under the Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017 to set up and facilitate the ombudsman institution. It comprises members of the Life and General Insurance Councils.
  • Complaints entertained include: repudiation of claims, delay in settlement, premium disputes, disputes on legal construction of policies, and non-issuance of documents after premium receipt.
  • There are 17 Ombudsmen in different cities. A complaint must be in writing to the Ombudsman having jurisdiction over the location of the insurer's office.

2.4.2 Appointment, tenure and jurisdiction

Point Textbook position
Appointed by Executive Council of Insurers, on recommendation of a committee of the Chairman of IRDA, Chairman of LIC, Chairman of GIC and a Central Government representative
Drawn from Insurance industry, Civil Services, Administrative Services, Judicial Services
Tenure 3 years or till age 70, whichever is earlier; eligible for re-appointment
Jurisdiction 17 Ombudsmen, each with a geographical area; can hold sittings at different places within it

Hook: Selection committee = "I L G C" → IRDA, LIC, GIC, Central Govt. Tenure = "3 or 70, whichever first."

2.4.3 Rights and powers (Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017)

Notified on 25 April 2017, revising the 2016 Rules. The Ombudsman receives complaints on:

Code Complaint type
(a) Delay in claim settlement beyond the time in the regulations
(b) Repudiation of claims, wholly or partly
(c) Disputes on premium paid or payable
(d) Misrepresentation of policy terms and conditions
(e) Legal construction of policies
(f) Policy servicing grievances against insurers, agents and intermediaries
(g) Policy issued not in conformity with the proposal form
(h) Non-issuance of policy after receipt of premium
(i) Any other violation of the Insurance Act or IRDAI rules, circulars, guidelines

Also:

  • The Ombudsman acts as counsellor and mediator only with the written consent of the parties.
  • The Ombudsman cannot handle a matter where he is an interested party or has a conflict of interest.

Hook for (a)–(i): "Don't Reject Policyholders — Mediate Legally, Promptly, Impartially, Never Opportunistically" → Delay, Repudiation, Premium, Misrepresentation, Legal construction, Policy servicing, Issued wrongly, Non-issuance, Other violations.

q

Example: Kiran's health claim is rejected. She writes to the insurer's grievance cell and gets an unsatisfactory reply. She can now file a written complaint under code (b) repudiation with the Ombudsman covering the insurer's office location.

2.5 Insurance Institute of India (III)
  • Formerly the Federation of Insurance Institutes, formed in 1955 in Mumbai to promote insurance education and training.
  • Awards certificates and diplomas recognised by the Government, IRDAI and insurers in India and abroad; also recognised by institutes in the UK, Canada and the US for exemptions from some of their papers.
  • Apex education and training provider for all sectors of the industry.

2.5.1 Authority and functions

Point Detail

Council members From LIC and GIC; they elect a President, Deputy President and other office-bearers
Terms President 2 years; Deputy President and other office-bearers 1 year
Council's duty Coordinate and direct the Institute's work; report annually on its position; make rules / bye-laws

Main functions:

  1. Run college and conduct exams for certificates, diplomas and degrees in insurance
  2. Prepare reading material and alternative education methods
  3. Form and maintain a library
  4. Provide scholarships, grants and prizes for research
  5. Ascertain and disseminate law and practice relating to insurance
  6. Help people in the industry acquire skills and expertise

2.5.2 Education and training

  • IRDAI recognises III as the examining body for pre-recruitment exams of insurance agents, corporate agents, web aggregators, IMFs, the renewal exam for insurance brokers, and the pre-licensing test for surveyors and loss assessors.
  • The Directorate of Postal Life Insurance licensed III to develop course material for PLI agents and to conduct their licensing exam.

Hook: III = "Insurance's Institute of Instruction" — born 1955, one year before LIC (1956).

4.3 Income sources and rate-fixing

Insurers earn in three main ways:

Source How it works
(a) Underwriting income Premiums collected minus claims paid. Large in years with few claims; small in years of heavy claims (e.g. natural calamities)
(b) Reinvesting Part of premiums invested in low-risk investments — stocks, bonds, interest-bearing accounts
(c) Lapsed coverage Premiums kept when policies lapse, or when the insured outlives a term policy — premiums collected, no payout

Rate-fixing (insurance pricing) = deciding the rates or premiums to charge — also for reinsurance and other risk-transfer methods. A rate reflects three elements:

  1. Loss cost per unit of risk exposure
  2. Administrative expenses
  3. Profit

Rates must keep equity among all insured and meet four actuarial criteria:

Criterion Meaning
Reasonable Not excessive
Not disproportionate Fair to the risk
Adequate Enough to pay claims and costs
Not unfairly biased No unfair discrimination

Hook: A good rate is "R-A-N-D" — Reasonable, Adequate, Not disproportionate, Doesn't discriminate unfairly.

4.3.1 Premium and types

Every policy is a contract: if the insured suffers a covered loss, the insurer pays. In return the insured pays a fee — the premium. Premium must cover losses, administrative costs and profit, but must not be excessive, and the same rate should apply to all members of an underwriting class with a similar risk profile.

Term Definition
Pure premium Part of the premium needed to pay losses and loss-related expenses (from actuarial studies)
Load Addition to cover other expenses (especially sales) and profit
Gross rate Pure premium + Load (per exposure unit)
Gross premium What the applicant pays = Gross rate × number of exposure units
Gross Rate = Pure Premium + Load
Gross Premium = Gross Rate × Quantity of Exposure Units

Example: Pure premium = Rs. 3.50 per Rs. 1,000 sum assured; load = Rs. 1.50 per Rs. 1,000. Gross rate = 3.50 + 1.50 = Rs. 5 per Rs. 1,000. For a sum assured of Rs. 50 lakh, exposure units = 50,00,000 ÷ 1,000 = 5,000. Gross premium = 5 × 5,000 = Rs. 25,000.

4.3.2 Factors in fixing premium; rate-making

Premium fixing considers selling goals, competition, legal restrictions, and estimated future costs of risk transfer: claims and their settlement expenses, operating and administrative costs, and the cost of capital.

The insurer's problem: premium is collected before losses and expenses are known; true costs are known only after the period ends. Large insurers keep their own databases of loss frequency and amount; small insurers rely on rating bureaus.

Life insurance rate-making is simpler because mortality tables give deaths at each age across a large population. Life rates are based on mortality, interest and expense. Important principles:

  • Rates should be fair
  • Rates should produce premium adequate to meet total losses, but not unreasonably large profits
  • Rates should be revised often enough to reflect current costs
  • Life rates rise with age, as death is more likely when older

Hook: Life pricing = "M-I-E" → Mortality, Interest, Expense.

4.3.2.1 Mortality tables and actuarial valuation

  • A mortality table (life table / actuarial table) shows the rate of deaths likely in a defined population over a time interval — the probability of dying before the next birthday at each current age.
  • It is expressed as expected deaths per 1,000 living in a year, usually from birth to age 100 in one-year steps.
  • Built separately for men and women; may also reflect smoking status, occupation, income and socio-economic class.
  • Actuaries use them to assess risk and the probability of a claim; separate sickness tables estimate losses from sickness.
  • Mathematically: work out the death benefit payable in each future year, its present value, and the probability of death in each year → premium. Higher claim probability = higher premium.

Example (illustrative numbers, mine): For a 1-year term cover of Rs. 1 crore at an age where the table shows 1.2 deaths per 1,000, probability of death = 0.0012. Expected claim = 1,00,00,000 × 0.0012 = Rs. 12,000. Discounted for 1 year at 6%: 12,000 ÷ 1.06 = Rs. 11,320.75 — that is the pure premium before load.

4.3.2.3 Rates of guaranteed benefits

  • In traditional plans, instead of a profit-dependent bonus, some insurers declare a Guaranteed Addition (GA) or guaranteed return — the investment grows at a guaranteed rate, as shown in the benefit illustration.
  • Designed for risk-averse investors: life cover plus guaranteed payouts at stages, besides maturity.
  • Structures vary: guarantee based on premium paid or on sum assured; may vary with policy term or premium-paying term; may start from the 2nd year or later.
  • Downside: higher premium than non-guaranteed plans, so returns after the cost of the guarantee are low. Choose only if it suits the client.

4.3.2.4 Right premium and adverse selection

  • Premium is the consideration that makes the insurance contract valid; charging the right premium keeps the insurer viable.
  • Normal premiums assume average good health and a safe work environment.
  • Adverse selection = the insurer accepts applicants at greater-than-normal risk who have concealed information about their condition. The insurer, with less information, charges the wrong (lower) premium → losses.
  • Defence: questionnaires to capture material information, followed by detailed underwriting.
  • Textbook example: a habitual smoker who doesn't exercise hides these facts to avoid higher premium → charged a normal premium despite higher risk.

Implications:

  • Insurance is not commercially viable when buyers know more about their risk than the insurer.
  • Above-average-risk people buy; below-average-risk people find it too costly versus their perceived risk and don't buy.
  • Premiums set on average risk (underwriting + mortality tables) become insufficient to pay claims.

Example (illustrative): A pool has 500 low-risk people (1% claim chance) and 500 high-risk (3%). Sum assured Rs. 10 lakh. Average risk 2% → pure premium Rs. 20,000. Low-risk people's expected loss is only Rs. 10,000, so they leave. Now the pool is all high-risk with expected claim Rs. 30,000 per person — the insurer loses Rs. 10,000 per policy.

q

Hook: Adverse selection = "those who need it most buy it most — and hide why."

4.4 Distribution of benefits (bonuses)

All premiums collect in the insurer's corpus. A large part is invested in Government-secured debt, a small part in equities. Based on investment earnings, valuation of assets and liabilities, and claims paid, the insurer distributes profit to "with-profits" policyholders — this is a bonus.

  • Bonuses are declared annually during the policy term.
  • A bonus may or may not be declared every year — it depends on profitability — and the amount varies each year.
  • Bonus types are spelled out in the policy brochure; check the bonus type before buying.

4.4.1 Bonus — "with-profit" or participating plans

  • Plans that share profit with policyholders are with-profit (participating) plans.
  • Policyholders pay a comparatively higher premium, which entitles them to — but does not guarantee — a share of profit.
  • The bonus percentage may vary by type of policy, term and premium.

4.4.2 Simple and compound reversionary bonus; Guaranteed Additions

Reversionary bonus = bonus declared regularly and paid at maturity, on surrender, or on death.

  • Once declared, it becomes part of the guaranteed benefits.
  • Expressed as a percentage of the sum assured; normally declared annually at the end of each financial year.
Type How it is computed
Simple reversionary bonus % of sum assured only. Past bonuses earn nothing further
Compound reversionary bonus % of sum assured + all accumulated bonuses (bonus on bonus)

Different insurers follow different approaches.

Guaranteed Additions (GAs):

  • A form of payout, calculated at a rate per thousand of sum assured.
  • Allowed for each year premiums are paid.
  • Disclosed upfront at purchase.
  • At claim, added to the basic sum assured.

Example — simple vs compound (my numbers): Sum assured Rs. 5,00,000; bonus rate 5% (Rs. 50 per Rs. 1,000) for 3 years.

Simple: 5,00,000 × 5% = Rs. 25,000 each year → 3 years = Rs. 75,000.

Compound: Year 1: 5% × 5,00,000 = Rs. 25,000. Year 2: 5% × 5,25,000 = Rs. 26,250. Year 3: 5% × 5,51,250 = Rs. 27,562.50. Total = Rs. 78,812.50.

q

4.4.4 Interim bonus

  • Paid when a policy matures or a death claim arises between two bonus declaration dates.
  • The policy has all declared bonuses up to the last financial year end but nothing for the gap period since. A pro-rata bonus at the interim bonus rate is added for that period.
  • Ensures mid-year claimants get benefit for the part-year the policy was in force.

Example: Bonus declared up to 31 March 2026. The policy matures on 30 September 2026. Interim bonus covers April–September (6 months) pro-rata at the interim rate.

Hook: Reversionary = Returns at the end. Terminal = Thank-you at Termination. Interim = In-between dates. Compound = bonus on bonus.

4.6 Loans against life insurance policies

After premiums are paid for a few years (usually 2–3), the policy builds cash / surrender value. The policyholder can borrow a portion of it.

  • The loan is secured, so the insurer does limited scrutiny (no credit-score focus) → faster and easier.
  • In early years the cash value is small, so the loan amount is limited.

Conditions insurers place:

Condition Textbook position
Eligible policies Not term plans. Only traditional plans — endowment, money-back, whole life — with guaranteed return; the policy is the collateral
Waiting period Three years, to check regular premium payment
Loan amount 80–90% of surrender value for traditional plans; for ULIPs, depends on corpus value at application
Interest Generally lower than personal loans; specified in the policy document
Premiums Must continue after the loan
Security Policy is assigned to the insurer till repayment

Repayment:

  • Loan and interest should be repaid during the policy term.
  • If only interest is paid, the principal is deducted from the claim at settlement.
  • If the policyholder dies during the loan, the pending amount is deducted from the claim paid to the nominee.
  • If unpaid interest keeps adding up and the loan exceeds the surrender value, the policy lapses and cover is lost.

Advantages: unlocks cash value while keeping risk cover and without surrendering; the loan is not income, so not taxable.

Example: Surrender value Rs. 2,00,000 → maximum loan at 90% = Rs. 1,80,000. Later the policyholder dies with Rs. 1,50,000 outstanding; SA plus bonus is Rs. 10,00,000. Nominee receives 10,00,000 − 1,50,000 = Rs. 8,50,000.

q

Exam trap: "Loan against a term plan" → not possible (no cash value).

4.7.3 Group gratuity schemes

Gratuity is a statutory benefit under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, paid by the employer in recognition of long service, on:

  1. Retirement
  2. Permanent total disablement during service
  3. Death during service
  4. Resignation after 5 continuous years of service
Point Textbook position
Applies to Organisations with 10 employees during the last financial year
Eligibility Employees with 5 years' continuous service, paid on leaving
Nature Defined benefit plan — based on last drawn salary and years of service
Higher benefit? Employer may give more than the Act requires

Group gratuity schemes let the employer build funds for this future liability by investing in insurers' Group Gratuity Plans (equity and debt funds, market-linked returns). The corpus pays gratuity when employees retire or leave.

  • Tax: premium paid is a business expense, reducing taxable income; the fund also earns returns.
  • Insurers review employee data each year (salaries, exits, joiners) and revalue the liability.
  • If the employer doesn't deposit each year's gratuity, accounts are not true and fair under AS-15, risking disputes.

4.7.3.1 Actuarial valuation

Purpose: compute the present value of future employee-benefit payments. Assumptions:

Assumption Use
Discount rate Converts future payments to present value — shown as the company's liability in its financial statements
Salary escalation and attrition Company's best estimates of future increments and staff turnover
Others Retirement age, disability, mortality, leave balance, resignation
  • Chapter IX of the Companies Act, 2013 requires an actuarial valuation and disclosures per ICAI accounting standards.
  • AS 15 requires valuation by an external certified actuary at least once a year — auditors or company officials cannot do it themselves.
  • Companies funding through an insurer can get AS 15 valuation certificates from the insurer, signed by a qualified actuary.

4.7.3.2 Methods to manage gratuity liability

Gratuity is not part of salary but a legal obligation on exit.

Pay-as-you-go (internal resources) Provision in the balance sheet on accrual basis using an actuarial report; pay from own funds when an employee leaves Provisions not deductible — Sec 40A(7)

Three tax benefits of funding:

  1. 8.33% of basic salaries paid into the fund each year is deductible
  2. 8.33% for each year of past service, when liability is funded for the first time, is deductible
  3. Interest / investment income inside the fund is tax-free

4.8 Investment-linked insurance — Unit Linked Insurance Plan (ULIP)
  • ULIPs combine protection with investment. Part of the premium buys life cover; the rest is invested in funds and the investor is allotted units.
  • Investment risk is borne entirely by the policyholder.
  • Charges — insurance cover, fund management, policy administration, surrender, fund switching — are deducted by selling (cancelling) units; remaining units stay invested.

4.8.1 Protection, investment and tax benefits (subject to lock-in)

  • The investor chooses the life cover and investment amount by varying the premium, and chooses funds to suit his risk profile.
  • Premium stays constant, but the cost of insurance rises each year with age → more units are sold for insurance charges, leaving fewer invested.
  • Unlike participating whole life / endowment plans, ULIPs do not guarantee cash values; fund value depends on unit prices at redemption / maturity.
  • As needs change, the investor can vary cover and investment mix, top up, withdraw and switch funds.

Tax (textbook):

Stage Rule
Premium Sec 80C, up to Rs. 1,50,000. Policy must be kept in force 5 years and premiums paid regularly
Maturity Sec 10(10D) — exempt if premium is up to 10% of sum assured throughout the term

4.8.2 Choice of funds and switch option

ULIPs give market-linked returns; NAVs rise and fall with capital markets, and the policyholder bears this risk.

Fund Invests in Risk Suits
Equity Equity funds, company stocks High (volatile) but higher long-term growth High risk appetite
Debt Corporate bonds, G-secs, T-bills, commercial paper, money market instruments Lower; aim is interest income Lower risk appetite
Hybrid / balanced Mix of bonds and equities Medium; stable and prudent Moderate
Money market (cash fund) Money market instruments; set returns on maturity Lowest Very conservative

Switch option: move units partly or fully between equity, debt and balanced funds as risk appetite and goals change, to safeguard returns from volatility. Some insurers allow unlimited switches; others 5–10 per policy year. Good results need constant monitoring.

Hook: Risk ladder from top: E > H > D > M — "Every House Deserves Money."

4.8.3 NAV-based redemption, maturity and claim settlement

NAV = market value of the fund's investments minus liabilities (management fees, marketing, operating and other expenses).

Fund value of a policy = Number of units held × NAV on that day

NAV is posted daily on the insurer's website, so payouts are not fixed. Three payout situations:

Event Payout (textbook)
Surrender during the 5-year lock-in Surrender value after deducting applicable charges from fund value
Surrender after lock-in, before maturity Fund value = NAV on that date × units
Maturity Fund value = NAV on maturity date × units
Death during term Nominee gets the higher of sum assured or fund value

Example: 12,000 units, sum assured Rs. 10 lakh. If NAV = Rs. 42.50, fund value = 12,000 × 42.50 = Rs. 5,10,000 → death benefit = Rs. 10,00,000 (SA is higher). If NAV = Rs. 95, fund value = 12,000 × 95 = Rs. 11,40,000 → death benefit = Rs. 11,40,000 (fund value is higher).

q

Hook: ULIP death claim = "whichever is bigger" — SA or fund value.

4.9 Contingency planning
  • A contingency (emergency) fund handles unexpected events that could cause temporary financial loss — e.g. a sudden job loss.
  • An emergency fund should be a household's first savings goal, against loss or reduction of income.
  • Keep it in liquid assets; replenish it as soon as possible after use; review its adequacy periodically.

4.9.1 Disability insurance with premium waiver option

Disability = inability to perform all the material duties of one's job; may be temporary (short-term) or permanent.

  • Disability insurance protects earned income against disability from sickness or injury; covers paid sick leave and short- and long-term disability benefits.
  • Especially needed by the self-employed and those whose employers don't provide it.
  • Premium varies by insurer, occupation and age.

Premiums are higher for policies that:

Feature Why costlier
Pay higher monthly benefits More payout
Pay for longer periods More payout
Start paying sooner after a claim Shorter waiting period
Define disability broadly Pays in more situations

Check before buying: (a) waiting period before income starts, (b) cover for short- and long-term disability, (c) cover for disability from accidents and/or illnesses.

Waiver of premium for disability: the insurer will not require premiums if the insured is seriously injured. Definitions of disability and how long the waiver lasts vary; insurers may charge a higher premium for this provision.

Hook: Disability premium rises when benefits are MORE, LONGER, SOONER, BROADER.

4.9.2 Child plans with premium waiver

  • A mix of investment and insurance for a child's future needs. Fixed amounts can be invested from an early age to mature at, say, 18; can be bought at the child's birth.
  • Fund big events — higher studies, marriage. Can be market-linked (NAV-based).
  • Premiums: Sec 80C; benefits: tax-free under Sec 10(10D).

Key features:

  • Single premium, limited-period or regular premiums; amount depends on sum assured and insurer
  • Tenure 10 years and above; covers children from birth to a maximum of 25 (with exceptions)
  • Maturity amount chosen to suit needs
  • Money-back child plans pay in stages — lump sum or yearly instalments
  • In ULIP versions, partial withdrawals are allowed for difficulties

Waiver of premium — an inherent rider in child education plans: if the parent (policyholder) dies within a stipulated period, the insurer waives all future premiums till maturity. The policy continues, the planned maturity benefit stays intact, in addition to the death benefit paid.

Example (illustrative): Ramesh buys a child plan at his daughter's birth: premium Rs. 50,000 a year, sum assured Rs. 10 lakh, maturity at age 18. Ramesh dies when she is 6. The family receives the Rs. 10 lakh death benefit now; the insurer pays the remaining 12 premiums; at 18 she still receives the full maturity benefit.

4.9.3 Married Women's Property Act (MWPA) and insurance planning

Problem: life insurance proceeds can be recovered by the deceased's creditors, whose rights supersede beneficial nominees.

Solution: buy the policy under Sec 6 of the MWP Act — it protects the wife and children, and creditors cannot attach it.

Sec 6 (paraphrased): a policy taken by a married man on his own life, expressed to be for the benefit of his wife, or wife and children, or any of them, is deemed a trust for their benefit. So long as any object of the trust remains, it is not subject to the control of the husband or his creditors, and does not form part of his estate.

Once bought under MWPA, while the named beneficiaries are alive, no one else has any right to the benefits — not the husband's parents, not his creditors, and not even the husband himself (including survival benefits).

Key points:

  • The policy is treated as a trust
  • Beneficiaries (wife and/or children), once named, remain unchanged for the whole term
  • Only trustees control the policy and receive the benefit
  • On death, proceeds go to the trust and are claimed only by trustees, held for the wife and/or children
  • The policyholder cannot take a loan against an MWPA policy
  • If a cash-value policy is surrendered, proceeds go to the beneficiaries
  • If the husband survives the term, maturity proceeds still go to the beneficiaries

Hook: MWP = "My Wife's Protection" — creditors can't touch it, parents can't touch it, even the husband can't touch it.

q

Example: Vikram, a businessman with Rs. 2 crore of business loans, buys a Rs. 1 crore term policy under MWPA for his wife and son. If he dies, the lenders cannot claim the Rs. 1 crore; trustees hold it for his wife and son.

q

Exam trap: MWPA policy → no loan, beneficiaries cannot be changed, and maturity / surrender proceeds go to the beneficiaries, not the husband.

4.10 Insurance policy document and legal implications

A policy is an integrated contract between insured and insurer — a policy of assurance subject to the conditions and privileges in the document.

  • It is legally enforceable; both parties are bound for the whole term.
  • It is issued after the first premium is paid.
  • It is the "bible" for insured and insurer.

4.11 Policy revival schemes
  • A life policy lapses if premiums aren't paid even within the grace period.
  • Automatic revival: pay outstanding premiums with interest, generally within 6 months of the first unpaid premium.
  • Other revival terms depend on sum assured, length of lapse, period the policy has run, and the life assured's age, health and occupation. Insurers usually want evidence of continued good health — a self-declaration and/or a medical report.
Scheme How it works (textbook)
4.11.1 Ordinary revival Pay all unpaid premiums + interest at the branch, within 6 months from the lapse date; self-declaration of good health, plus medical reports depending on age

4.11.5 Surrender of policy

Exiting before maturity = surrender. Textbook: a regular premium policy can be surrendered after 3 years of continuous premiums. The surrender value is part of accumulated bonus and premiums, and differs across insurers.

Type Textbook definition

4.11.6 Assignment of policy

  • The assignee cannot increase the death benefit.

4.12 Claims

A claim is the insured's demand that the insurer settle as promised in the contract, triggered by an insured event. Prompt settlement is one of the insurer's most important services. At settlement the insurer checks:

  1. Has the insured event happened?
  2. Has the policyholder met his obligations — premiums, age proof, etc.?
  3. Who is entitled to the money?

4.12.1 Maturity claims

  • Paid on completion of the term, if all premiums were paid on time.
  • Submit the original policy document and a filled maturity claim (discharge) form.
  • Textbook: maturity money is tax-free under income tax law.
Plan Maturity amount
Money-back (4.12.1.1) Periodic survival benefits paid during the term; at maturity: sum assured less survival benefits already paid
Endowment / whole life (4.12.1.2) Sum assured + accumulated bonuses − outstanding premiums and interest

Example: 20-year money-back, SA Rs. 5 lakh, survival benefits of 15% at years 5, 10 and 15 = 3 × 75,000 = Rs. 2,25,000. Maturity = 5,00,000 − 2,25,000 = Rs. 2,75,000 (plus bonuses, if the plan has them).

4.12.2 Death claims

  • Payable where premiums are up to date or death occurs within the grace period.
  • Other triggers like terminal or critical illness may apply depending on policy terms.
  • Paid as a lump sum or annuity (for a stated period or the beneficiary's lifetime).

4.12.2.1 Claimant — nominee / assignee or legal representative:

  • The policyholder names a nominee; if the nominee is unavailable or dies, the legal heir becomes the beneficiary.
  • Intimation can be made by relatives even if not nominee or assignee; the nominee completes the procedure.
  • Beneficiaries can be a business, charity or group.
  • Minor nominee → money goes to the appointee or legal guardian.
  • Critical illness, accident and disability rider benefits go to the life insured if alive.
  • No nomination → claimant must provide proof of title; insurer may ask for more documents.

4.12.2.3 Non-early death claim (beyond 3 years) — missing persons:

  • Secs 107 and 108 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872: a person not heard of for 7 years is presumed dead.
  • Insurers usually need a decree from a competent court.
  • The insurer may act without a decree if strong circumstantial evidence shows the person could not have survived a fatal accident or hazard.
  • Premiums must be paid until the court decrees presumption of death; insurers may waive premiums during the 7 years as a concession — at their option.

5.4 Loss assessors

A loss assessor is an expert contracted by the person making the claim to get it processed successfully.

  • Handles the regulations and processes needed for settlement; documents the claim; answers queries from both the customer and the insurer — from paperwork to practical aspects.
  • Meets the insurer's staff and their loss adjusters to negotiate the best settlement. Strong negotiator, expert on policy terms, quantifies claims accurately and realistically, understands how claims departments work.
  • Useful where a claim has been declined or delayed.
  • Engage early — ideally as soon as you know you must claim. The later, the harder to build a successful claim.

How a loss assessor works (house fire example from the textbook): inspects the house, checks its monetary value, evaluates damage and whether it is claimable → collects evidence → prepares a report and sends it to the insurer on behalf of the claimant → negotiates until the claim is properly settled.

Hook: Loss assessor = the claimant's champion. Loss adjuster = the insurer's person.

5.5 Classification of general insurance

Any insurance that is not life insurance is general insurance (named differently in different countries). It covers valuables other than life — house, land, office, cargo, travel, motor, marine — i.e. property, vehicles, cash, household goods, and liability to others.

Broad categories:

  1. Non-life (home and contents, travel)
  2. Health
  3. Agriculture
  4. Credit
  5. Reinsurance
  6. Liability
  7. Motor vehicle

A general insurance policy usually runs for one year, renewable yearly. Premium and cover depend on type and extent of insurance.

5.5.1 Non-life insurance (property)

Insurance of physical goods and property — building, machinery, equipment, furniture, vehicles — against fire, earthquake, accident or theft. Examples: home-owners policies, fire damage, marine accidents, travel, theft, automobile policies. Because these risks' probability is hard to estimate, the damage is hard to measure in advance.

5.5.2 Health insurance

  • Reimburses medical expenses — treatments, surgeries, hospitalisation — from injury or illness, including future medical expenses.
  • A contract between insurer and customer (the textbook says "an employer or an organisation"); usually renewed yearly, sometimes lifetime.
  • In India, health insurance usually pays only for hospitalisation of at least 24 hours. Nursing, bed, surgeon / doctor fees and OT charges are covered in most cases.
  • Certain specified diseases may be excluded or covered only after some years. Aesthetic treatments and treatments not needing a hospital stay are excluded.
  • Mediclaim policies were India's first health policies; private insurers then introduced family floaters, top-ups, critical illness and hospital cash plans.

5.5.2.2 Group health insurance for corporates — tax

  • Bought by an employer for eligible employees as a key benefit; sometimes a statutory obligation; may cover family members.
  • Members join and leave as they join and leave the company; premiums are low (lower risk); lenient on pre-existing illnesses.
  • Tax: the employer deducts the premium it pays as an employee-benefit expense; the employee must be in active employment to benefit.
  • The employee cannot claim 80D for group premium paid by the company.

Exam trap: Employer-paid group health premium → business expense for the employer, no 80D for the employee.

5.5.3 Agriculture insurance

Why it matters in India: (1) agriculture is risky because of unpredictable, uncontrollable perils; (2) most farmers are small and can't absorb losses.

  • Covers loss of crop production from natural disasters, and loss of revenue from a fall in commodity prices. Bought by farmers or planters.
  • Textbook: agriculture insurance in India started in 1985; the Comprehensive Crop Insurance Scheme (CCIS) covered major crops (see Discrepancy Box on the wording).
  • Government schemes: NAIS, Pilot WBCIS (weather-based), Pilot MNAIS (modified NAIS), Pilot CPIS (coconut palm).
  • Private players entered in 2003.

Covers the payment risk from selling goods or services on credit — e.g. an Indian manufacturer selling to an overseas buyer on credit. The insurer covers a group of buyers and pays a percentage of receivables left unpaid because of insolvency, bankruptcy or protracted default. The buyer may be in the same or another country.

5.5.5 Reinsurance — General Insurance Corporation of India (GIC Re)

Reinsurance = insurers buying insurance from other insurers to share and limit their own loss. It lets an insurer accept clients whose cover would be too big for it alone; premium is shared among the participating insurers.

Term Meaning
Primary insurer (ceding company) Issues the policy and cedes part of the risk
Reinsurer (accepting company) Accepts future liability from the primary insurer

GIC Re background:

  • General insurance was nationalised by the General Insurance Business (Nationalisation) Act, 1972 (GIBNA). The Government took over the shares of 55 Indian insurers and the undertakings of 52 insurers (55 + 52 = 107).
  • GIC was formed under Sec 9(1) of GIBNA, incorporated in 1972 under the Companies Act 1956 as a private company limited by shares, to superintend, control and carry on general insurance.
  • November 2000: re-notified as the Indian Reinsurer.
  • GIC Re is the only domestic reinsurer giving reinsurance directly to Indian general insurers, and receives a statutory cession of 5% on every policy, subject to limits.
  • It also runs reinsurance programmes for insurers in SAARC countries, South-East Asia, the Middle East and Africa.

5.5.5.2 Concept of ceding

  • An insurer needs enough capital to pay all future claims on policies issued — this protects consumers but limits how much business it can write.
  • By transferring part of the liability to a reinsurer, it needs less capital; the freed capital supports more or bigger policies and helps keep premiums lower.
  • The ceding company hedges against unwanted exposure and stays solvent even after a big claim. The reinsurer receives premium and pays its share of claims. Their agreement is the reinsurance contract.

Example (textbook rate): An insurer writes a refinery for Rs. 500 crore. Obligatory cession at 5% = Rs. 25 crore to GIC Re. The insurer retains, say, Rs. 75 crore and places the remaining Rs. 400 crore facultatively with other reinsurers.

5.5.6 Liability insurance — legal liability policies

Liability insurance protects individuals and businesses against risks for which they may be held legally liable — claims for malpractice, injury, negligence, and damage to people or property. The insurer pays what the insured would owe if found liable.

It protects against loss when the insured's actions or negligence, or his property, cause injury / death or damage to someone's property, or when someone suffers a loss relying on the insured's services or advice.

  • Best suited to business owners, professionals and the self-employed.
  • Covers two financial risks: (a) legal cost of defending a claim, (b) compensation payable.
  • Three forms: public liability, professional indemnity, product liability.

5.5.6.1 Public liability

  • Covers legal costs if an individual or entity is liable for injury or death of another person, economic loss, or property damage due to its negligence.
  • Optional but recommended, since negligence suits are likely and costly. For public events it is compulsory and checked by the licensing authority.
  • Small industries often skip it because of high premium. Where risk is exceptionally high, insurers refuse or charge excessive premium.
  • Does not cover people or establishments in hazardous and criminal activities.

The Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991

Why: hazardous industries endanger employees and the public nearby. Employees are protected by other laws, but the public — often from weaker sections — faced long legal battles. So public liability insurance was made mandatory for hazardous installations.

  • Sec 4: every owner must take a public liability policy before starting to handle any hazardous substance, to give immediate relief for injury, death or property damage.

Environmental Impairment Liability (EIL)

  • Environmental damage = injurious presence of solid, liquid, gaseous or thermal contaminants, irritants or pollutants on land, air or water — sometimes immediate, sometimes evident years later.
  • EIL (pollution legal liability) covers injury, property damage, natural resource damage, clean-up costs etc. from pollution emanating from a site.
  • Examples: chemical companies whose substances poison groundwater; manufacturing by-products reaching fields and water bodies; tanneries dumping waste in open plots, spreading disease.
  • EIL is written on a claims-made basis: the claim must be first reported during the policy period, and the pollution must have occurred after the policy's retroactive date.

Hook: Claims-made = "Reported NOW, happened AFTER the retro date."

5.5.6.2 Product liability

  • Law expects every product to meet the ordinary expectations of the consumer; a defect or danger means it doesn't.
  • Product liability governs the liability of manufacturers and sellers in the distribution chain for injury to persons or property from using the product.
  • Product liability insurance covers third-party damage, injury, illness, accidental death or property damage caused by the product or service — including packaging defects and missing warning labels / precautions.
  • Can be taken by manufacturers and all partners in the seller chain; also extends to their vendors.
  • Indian courts take a pro-consumer approach, awarding damages more punitive than compensatory.

5.5.6.3 Professional indemnity

  • Protects doctors, engineers, architects, lawyers, financial consultants etc. from legal costs and claims for monetary loss or property damage after following their advice or service.
  • Should also cover directors, partners, employees and related entities acting within their duties.
  • Covers amounts the professional becomes legally liable to pay as damages to third parties; sometimes defence costs, within the indemnity limit.
  • Only civil liability is covered — criminal acts or acts violating law are not.
  • Claim size is unknown, so insurers set a limit of liability; the balance is borne by the insured.
  • Premium: 0.30% to 1% of the amount insured, depending on profession, experience, income limits, jurisdiction and claim experience.

Example: A financial planner buys PI cover with a limit of Rs. 1 crore at a rate of 0.5% → premium = 0.005 × 1,00,00,000 = Rs. 50,000. If a client wins a Rs. 1.3 crore award, the insurer pays Rs. 1 crore; the planner bears Rs. 30 lakh.

5.5.6.4 Employer's liability insurance

  • Employers are responsible for employees' health and safety at work and may be liable for accidents arising in the course of employment — e.g. injury from factory chemicals, food infection from the canteen.
  • Employers' liability insurance pays the cost of employees' injury, illness or death from workplace conditions or practices, on site or off site, and saves the employer from financial loss and lawsuits.
  • Public liability is different: it covers claims by members of the public or other businesses, not employees.

Hook: Employer's liability → inside people (employees). Public liability → outside people (public).

Employer's Liability Act, 1938

  • Protects workmen at their workplaces.
  • Defines the employer for workers deputed by contractors or manpower agencies — useful when work is outsourced.

  • Abolishes the old rule that an employer is not liable if the injury was caused by the fault or negligence of a fellow employee.

5.6 Motor insurance
  • Compulsory for every vehicle on the road. Protects against bodily injury, death, physical damage and third-party liability from accidents.
  • Follows the principles of property and liability insurance. The owner has a legal relationship with the vehicle — he gains from its safety and loses from its damage or liability (i.e. insurable interest).
Type Textbook description

5.6.1 The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

An Act of Parliament regulating all aspects of road transport vehicles. No motor vehicle can be used in a public place without a policy from an authorised insurer covering liability for death or bodily injury to third parties and damage to third-party property.

Reasons for the Act (textbook list):

  • Road safety standards, pollution control, standards for transporting hazardous and explosive materials
  • Effective ways to track traffic offenders
  • Stricter procedures for granting driving licences and their validity
  • Fitness certificates for vehicles also by authorised testing stations
  • Higher compensation in hit-and-run cases and in "no-fault liability"
  • Compensation from the insurer to accident victims up to actual liability for all vehicle classes
  • State registers of driving licences and vehicle registration
  • Road Safety Councils
  • Adequate compensation to victims without long-drawn procedure; protecting consumers in transport
  • Greater powers to State Transport Authorities; rationalising roles of public authorities

5.6.2 The Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Bill, 2019

Brought to amend the 1988 Act on road safety, third-party insurance, vehicle fitness and victim compensation.

  1. Higher penalties — drunken driving, juvenile driving, driving without licence, over-speeding, dangerous driving, overloading
  2. Mandatory fitness testing, removal of unfit vehicles, testing standards
  3. Penalty for deliberately violating safety and environmental rules
  4. Recall of defective vehicles that may threaten the environment
  5. National Road Safety Board to advise on road safety, registration and licensing
  6. Good Samaritan guidelines for people helping accident victims
  7. Compensation for victims and a scheme for cashless treatment
  8. Motor Vehicle Accident Fund by the Centre to give compulsory insurance cover to all road users

5.6.4.2 Third-party liability — compulsory insurance

Indemnifies the insured for an accident caused by use of the vehicle, for:

  • Death or injury to any person, including occupants of the vehicle
  • Damage to property not belonging to the insured

Process: inform the insurer immediately of any incident likely to cause a liability claim → submit claim form with copies of RC, driving licence and FIR → send any court summons to the insurer at once.

Example: Rahul's delivery van hits a pedestrian and damages a shop shutter. He has a liability-only policy. The pedestrian's injury and the shop damage are covered (third party). Repairs to Rahul's own van are not covered — that needs a comprehensive policy.

6.1 Insurance taken from multiple companies

Life insurance policies

  • Major life events — buying a house, marriage, a child, starting a business — raise liabilities, so life cover should rise too: buy an additional policy or increase the existing cover.
  • Multiple goals: insurers offer plans per goal — children's education, endowments, deferred annuities.
  • Term policies can be bought with terms matched to a life event — e.g. the length of a business or home loan, or the years the family needs support.
  • Reducing term / ladder strategy: buy several term policies with different terms that expire as debts are paid down. Total premium over time is lower than one large single-cover policy.
  • Multiple insurers: spreads the risk of any one insurer and gives competitive premiums. But avoid applying to several insurers at the same time to prevent complications.
  • Remember: insurance is meant to restore the earlier financial state, not to profit.

Human life value (HLV) and limits:

  • Cover is based on current income, earning capacity and assets.
  • A new insurer asks for details of all existing life policies to see how much uncovered value remains.
  • Total cover across all policies cannot exceed the future income the person will earn.
  • Insurers also check premium-paying capacity — gross income, disposable income, potential growth.
  • These HLV limits don't apply strictly to endowment plans, where current and future earning potential mainly decide premium capacity. In with-profit policies, life cover is small relative to premium compared with term plans.

Example (ladder strategy): Ajay needs Rs. 1 crore of cover today: Rs. 50 lakh for 25 years (family support), Rs. 30 lakh for 15 years (home loan) and Rs. 20 lakh for 10 years (business loan). He buys three term policies. Cover steps down as loans end: Rs. 1 crore → Rs. 80 lakh (after year 10) → Rs. 50 lakh (after year 15). Per the textbook, this costs less over time than one Rs. 1 crore, 25-year policy.

Group life insurance

Often part of an employee benefit; no individual underwriting; optimum premium. Factories and small manufacturers may extend it to workers under a group savings-linked insurance scheme — such employers must provide it.

Property insurance taken by a housing society

A society's insurance on the whole building is not sufficient for individual flat owners:

  • It mainly restores the skeletal structure after permanent damage; only partial cover applies for limited damage.
  • A member's own damage may be extensive while the building policy pays little or nothing.
  • If the damage starts inside a member's flat and is mostly internal, the building cover may not be invoked at all.
  • Movables — furniture, gadgets, furnishings — and the flat's interiors are not covered even if the society's policy applies.

6.3 Surrender of a life policy — merits and demerits

Surrender = encashing a life policy before its maturity.

  • A policy acquires surrender value only after full three years' premiums are paid (textbook). A ULIP can be stopped and surrendered after five years from the start.
  • Only policies with a savings element (ULIPs, endowments) return part of the money. Pure term policies return nothing if not renewed.
  • Surrender should be a last resort — only when sufficient alternative cover is in place, or the premium is too high for the benefits.
  • The surrender value comes at a steep discount.
  • Alternatives: some endowment plans give loans; or the policy can be made paid-up, keeping life cover plus a terminal value on survival.
Merits of surrendering Demerits of surrendering
Exit long-term policies with high premiums and fancy promises — bonuses and money-backs are your own premium being returned Loss of life cover — family exposed to death / disability risk
Little sense in keeping many small-SA policies from different insurers; missed premiums cause lapse, penalties and medicals for revival Tax benefits (80C) and regular / loyalty bonuses stop
— Surrender charges on the paid-up value can cut the surrender value significantly

Hook: Before surrendering, climb down the ladder: Loan → Paid-up → Surrender (last), and never before replacement cover is in force.

Example (illustrative): Kavya (32) has an endowment policy with SA Rs. 12 lakh and premium Rs. 60,000 a year, paid for 4 years. Her family actually needs Rs. 1 crore of cover. Sequence: first buy adequate term cover, then decide between making the endowment paid-up or surrendering it, after comparing the surrender value net of charges.

Four broad categories in India: term, endowment, money-back, ULIP.

More detail from the textbook:

  • Term insurance can be bought for long tenures — 10, 20 or 30 years — and scales to cover the future income of the earner (human life value).
  • With-profit policies pay mortality charges out of premium; after admin charges the rest goes into safe investments whose final return barely beats inflation.
  • Endowment combines life cover and savings; it may look attractive with tax benefits if premium stays within the defined range versus SA. But over long tenures the investment case is weak, life cover is insufficient, and inflation erodes the maturity value even after bonuses and loyalty additions.
  • Money-back gives cash flows every 4–5 years but with high premiums.
  • ULIP has a low-cost structure, liquidity, returns and tax benefits, and the flexibility to move from equity to debt as goals approach.

Example (using the textbook's 10× ratio, illustrative): If Rs. 1 crore of term cover costs about Rs. 10,000 a year, the same Rs. 1 crore of cover through a with-profit endowment would cost roughly Rs. 1 lakh a year.

Hook: The textbook's logic boils down to "Buy term for protection; invest separately for goals."

6.6 Health insurance vs critical illness insurance — the crucial difference
  • Health insurance pays hospitalisation expenses — bed / ICU, room rent, doctor's fee, ambulance, medicines given in hospital — reimbursing only actual expenses, subject to limits and the sum insured.
  • Critical illness cover pays for serious, life-threatening illnesses listed in the policy — cancer, open-heart CABG, open-heart valve replacement / repair, kidney failure, etc. Hospitalisation is not required: once diagnosed and the policy conditions are met, a lump sum is paid.
Point Health insurance Critical illness cover
How it works Reimburses actual costs of hospitalisation, subject to limits Pays a lump sum on diagnosis of a listed critical illness
What it covers Actual medical expenses billed by the hospital during hospitalisation The policy's specified benefit — no link to actual cost, no hospitalisation condition
Range Many illnesses, widespread cover Only the critical illnesses listed

Example: Rohan is diagnosed with cancer. His health policy (SI Rs. 10 lakh) pays the hospital bills of Rs. 6 lakh. His critical illness policy (Rs. 25 lakh) pays the full Rs. 25 lakh on diagnosis, whatever the bills — money he can use for lost income, EMIs or home care.

6.7 Basic health policy, top-up / super top-up, and floater

A basic health policy covers hospitalisation, including pre- and post-hospitalisation, medicines, doctor's fees and diagnostic tests. It comes in two variants:

Variant Textbook position
Individual plan Covers one person up to their sum insured. Useful when a member is high-risk (family history, pre-existing ailment). If several members are high-risk, separate policies with adequate SI each are recommended — but this gets expensive for the family budget
Family floater Optimises premium: one sum insured usable by any member in the year, assuming not everyone is hospitalised together. Usually covers husband, wife, dependent children (up to about 25) and dependent parents. Recommended as a first cover for a young, healthy family

Top-up plans:

  • If the base policy (including a floater) has a low SI, a top-up raises cover at a much lower cost than the base policy.
  • The textbook recommends a top-up — with the same or a different insurer — over raising the base SI, which may carry a scalable premium.
  • A top-up has a deductible up to which the base policy pays. Textbook example: a Rs. 20 lakh top-up that starts only above Rs. 10 lakh, which the base policy covers.
Type How it works (textbook)
Simple top-up Pays when a claim exceeds the deductible in a single hospitalisation. Each claim is treated individually; multiple claims in the year are not aggregated
Super top-up Pays for the cumulative medical expenses of multiple hospitalisations in the policy year, of one or more members (family super top-up). Can be bought from any insurer, not just the base insurer. A long-run cost-saver; especially useful for senior citizens with frequent expenses

The drawback of a simple top-up: it doesn't help when a person relapses or another illness strikes in the same year with bills that each stay below the deductible.

Worked example — why super top-up wins (my numbers): Base policy Rs. 5 lakh. Top-up / super top-up Rs. 20 lakh with a Rs. 5 lakh deductible. Two hospitalisations in the year: Rs. 4 lakh and Rs. 4 lakh (total Rs. 8 lakh).

Base policy: pays Rs. 4 lakh for the first and the remaining Rs. 1 lakh for the second → Rs. 5 lakh in total, leaving Rs. 3 lakh unpaid.

Simple top-up: checks each bill separately; each Rs. 4 lakh bill is below the Rs. 5 lakh deductible → pays nil. Out of pocket = Rs. 3 lakh.

Super top-up: adds the year's bills: Rs. 8 lakh exceeds the Rs. 5 lakh deductible by Rs. 3 lakh → pays Rs. 3 lakh. Out of pocket = nil.

q

Second case: One hospitalisation of Rs. 12 lakh. Base pays Rs. 5 lakh; both a simple top-up and a super top-up pay Rs. 7 lakh (the amount above the deductible).

Situation Simple top-up Super top-up
One big bill above the deductible Pays excess Pays excess
Several bills, each below the deductible, total above it Pays nothing Pays the excess of the total

6.11 Global coverage of insurance policies

Life insurance policies

  • Life policies have worldwide coverage — the insurer honours the policy whether the insured is in India or abroad at the time of the event. If the policyholder dies abroad, the nominee is eligible.
  • Insurers usually ask applicants to fill a travel questionnaire. They may decline frequent travellers to high-risk zones, or charge higher premium for adventure sports abroad. Once issued, the cover is worldwide.
  • An existing Indian policy continues when you move abroad, if you keep paying and give accurate information about past, current and future residency / travel. Some exclusions may apply.

Factors an insurer considers before continuing cover abroad:

Factor Insurer's view
How long abroad Usually no issue for short periods
Where Some countries covered; high-risk countries excluded
What you'll do Dangerous activities — sky-diving, para-gliding, hot-air ballooning, water skiing, ice-skating, car racing, other adventure sports — may lead the insurer to refuse cover abroad

Practical conditions:

  • Some insurers insist premiums are paid from an Indian bank account, and may want a valid Indian address proof.
  • For claims, all documents — claim form, death certificate, certificates from authorities — are submitted in India; the policy sum is payable only in India.
  • For Rupee policies issued to foreign nationals who are not permanent residents, bonus, maturity, surrender or claim money may be paid in Indian Rupees or remitted abroad if the claimant wishes.
  • Example (an insurer's NRI plans): remittances are made in Indian Rupees; if premium was paid in foreign currency, remittance is permitted only in proportion to the premium paid in foreign currency.

Example (proportion rule): Total premiums Rs. 10 lakh, of which Rs. 6 lakh paid in foreign currency (60%). Maturity value Rs. 20 lakh → up to 60% = Rs. 12 lakh can be remitted abroad.
