How Indian succession law evolved

India has no uniform civil code, so religious personal laws governing succession had to be codified.

Year Statute (textbook) Note
1865 Indian Succession Act, 1865 (repealed) Based on English law; excluded native Indians through many exceptions
1865 Parsi Intestate Succession Act For Parsis
1870 Hindu Wills Act Brought wills and codicils made by Hindus within its ambit
1881 Probate and Administration Act Applied to Hindus and Muslims
1889 Succession Certificate Act —
1925 Indian Succession Act, 1925 (ISA) Enacted to bring uniformity and consolidate succession law

Hook: "65 – 70 – 81 – 89 → 1925 merges them all." The 1925 Act is the consolidator.

Example: Two brothers dispute their late father's flat in Hyderabad with no will. Under the textbook's warning, the flat can be locked in litigation for years — rent unpaid, maintenance neglected, value eroding. A registered will or a family arrangement made while the father was alive would have avoided this.

1.1 Laws of inheritance

India's religious diversity makes a uniform civil code hard; communities follow their own personal laws for marriage, divorce, succession etc. Personal law = law applying to a class or group, based on religion, faith and culture. Succession law is complex and depends on the deceased's personal law, usually based on religion.

The three laws of succession in India:

Law Governs
Hindu Succession Act, 1956 Hindus, Jains, Sikhs and Buddhists
Mohammedan law — uncodified, mainly based on Shariat Muslims
Indian Succession Act, 1925 (ISA) Parsis, Indian Christians, persons married under the Special Marriage Act, 1954

Hook: "HSA = Hindu + JSB" (Jain, Sikh, Buddhist). "ISA = CPS" (Christians, Parsis, Special Marriage Act). Muslims = Shariat.

1.2 Hindu law and 1.3 Muslim law

Hindu law

  • The Hindu Succession Act (HSA) came into force on 17 June 1956; it amended and codified intestate succession among Hindus.
  • Contains the entire law of inheritance for Hindus, Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists.
  • Covers devolution under the two main schools — Mitakshara and Dayabhaga — classes of heirs, devolution on each class, disqualification of heirs and failure of heirs.
  • Textbook: the 2005 Amendment radically changed the law, abolishing the limited estate of Hindu women and giving them absolute rights over self-acquired property (see Discrepancy Box). Details in Chapter 2.

Muslim (Mohammedan) law

  • Uncodified, based on Shariat (commandments of Allah); multi-layered, nuanced, applies differently to different factions; developed over centuries from many sources.
School Basis Sub-schools / law
Sunni The Sunnah — record of the Prophet's teachings Four schools: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafii, Hanbali; follows "Sunni law"
Shia Interpretations by ayatollahs (religious leaders) of the Prophet's teachings Follows "Shia law"
  • Shariat is a code of obligations covering spiritual, religious, legal and social guidance, plus marriage, dower, divorce, maintenance, Wakfs, testamentary and intestate succession.
  • Inheritance rules set out heritable property, classes of heirs, their shares, devolution principles and how they differ across schools. Details in Chapter 2.

Hook: Sunni's four schools = "Hanafi Makes Shafi Happy" → Hanafi, Maliki, Shafii, Hanbali.

1.4 ISA applicability, lex situs and lex domicilii

ISA applicability (textbook):

  • Largely governs succession of Parsis and Indian Christians.
  • Also governs the testamentary succession of Hindus, and persons married under the Special Marriage Act, 1954.
  • Part V deals with intestate succession:
Part V Covers
Chapter II Rules for intestates other than Parsis (largely Indian Christians) — devolution, rights of a widower
Chapter III (Secs 50–56) Special rules for Parsis dying intestate

Two English principles for devolution:

Principle Section Rule (textbook)
Lex situs — law of the place where property is situated Sec 5(1) Immovable property in India of a person dying intestate is governed by Indian law, regardless of domicile at death
Lex domicilii — law of domicile Sec 5(2) Movable property of an intestate is governed by the law of the country of domicile at death

Hook: Situs = Site (land stays where it sits). Domicile = Dweller (movables travel with the person's home).

Example: Dr Kumar, domiciled in the UK at death, dies intestate owning a flat in Pune and shares in an Indian company. The Pune flat follows Indian law (lex situs). The shares (movable) follow UK law, the law of his domicile (lex domicilii).

Exam trap: Domicile ≠ residence. Residence decides income tax (a days test); domicile decides succession (presence + intention).

1.4.3–1.4.5 Domicile
Concept Textbook position
Residence Relevant for income tax; based on a minimum number of days in a country
Domicile The country a person treats as permanent home, or lives in with a substantial connection (Oxford); captures physical presence + intention to stay. Relevant to non-tax matters like succession
How many? Sec 6: only one domicile for succession
Types Domicile of origin, domicile of choice, domicile by operation of law

Domicile of origin (Secs 7–9):

Child Domicile of origin
Legitimate Country where the father was domiciled at the child's birth
Illegitimate Country where the mother was domiciled at the birth
Duration Sec 9: prevails until a new domicile is acquired

Acquiring a new domicile / domicile of choice:

  • Sec 10: a new domicile is acquired by taking up fixed habitation in another country. "Fixed habitation" isn't defined — understood as an intention to acquire a new domicile.
  • Central Bank v Ram Narain (SC): domicile of origin stays even if the person leaves intending never to return, until a domicile is acquired elsewhere.
  • Dr Yogesh Bharadwaj v State of UP (SC): domicile of origin cannot be "shaken off easily"; it continues unless an intention to reside elsewhere permanently is proved.
  • Need both residence and intention to reside permanently or for an unlimited period.
  • Kedar Pandey v Narain Bikram Shah (SC): burden of proof is on the person asserting a domicile of choice.
  • Business connections or owning assets elsewhere do not by themselves prove domicile of choice.

Special mode — Sec 11: a person resident in India for at least one year who wants Indian domicile can submit a written declaration of that desire to the relevant government office.

Domicile by operation of law (Secs 14–18): for dependants whose domicile follows another's — minors, married women, lunatics / persons of unsound mind.

To ascertain domicile: apply these rules and check whether domicile of origin has been overridden by domicile of choice.

Hook: Domicile of choice = "Live there + Love to stay" (residence + intention). Origin is "sticky" — hard to shake off.

Exam trap: An NRI who has lived in Dubai for 20 years but always intended to return to Kerala still has an Indian domicile of origin — long residence alone isn't enough; intention matters, and the burden lies on whoever claims the change.

1.4.6–1.4.7 Consanguinity (kindred) and degrees
  • Part IV of the ISA (consanguinity) does not apply to Hindus, Muslims, Buddhists, Sikhs, Jains or Parsis — each is governed by personal law.
  • Sec 24: consanguinity = the connection of persons descended from the same or a common ancestor.
Type Section Meaning Examples
Lineal Sec 25 One directly descends from the other Parent–child (1st degree), grandparent–grandchild (2nd degree)
Collateral Sec 26 Common ancestor, but neither descends from the other Siblings (common parents), cousins (common grandfather)

Sec 27 rules for succession:

# Rule
(a) No distinction between relatives through the father or mother (same degree)
(b) No distinction between half blood and full blood (same degree)
(c) A child in the womb at the death, later born alive, is treated as already alive

Computing degrees (Schedule I):

Type Method
Lineal — Sec 25(2) Each generation up or down = one degree
Collateral — Sec 26(2) Count up from the deceased to the common ancestor, then down to the relative; one degree per person, up and down

Textbook illustrations: Brothers A and B share father X: A→X (1) + X→B (1) = 2nd degree. Cousins C and D share grandfather Z: C→Z (2) + Z→D (2) = 4th degree.

Worked example (my addition): Uncle (father's brother): deceased → father (1) → grandfather (2) → uncle (3) = 3rd degree. Nephew (brother's son): deceased → father (1) → brother (2) → nephew (3) = 3rd degree. Under Sec 27 they stand in the same degree.

Hook: Collateral degrees = "Climb up, slide down, count the steps."

Exam trap: ISA (Christians) ignores half vs full blood. The HSA prefers full blood over half blood (Sec 18). Same topic, opposite rules.

1.5 Intestate and testamentary succession

Intestate = a person who died without making a will (Oxford). Intestate succession = inheritance law applying when no will is left.

  • Except for Hindus, Muslims, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains, an intestate's assets are distributed under the ISA; for Hindu and Muslim intestates, under personal laws.
  • Sec 30: a person is deemed to die intestate in respect of all property of which he has not made a testamentary disposition capable of taking effect.
Situation Name
No will at all Total intestacy
Will doesn't deal with some assets (or is partly invalid) Partial intestacy — excluded assets pass under intestacy law
Will has a general / residuary (catch-all) bequest Not partial intestacy — all assets are dealt with
  • Intestate succession often causes disputes — distribution is fixed by law, with no flexibility unless sharers agree.

Testamentary succession — a will is the most common instrument. Sec 2(h): a will is the legal declaration of the testator's intention about his property, which he desires to be carried into effect after his death.

Key factors when making a will (textbook):

# Factor
(a) Clear, describing the estate in detail, with unambiguous bequests and alternate bequests if primary ones fail
(b) A residuary provision for anything not specifically dealt with
(c) Naming an executor is not mandatory but advisable
(d) An in terrorem clause — a beneficiary who challenges the will loses all benefits under it; doesn't stop a challenge but deters it

Hook: In terrorem = "In terror of losing it all."

Example: Ramesh's will gives his house to his son and "all my remaining assets" to his wife. A fixed deposit he forgot to mention goes to the wife under the residuary clause — no partial intestacy.

1.6 Wills — general overview

A will is always open to challenge, so meet these conditions:

# Condition Section
(a) Testator of sound mind and age of majority Sec 59
(b) In writing, executed before two witnesses who each attest (textbook: "all in each other's presence") Sec 63
  • Registration and safe custody are not compulsory under the Registration Act, 1908. Even if registered or deposited, there is no presumption of correctness or validity — it can still be challenged (footnote: Sec 18, Registration Act).

Grounds that make a whole will void:

Ground Section
Made by a person of unsound mind or a minor Sec 59
Obtained by fraud, coercion or importunity Sec 61
A privileged will where the testator survives beyond a month (textbook wording) Sec 66(h)
No definite intention — void for uncertainty Sec 89

A void bequest usually doesn't void the whole will. Void bequests:

Void bequest Section

Not expressive of definite intention — uncertainty Sec 89
To a person not in existence at the testator's death Sec 112

Hook (void bequests): "Witness Unclear, Unborn Life, Perpetual, Prior, Impossible, Immoral" → 67, 89, 112–113, 114, 116, 126, 127.

Example: Seema's will leaves Rs. 5 lakh to her neighbour Anil, who also signed as an attesting witness. The bequest to Anil is void (Sec 67), but the rest of the will stands.

Exam trap: "Is a will invalid because it isn't registered?" — No. Registration is optional and gives no presumption of validity.

1.8 Mutation and distribution of the estate

Mutation = change of title ownership of immovable property (plot, flat, house, bungalow) from one person to another on sale or transfer; the new owner gets recorded in the government authority's revenue records.

  • Vital for land. Example: if land is acquired by government and the registry is in A's name but mutation is in B's name, compensation goes to B — B is the owner in the revenue records.
  • Check mutation details periodically — it can reveal illegal transactions on the property.
Mandatory documents — sale Documents — inheritance or will
Sale deed copy Death certificate
Mutation application with court fee stamp Copy of will or succession certificate
Indemnity bond on stamp paper Indemnity bond on stamp paper
Affidavit on stamp paper Affidavit on stamp paper, attested by a notary
Receipt of up-to-date property tax Property tax receipt (in case of POA); copy of POA
Copy of will; receipt for payment registered with sub-registrar; mutation application with court fee stamp

Hook: Registry proves the deal; mutation proves the record. Government pays whoever is in the revenue record.

Exam trap: In the textbook's land-acquisition example, the person in the mutation record (B) gets the compensation, not the person in the registry (A).

2.1 The Hindu Succession Act, 1956 — who it covers

HSA governs the intestate succession of:

# Person
(a) A Hindu by religion in any form, including followers of Prarthana, Brahmo or Arya Samaj
(b) A Sikh or Buddhist (and Jains, per Chapter 1)
(c) Any person who is not a Christian, Muslim, Jew or Parsi by religion
  • Not retrospective — doesn't affect succession of a Hindu who died before 17 June 1956.
  • Excludes property of Hindus married under the Special Marriage Act, 1954 — those cases go to the ISA.

Exam trap: A Hindu married under the Special Marriage Act → intestate succession under ISA, not HSA.

2.1.1 Principle of propinquity
  • HSA succession is based on the Mitakshara school.
  • Propinquity = order of nearness of blood relation — the nearest degree of blood relation is the basis of succession; preference goes to heirs closest to the deceased.
  • Originally, parental property was allocated by right by birth: sons had exclusive right by birth in joint family property; daughters had none. This was the doctrine of survivorship.

Hook: Propinquity = Proximity. "Closest blood takes first."

2.1.3 General rules of succession
Rule Section Textbook position
(a) Full blood preferred over half blood Sec 2(e), Sec 18 Full blood = descended from a common ancestor by the same wife. Half blood = common ancestor but different wives. Uterine blood = common ancestress but different husbands. ("Ancestor" includes father; "ancestress" the mother.) Full-blood heirs are preferred to half-blood heirs
(b) Child in the womb Sec 20 A child in the womb when the father dies inherits as if born before his death; the share is reserved till birth
(c) Simultaneous death Sec 21 If it's impossible to know who died first, the law presumes the younger survived the elder
(d) Two or more heirs Sec 19 Heirs take per capita (each an equal share), not per stirpes; and as tenants in common, not joint tenants
  • Per stirpes ("by representation") = divided by class of heirs, or equally among each branch of the family.
  • Tenants in common → on an heir's death his share goes to his heirs. Joint tenants → it goes to the surviving joint tenants, not heirs.

Hook: Blood types = Full = same Father & same mother; Half = same Father, other mother; Uterine = same Uterus (mother), other father.

Example (Sec 21): Raghav (60) and his son Arjun (30) die together in a car crash; the order of death can't be proved. HSA presumes Arjun, the younger, survived — so Raghav's property first passes into Arjun's estate, and then to Arjun's heirs.

Exam trap: This presumption is an HSA rule. Under the ISA (Chapter 3), when testator and legatee die together with no proof of who died first, the legacy lapses.

2.1.3.1–2.1.3.2 Class I and Class II heirs and their shares

Sec 8 — when a Hindu male dies intestate, property goes by class, each class excluding the next:

Order Class Textbook examples
1 Class I — "preferential heirs" Sons, daughters, widow (Rule 2 also names the mother)
2 Class II (only if no Class I) Brother(s), sister(s) of the deceased
3 Agnates (only if no Class II) Textbook: son's son, father's brother's widow, father's brother's daughter (see Discrepancy Box)
4 Cognates (only if no agnates) Textbook: daughter's children, son's daughter's children (see Discrepancy Box)

Sec 9: Class I heirs inherit simultaneously to the exclusion of all others. Class II has nine entries; an earlier entry excludes a later one (Entry I beats Entry II).

Sec 10 — sharing among Class I heirs (they take together but shares aren't always equal):

Rule Textbook position
Rule 1 The widow (or all widows together) take one share
Rule 2 Each surviving son, daughter and the mother take one share each
Rule 3 The heirs of each pre-deceased son or pre-deceased daughter take one share jointly
Rule 4 Pre-deceased son's branch: shared equally by his widow(s), sons and daughters. Pre-deceased daughter's branch: shared equally by her sons and daughters — her husband is excluded

Sec 11 — Class II: heirs in the same entry take equal shares (brother and sister are both in Entry II → equal).

Read Secs 8–11 with Sec 19: per capita, tenants in common.

Worked example 1 (Rule 1 + 2): Mohan dies intestate leaving a widow, two sons, one daughter and his mother. Estate Rs. 50 lakh. Shares: widow 1 + son 1 + son 1 + daughter 1 + mother 1 = 5 shares → Rs. 10 lakh each.

Worked example 2 (Rules 3 + 4): Suresh leaves a widow, a daughter, and a pre-deceased son survived by that son's widow and two children. Estate Rs. 60 lakh. Shares: widow 1, daughter 1, pre-deceased son's branch 1 = 3 shares of Rs. 20 lakh. The branch's Rs. 20 lakh is split equally among the son's widow and 2 children → Rs. 6.67 lakh each.

Worked example 3 (pre-deceased daughter): If instead the pre-deceased child was a daughter survived by her husband and two children, her branch's share goes only to her two children — her husband gets nothing.

Worked example 4 (Class II): Vikram dies unmarried, no children; his mother has also died. His father is alive and he has a brother and a sister. Father (Class II, Entry I) takes everything; brother and sister (Entry II) get nothing. Had the father also died, the brother and sister would share equally.

Hook: Class I sharing = "Wives share one; each child and Mum get one; a dead child's family gets one between them."

Exam trap: All widows together take one share — not one share each.

2.2 Hindu Undivided Family (HUF) and types of property

HUF — a unique concept of uncodified Hindu law:

  • Persons lineally descended from a common ancestor, including wives and unmarried daughters (textbook).
  • Formed only by a family, not an individual. Common example: husband, wife and children. Comes into existence on its own on marriage; a married couple can be an HUF.
  • Income earned by members is treated as belonging to the family.
  • Sec 2(31), Income Tax Act, 1961: an HUF is a "person", assessed as a separate entity; its income is taxed in its hands.

Constituents:

Role Textbook position
Coparcener Acquires interest by birth, up to three generations from the senior-most male. Has an interest in HUF property and the right to demand partition

A revolutionary change, mainly giving Hindu women rights and liabilities in property and inheritance.

2.4 Muslim personal law

3.4.1 Persons capable of making a will

Sec 59: soundness of mind and age of majority are prerequisites. Soundness of mind = capacity to understand the meaning and consequences of one's actions, especially the disposition of property under a will.

Explanation to Sec 59 Rule
Explanation 2 The deaf, dumb and blind can make a will if they know what they are doing
Explanation 3 An ordinarily insane person can make a will during a lucid interval; later insanity doesn't invalidate a will
Explanation 4 No will while in a state of not knowing what one is doing — includes intoxication and illness

General principles:

  • A will that appears duly signed and attested is presumed made by a person of sound mind unless proved otherwise.
  • The propounder (person offering it for probate) must show by satisfactory evidence that the testator signed it, was of sound and disposing mind at the time, understood the dispositions, and signed of his own free will (Mahesh Kumar v Vinod Kumar, SC 2012).
  • If rational on its face and duly executed, it is presumed valid absent contrary evidence.

Textbook illustrations: X has basic awareness but no competent understanding of his property, who should succeed, or a will's effect — cannot make a valid will. Q has a serious illness but understands her property and makes a rational choice of heirs — her will is valid.

So a testator must (i) understand and recollect the extent of his property, and (ii) understand he is bequeathing it to one or more persons.

Hook: Capacity = "Know what you own, know who gets it, know what you're doing."

3.4.2 Animus testandi and 3.4.3 Lapse of legacy

Animus testandi

  • Animus = intention; testandi = to make a will. Animus testandi = intention to make a will — a basic requirement.
  • A document can meet every formality yet fail if there was no intention that it govern property after death. A mentally disabled person lacks this intention.
  • Courts ask whether the testator intended to convey property by that instrument (Satappa v Shantappa, 2017).

Lapse of legacy

Rule Section Effect
Legatee does not survive the testator Sec 105 Legacy lapses and falls into the residue, unless the will gives it to someone else. To claim for a legatee's representatives, it must be proved he survived the testator
Legacy to two persons jointly, one dies first Sec 106 The survivor takes the whole legacy

Textbook illustrations: - Property to A; A dies before the testator → lapses to residue. - Bequest to B and his children; B dies first → legacy to B and his children lapses. - Legacy to X, and if X dies first, to Z; X dies first → goes to Z. - Testator and legatee die in the same car accident, no evidence who died first → legacy lapses.

Hook: Lapse = "Legatee left first, gift falls back." Fix it with an alternate legatee (the X-then-Z clause).

Exam trap: Under the ISA, a common-accident death with no proof → legacy lapses. Under the HSA (Sec 21), the younger is presumed to survive. Don't mix them.

3.4.4 Bequest to a class and 3.4.5 Rule against perpetuity

Bequest to a described class — Sec 111

  • Where a bequest is simply to a described class, it goes only to those alive at the testator's death.
  • Exception: if the class is described by relationship to a specified person and possession is deferred (e.g., by a prior bequest), it goes to those alive at that later time plus the representatives of any who died after the testator.

Textbook illustrations: - A leaves a garage to "the children of B". B had died earlier, leaving X, Y, Z. Z dies after the will but before A; X and Y survive A → garage to X and Y only, excluding Z's representatives. - A leaves a house to B for life, then equally among C's children. Up to B's death, C has no child → the bequest after B's death is void.

Rule against perpetuity — Sec 114

A bequest is invalid if vesting may be delayed beyond the lifetime of one or more persons living at the testator's death plus the minority of someone in existence at the end of that period who is to take on attaining full age.

  • Applies to any property, movable or immovable; also in Sec 14, Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (limits how far into the future property can be tied up).
  • Does not apply to charitable or religious endowments.

Textbook illustrations: - Fund to A for life, then B for life, then to B's sons who first reach 25. B dies in the testator's lifetime leaving sons — they are alive at the testator's death, and reaching 25 falls within their own lives → valid. - Fund to A for life, then B for life, then divided among B's children who reach 18, failing which to C. Division must happen at the latest 18 years after B's death (B was alive at the testator's death) → all valid.

Hook: Perpetuity limit = "Lives in being + one minority." Charity is exempt — it can last forever.

  4. A statement of circumstances showing the reason or inducement for making the will doesn't make it contingent.
  5. If doubtful, consider the circumstances of execution and its language.

Joint will — Minakshi Ammal v Viswanatha Aiyar (1909): a joint will can validly be made and can take effect after both deaths; if it disposes of joint property after the survivor's death, it can't be proved until the survivor dies.

  • The Evidence Act and Registration Act give registered documents evidentiary value, especially when challenged.

(c) Certified copy given only after death, to an applicant producing the death certificate — not to relatives with malafide intent

4.6 Attestation

"Attest" = to bear witness to a fact. Sec 63(c) requires:

# Requirement
(a) Attested by two or more witnesses
(b) Attestation in the presence of the testator
(c) Each witness saw the testator sign / affix mark, or received his personal acknowledgement of the signature
(d) Witnesses need not be present at the same time; but each must sign in the testator's presence (Punni v Sumer Chand)
  • An attesting witness signs in the testator's presence after seeing execution or receiving personal acknowledgement (Beni Chand v Kamla Kunwar, SC).
  • Must sign animo attestandi — with the intention of attesting.
  • Purpose: an added level of proof of validity.

Proof in court (Sec 63(c) read with Secs 68 and 69, Evidence Act):

Evidence Act Rule (textbook footnote)
Sec 68 A document required by law to be attested can't be used as evidence until at least one attesting witness is called (if alive and capable)
Sec 69 If no attesting witness can be found, prove that one witness's attestation is in his handwriting and the executant's signature is in the executant's handwriting
  • Sec 68 doesn't require examining both witnesses; if one proves the testator's acknowledgement, the other needn't.
  • Witness signatures at the end or elsewhere on the document are enough to show they saw execution / got acknowledgement; they need not state they signed in the testator's presence (Dulhin Ful Kueri v Moti Jharo Kuer).

Hook: "Two witnesses, one at a time is fine — but always in front of the testator." In court, one witness is enough to call.

Exam trap: A beneficiary should not be an attesting witness — the bequest to that witness is void (Sec 67, Chapter 1), though the will survives.

4.8 Amending a will — codicil or new will
  • Advisable to state the will strongly, but the testator isn't bound — he can alter or revoke at any time (Sec 62).

# Method Textbook view
(i) Overwrite the original with fresh signatures of attesting witnesses Allowed, but risks confusion
(ii) Codicil Preferred for changes
(iii) New will Preferred for structural / substantial changes

Codicil:

  • Instrument made in relation to an executed will that explains, alters or adds to it; part of the will (Sec 2(b)).
  • Endorsed on the original or a separate document; executed like a will; doesn't bar further amendments.
  • Should refer to the specific provisions changed.
  • Bhagat Ram v Suresh (SC, AIR 2004 SC 436): the same execution rules apply to codicils; proof must meet the same standard.
  • Registration optional, but if the will is registered, register the codicil too — to support authenticity and reduce litigation.

New will:

  • For structural changes, overall change, substantial new bequests, or after several codicils (an "operational nightmare" for executors, improper distribution, unhappy beneficiaries).
  • A subsequent will is deemed to revoke earlier wills and codicils; only the last valid will operates.
  • No need for an express revocation clause once execution of the new will is proved.
  • Lachho Bibi v Gopi Narain (1901): the onus lies on the propounder to satisfy the court that it is the last will of a capable testator.

Hook: Codicil = Correction slip. New will = New book. Many corrections → write a new book.

6.1 Tenancy-in-common and joint tenancy

Co-ownership = two or more persons with joint title to immovable property. Each co-owner has an inherent right to possession, use and enjoyment, and to dispose of his interest.

Sec 44, Transfer of Property Act (TOPA): when one co-owner transfers his share, the transferee gets the transferor's rights, including joint possession and the right to demand partition. Exception: a transferee of a share in a dwelling house of an undivided family who is not a family member gets no right to joint possession of the house.

Housing societies

  • Co-operative society law is state-specific (e.g., Maharashtra Co-operative Societies Act, 1960 lets a member nominate persons to whom shares and interest pass on death).

6.6 Power of attorney

Powers-of-Attorney Act, 1882: a POA is "any instrument empowering a specified person to act for and in the name of the person executing it" — creates a principal–agent relationship by a legally binding contract.

Why needed: old age, poor health, disability; geographical inaccessibility; lack of expertise; businesses authorising officials for routine compliance — covers property, business, financial transactions and medical care.

Exam trap: A POA holder cannot distribute assets after the principal's death — the POA has ended; the will / succession law takes over.

7.1 Gifts

Sec 122, TOPA: a gift is a voluntary transfer of property inter vivos (between living persons) without consideration. It need not be out of natural love and affection. Secs 123–129 cover gifts, registration, revocation etc.

Point Textbook position
Effect Transfers the entire ownership; donor gives up title completely
Reservations For immovable property, the donor may keep reasonable fetters for life — restrictions on alienation, a life interest / right of residence for himself and family
Parties Donor (giver) and donee (recipient) execute an instrument of gift
Immovable property Textbook: if above Rs. 100, the gift instrument must be compulsorily registered, attested by two witnesses, and record the donee's acceptance (see Discrepancy Box)
Movable property Can be by delivery; advisable in writing (registration optional); acceptance needed
Acceptance Express or implied, during the donor's lifetime and while he is capable of giving. If the donee dies before accepting, the gift is void

Other matters:

# Rule
(a) A gift by a minor is void (can't contract). A natural guardian can accept a gift to a minor, on condition that the person named in the deed manages the property
(b) A gift can't be revoked once made, except under Sec 126 TOPA
(c) Mohammedan law allows, with conditions, an oral gift of immovable property — "Hiba"

Sec 126 TOPA (footnote): donor and donee may agree that a gift is suspended or revoked on a specified event not depending on the donor's will; a gift revocable at the donor's mere will is void (wholly or partly); a gift may also be revoked where a contract could be rescinded (except for want of consideration); rights of bona fide transferees for consideration are protected.

Hook: Valid gift = "Voluntary, Free, Accepted, Alive" — voluntary, no consideration, accepted, while the donor is alive.

Exam trap: A gift deed saying "I may take the flat back whenever I wish" is void — revocation can't depend on the donor's mere will.

7.1.1 Inheritance tax and 7.1.2 Tax on gifts

Inheritance tax

  • Inheritance tax is levied on assets received by heirs (testamentary or intestate).
  • India's version, Estate Duty, was abolished in 1985. There is no inheritance tax in India at present.

Tax on gifts (Income Tax Act)

Rule Textbook position
Threshold Money or property received by an individual or HUF from unrelated persons over Rs. 50,000 in a financial year is income
Aggregation If all gifts together exceed Rs. 50,000, the whole aggregate is taxable — no threshold exemption
Death-related Money received in contemplation of death of an individual or Karta / member of an HUF is exempt; inheritance / willed assets are tax-free
Relatives Gifts from specified relatives are exempt without limit — parents, children, spouse, siblings, spouse's siblings, lineal ascendants and descendants of the person and spouse, and spouses of these persons
Who pays Taxed in the recipient's hands under "Income from Other Sources"; no tax on the donor
Section Sec 56(2); "gift" = money, movable or immovable property received without consideration

Textbook case (Smt Geeta Dubey v ITO, ITAT Indore 2018): She received Rs. 50,000 from her father and Rs. 50,000 from her sister-in-law via banking channels. The AO accepted both as "relatives" but taxed them because she didn't state the occasion. ITAT held: once identity and relationship are established, proving the occasion is not required — exempt.

Worked example (my numbers): In FY 2025-26, Priya receives Rs. 30,000 from a friend, Rs. 25,000 from a colleague and Rs. 2 lakh from her brother. Non-relative gifts = 30,000 + 25,000 = Rs. 55,000 > Rs. 50,000 → the entire Rs. 55,000 is taxable. Brother's Rs. 2 lakh is exempt (sibling). Had the colleague given Rs. 15,000 (total Rs. 45,000), nothing would be taxable.

Hook: "Fifty-thousand cliff" — cross it by one rupee and the whole amount is taxed.

Exam trap: A gift from a cousin is not exempt — cousins aren't in the relatives list.

7.1.2.1 Fair market value and 7.1.2.2 Stamp duty

Fair market value (FMV) — movable property

  • FMV = price between a willing buyer and willing seller, neither under compulsion, both reasonably informed.
  • Sec 2(22B), IT Act: "the price that the capital asset would ordinarily fetch on sale in the open market on the relevant date." Important for working out tax on gifts.

Rule 11UAB (from 1 April 2019, prospective) — FMV of inventory converted into / treated as a capital asset (for Sec 28):

Asset FMV basis
Immovable property (land / building) Value adopted or assessable by a government authority for stamp duty on the conversion date
Jewellery, archaeological collections, drawings, paintings, sculptures, art, shares / securities (Rule 11UA) Value under Rule 11UA(1), valuation date = conversion date
Other property Price it would ordinarily fetch in the open market on the conversion date

Stamp duty — immovable property

Point Textbook position
Law Indian Stamp Act, 1899 (federal); stamp duty is a State subject — states have their own Acts or adopt the 1899 Act with amendments
Charged on Instruments of transfer, usually ad valorem (market value)
Rate factors Vary by state; nature of property, development potential, user, age and location, amenities; concessions for some persons and properties
Nature A tax on the instrument, not the transaction or property
Unstamped instrument Inadmissible as evidence; an unstamped, unregistered transfer instrument doesn't pass title
Gift deed A gift of immovable property attracts stamp duty; rates vary by state; an unstamped gift instrument won't be registered, and an unregistered gift is void

Hook: Stamp duty taxes the paper, not the property. No stamp → no registration → no title.

7.2 The Indian Trusts Act, 1882 — definitions
  • The Indian Trusts Act (ITA) is the principal law for private trusts: creation, types, parties, rights and liabilities of trustees and beneficiaries, a valid trust deed.
  • Does not apply to waqfs and Muslim religious / charitable endowments, or to "mutual relations of members of an undivided family as determined by customary or personal law". Some states have laws for charitable and religious trusts (not private trusts).
  • Sec 3: a trust is "an obligation annexed to the ownership of property, and arising out of a confidence reposed in and accepted by the owner, or declared and accepted by him, for the benefit of another, or of another and the owner." Simply: property of one is held by another for a beneficiary.
Party / term Textbook definition
Author / settlor Creates the trust of property owned by him; decides mode, purpose, trustees, beneficiaries, administration; transfers the initial property (corpus) to trustees
Trustee Holds and manages trust property for beneficiaries; anyone capable of contracting (major, sound mind, not disqualified); can be a person or entity (corporation, firm, bank); professional trustees exist. Acts in a fiduciary capacity, holds legal title, not for his own benefit; duties in Chapter III
Protector Appointed (or the settlor himself) to keep oversight without being a trustee — powers may include removing / replacing trustees and approving disposal, investment or income application. Trustees remain the primary functionaries and alone hold the property
Beneficiary Person for whom the trust is created, usually receives income; any person capable of holding property — including minors, disabled persons, unborn children, bodies and artificial entities. Rights and liabilities in Chapter VI
Trust property Corpus / trust fund — plus accretions and income if the deed provides; can be added to by the settlor or others if the deed allows. In an irrevocable trust the settlor gives up title and control
Trust deed Sec 3: the instrument by which the trust is declared — trustee powers and duties, investment and application of property and income, distributions, successor beneficiaries, appointment / removal of trustees, termination date and final distribution

Hook: Trust triangle = "Settlor gives, Trustee holds, Beneficiary enjoys"; the Protector watches.

Exam trap: Only an irrevocable trust ring-fences assets from the settlor's creditors and keeps them out of his estate.

7.3 Advantages of private trusts and 7.4 the HUF exception

Trusts help plan succession, avoid will disputes, ring-fence assets, plan tax and hedge against a possible future inheritance tax.

# Advantage Textbook explanation
7.3.1 Planning succession Assets settled during life (irrevocable trust) are not part of the estate at death → avoids disputes, litigation and long tie-ups; settlor can settle only dispute-prone assets
7.3.2 Ring-fencing Irrevocable trust can shield assets from tax and creditors' claims. But a trust created mainly to defeat government or creditors can be challenged, voided and attached (incl. under bankruptcy law)
7.3.3 Tax planning Generate wealth / income for beneficiaries; divest assets ahead of a possible inheritance tax

7.3.6 Transparency Trustees bound by the deed — Sec 11 (fulfil trust purposes, follow settlor's directions), Sec 17 (act impartially for all beneficiaries); beneficiaries have remedies
7.3.7 Strategic objectives Even in an irrevocable trust, the settlor can keep effective control via the deed and a protector (possibly himself)
7.3.8 Pass-through Trusts pool investments; income passes to beneficiaries and is taxed in their hands. Hook: Trust advantages = "Succession, Shield, Save tax, Special needs, Stretch (flexible), See-through (transparent), Strategy, Straight-through (pass-through)" — 8 S's.

Exam trap: A Karta can't settle HUF property into a private trust as if it were his own.

7.5–7.6 Succession planning for businesses

Small businesses

  • A sustainable succession plan is essential for small and especially small family businesses.
  • If the business is a company, a non-testamentary trust can hold its shares: collect and distribute dividends among family beneficiaries, oversee management, retain control over investment, expansion and divestment decisions; trustees act for all beneficiaries — avoiding disputes among a disparate group of shareholders that could hurt operations and value.

Business succession

  • Credit Suisse (2018): India had the third-largest number of family-owned businesses — 111 companies, total market cap USD 839 billion. Promoters often hold controlling stakes; relatives fill prime managerial posts.
  • A comprehensive plan enables smooth inter-generational transfer, reduces conflict, ensures continuity and preserves wealth.
  • Can be coupled with a trust: appoint successors / trustees (possibly family), set guidelines for operations and distribution of income / dividends, retain management control.
A business succession plan can (textbook)
Separate members' personal and professional responsibilities
Define professional roles and duties
Demarcate ownership interests / shares
Ensure transparent functioning and reporting of decisions
Set up an efficient dispute resolution mechanism
Provide independent expert advice, including through a trust

Intellectual property (IPR): disputes are common when family businesses split. A family trust holding the IPR, with family members as beneficiaries, can apportion royalties, set non-compete agreements and allow concurrent use (same or different classes of goods / services).

Example: Two brothers who inherited a pickle brand split the business. A family trust owns the trademark, licences it to both companies for different product lines, and divides royalties equally — no fight over who owns the brand.

7.7 Offshore trusts
  • With HNIs holding assets in India and abroad, cross-border tax and regulatory effects matter. RBI, through FEMA, 1999 and exchange control regulations, controls cross-border fund transfers and regulates trust parties by residential status.
  • Offshore trust (Indian context) = a trust formed outside India by an Indian resident — subject to the foreign jurisdiction's law plus Indian exchange control and tax law; also consider the PMLA, 2002 and the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015.
What a resident may hold abroad (FEMA) Funding route
Foreign currency, securities, immovable property acquired while resident outside India, or inherited from a person resident outside India Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) — up to USD 250,000 per person per financial year without RBI permission
  • Choice of structure depends on residency of beneficiaries and location of assets, aiming for tax efficiency in India and abroad.

Hook: Offshore trust laws = "FEMA + Income tax + PMLA + Black Money Act" — four Indian laws follow the money abroad.

7.8 Family arrangements

A family arrangement / settlement = a vehicle families use to agree and effect distribution and transfer of businesses and assets among members. It is effectively a contract — immediate transfers and / or future ones (a date, a period, or an event like a member's death).

  • No one size fits all. Prime motive: settle existing disputes or avoid future ones, especially as later generations arrive.
  • May involve all or some members and all or some assets.
