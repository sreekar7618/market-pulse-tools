Introduction to the Regulatory Environment

What financial regulation is for, who the regulators are, and how the EU (MiFID II / ESMA) codified advisor competence

Regulation exists to fix the three ways financial markets fail on their own: abuse of market power, ordinary people who can't judge complex products, and one firm's collapse spilling over onto everyone else.

Learning objectives 1-1 to 1-4: explain how regulation relates to financial services; describe regulators and their functions; know the regulatory, economic and political environments; discuss the impact of compliance on advice.

1.1 What is financial regulation?

Core idea

Financial regulation is the set of laws and rules that govern what financial institutions (banks, brokers, investment firms, insurers) may do. It is issued by government regulators or international bodies to protect investors, keep markets orderly and promote financial stability. In practice it covers minimum standards for capital and conduct, regular inspections, and investigating and prosecuting misconduct.

The textbook opens with Brunnermeier's (2009) three classic purposes of regulation. Learn them as three market failures:

PurposeMarket failure it fixesEveryday example
1. Constrain monopoly power, prevent distortions to competition, maintain market integrityA dominant player can rig prices or manipulate a thin marketRules against insider trading and price manipulation; merger control
2. Protect the essential needs of ordinary people where information is hard or costly to obtain and mistakes could devastate welfareInformation asymmetry: the seller knows far more than the buyerMandatory disclosure of fees and risks before a retail investor buys a product
3. Act where externalities are large enough that the social cost of failure exceeds the private cost plus the cost of regulatingOne bank's failure triggers a run on healthy banks (systemic risk)Capital requirements, deposit insurance, lender of last resort

Example

A small saver puts ₹8 lakh in a fixed deposit. She cannot read the bank's balance sheet, has no way to know whether it is over-lending to real estate, and if the bank fails she loses her savings. Purpose 2 (protect people who can't evaluate the product) and Purpose 3 (a bank failure hurts everyone, not just the bank) both apply, so regulators impose capital rules and deposit insurance rather than leaving it to the market.

1.2 The IMF three-way classification: Economic, Safety, Information

The textbook leans on an IMF paper (White, 1999) that sorts all regulation into three buckets. This is a favourite review question, so learn the labels and what each bucket is for.

Economic regulation

The starting point is "if it isn't broken, don't fix it". Competitive markets have good "welfare properties": the invisible hand guides prices, wages and interest rates through millions of individual decisions, and resources end up allocated efficiently. So economic regulation should be light: keep the sector open to entrants and new products, let better ideas and more efficient firms win. If there is a safety or information problem, fix it with a safety or information rule, not by restricting competition.

Safety (prudential) regulation

Prudential regulation requires financial firms to control risks and hold adequate capital. It should be vigorous for banks, insurers and defined-benefit pension plans, because widespread insolvency among these destroys public confidence. Information regulation

Timely, periodic disclosure by listed companies is the "lubricant" of securities markets. Information should be standardised (same accounting rules) so investors can compare companies. Like prudential rules, it only works if enforced by a well-resourced regulator. Regulation, in the end, needs two things: laws (legislation and regulations) and regulators to oversee, explain and comment on them.

Memory hook

E-S-I: Economic = "don't interfere unless broken"; Safety = "capital and control"; Information = "disclose and standardise". Review question 1 asks for exactly these three words.

1.4 Legislation and regulation: the post-2008 reset

Core idea

Most current financial regulation is a direct response to the 2007–2008 global financial crisis. There was regulation before; it simply failed to prevent the crisis, so it was reworked, amended and added to. The aim of regulation is not to punish the industry but to support territorial and global economic well-being and growth.

Three post-crisis themes run through everything else in the course:

  • Safety now means the whole system, not just individual investors: banks must hold adequate capital (Basel III).
  • Accurate, clear accounting so regulators can verify capital is maintained and risk is managed.
  • Transparency: timely, accurate financial reports written clearly and distributed openly, including to regulators.

The firm-level rules (V.IV) you should be able to reproduce:

  • Clearly separate the roles of giving advice and giving information in job descriptions.
  • Assess staff through an appropriate qualification and appropriate experience.
  • Review, at least annually, staff development needs and regulatory changes; require continuous professional development and product-specific training before new products are offered.
  • Submit competence records to the Competent Authority (CA) on request.
  • Staff without the necessary knowledge and competence cannot provide the service; staff lacking the qualification or experience may work only under supervision, with intensity matched to the gap (including sitting in on meetings, calls and emails). Example

Priya joins an advisory firm with a relevant degree but no client experience. Under V.IV she may meet clients, but a qualified supervisor sits in on her first meetings, reviews her emails and signs each suitability report as though it were her own work. The firm logs Priya's qualification, her supervised hours and its annual review, because the regulator can ask for the file. If Priya has not become fully competent within four years, she must stop giving advice.

India anchor

The Indian version of "appropriate qualification" is the NISM X-A and X-B certifications you cleared, plus the experience and (for individuals) the PG qualification requirements in the IA Regulations; the annual review idea shows up as continuing professional education requirements for certifications. The information-vs-advice distinction maps to mutual fund distributor (NISM V-A, product information and "appropriateness") versus investment adviser (advice and suitability).

EIOPA (insurance and pensions in the EU)

The insurance twin of ESMA. Its five goals: better protect consumers and rebuild trust; ensure high, effective and consistent regulation and supervision across member states; greater harmonisation of rules; stronger oversight of cross-border groups; promote a coordinated EU supervisory response.

Where FPSB fits

Beyond governments, most territories have non-governmental or quasi-governmental bodies that raise standards among members. FPSB is one: it develops high-level professional standards for financial planners through its member organisations. This is where the Code of Ethics (covered in the Personal and Professional Ethics course) comes from.

Exam traps for Chapter 1

  • IOSCO = securities; IAIS = insurance; EIOPA = EU insurance and pensions; ESMA = EU securities. Don't swap them.
  • IOSCO principles are endorsed by the G20 and the FSB, and used by the IMF/World Bank in FSAPs. Three separate bodies; keep them straight.
  • MiFID II: adopted May 2014, in force January 2018. ESMA Guidelines: published 2015, effective 3 January 2017. Supervision cap: four years.
  • The "underlying desire of regulators" (review question 2) is to keep the territorial and global economy thriving and growing, not to punish firms.

Legislated "Client Best Interest" Requirement

The fiduciary standard, and why it is a higher bar than mere suitability

Everyone agrees the client comes first. The whole chapter is about what that actually means when two products both "fit" the client but only one pays you.

Learning objective 2-1: describe the legal framework within which financial advisors operate, including their legal, social and ethical responsibilities.

2.1 Client first: three sources, one principle

The textbook shows that the "client first" idea appears in three places at once, which is why it feels uncontroversial:

SourceWhat it says
MiFID II, amplified by the ESMA GuidelinesFirms and advisors must act in the best interest of the client (ESMA p. 35) and select products best suited to the client profile (ESMA p. 42)
FPSB Code of Ethics and Professional ResponsibilityPrinciple 1: place the client's interest first
MiFID's description of good adviceAdvice should be fair, transparent, efficient and integrated

The difficulty is not the principle but its application. Is it enough that a recommendation is "suitable" (meets the client's stated needs and objectives), or must the advisor meet the deeper "client first" standard, which in Anglo-Saxon countries is called a fiduciary (or fiduciary-like) standard?

2.2 The fiduciary standard

Core idea

A fiduciary relationship is one of trust, confidence and reliance: the client relies on the advisor's discretion and expertise, and the advisor knowingly accepts that trust. At its core, being a fiduciary means acting with undivided loyalty to the client, in other words, in the client's best interest.

One professional body breaks fiduciary duty into three requirements, each with a test question:

RequirementWhat it meansThe question to ask
TrustA client who does not fully trust the advisor can never be confident they are getting the best adviceWithout trust, can client confidence be achieved?
LoyaltyA loyal advisor is not swayed by outside forces to recommend higher-commission productsWithout loyalty, can clients be sure their interests are looked after?
DisclosureClients must know and understand how the advisor is paid and whether conflicts exist that could compromise independenceWithout disclosure, can the advisor provide prudent advice?

Memory hook

T-L-D: Trust, Loyalty, Disclosure. Chapter 2's only review question asks you to "describe the concept of operating as a fiduciary" and the model answer is: undivided loyalty, act in the client's best interest, three requirements of trust, loyalty and disclosure.

The distinction becomes visible only when there is a conflict of interest. The textbook's own scenario is worth memorising because exam questions tend to be built on it.

Worked example

Ramesh, 38, wants ₹10 lakh in a diversified large-cap equity fund for a 10-year goal. Two options fit: a regular plan (expense ratio ~1.8%, trail commission to the distributor) and a direct plan of the same scheme (expense ratio ~0.9%, no commission). Both are suitable: same underlying portfolio, same risk, same horizon fit. Under suitability alone, recommending the regular plan is defensible. Under a fiduciary standard the direct plan must be recommended, because it delivers the same benefit at lower cost. Roughly, 0.9% a year saved on ₹10 lakh growing at 12% is over ₹2 lakh of extra terminal wealth after 10 years (the difference between compounding at 11.1% and 10.2%: about ₹28.6 lakh vs ₹26.4 lakh). That gap is the economic meaning of "all else being equal, choose the lower-cost option".

India anchor

India legislates both standards side by side. Regulation 15(1) of the SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations 2013 says an IA "shall act in a fiduciary capacity towards its clients". A mutual fund distributor (your NISM V-A track) operates on appropriateness/suitability and earns trail commission, which is exactly why the regulator does not let one person be both adviser and distributor to the same client. The textbook's US example (ERISA for retirement pensions, with fines and imprisonment for breach) plays the same role that Regulation 15 plays here: a specific, legally enforceable fiduciary duty.

2.4 When the law is unclear: how to behave anyway

Even where "fiduciary" is a legal term, people disagree about applying it, and the word "legal" itself distorts behaviour because interpretations and common practice differ. The textbook's guidance:

  • Know your territory's law. If you are under a specific fiduciary regime (like ERISA in the US), be clear about it and comply.
  • Where the law is unclear, choose to put the client first anyway.
  • Start from "first, do no harm" (primum non nocere, borrowed from medicine): abstain from what harms the client and do what benefits them. This is the foundation; beyond it, application gets harder.
  • Use the firm's management and compliance specialists when a question arises. Shared wisdom illuminates a path forward.
  • Document client meetings and decisions. Documentation shows the advisor's intent, and the client's agreement, to put the client first.

Embracing the principles-based client-first standard is described as moving "a long way on the path toward greater professionalism". The Personal and Professional Ethics course goes deeper.

Exam traps for Chapter 2

  • Suitability does not require choosing the lower-cost option; fiduciary does (all else equal). This is the single most testable sentence in the chapter.
  • Rules-based → suitability; principles-based → client first. Not the other way round.
  • Fiduciary is the greater standard; suitability the lesser. Both aim at the client's interest; the difference is "subtle but significant".
  • ERISA is a US retirement-advice law with fiduciary requirements; it is the textbook's example of a specific legal regime, not a global standard.

Chapter 2 review question

1. Whether or not your territory recognises the legal concept of fiduciary, how would you describe the concept of operating as a fiduciary?Review Q1

A fiduciary acts with undivided loyalty to the client, in the client's best interest. The relationship involves trust, confidence and reliance on the advisor's expertise and discretion, which the advisor knowingly accepts. It rests on three requirements: trust (the client can be confident they are getting the best advice), loyalty (the advisor is not swayed by commissions or outside incentives) and disclosure (the client knows how the advisor is paid and what conflicts exist). In practice this means choosing the option that is best for the client even when another suitable option pays the advisor more, and fully disclosing any conflict before the client decides.

3.1 Monetary policy versus fiscal policy

Core idea

Governments have two levers to steer the economy. Monetary policy belongs to the central bank and works through money supply and interest rates. Fiscal policy belongs to the government's budget and works through taxation and spending. Both try to speed up or slow down growth.

Suppose a bank has ₹100 crore of deposits and the reserve requirement is 4%. It must hold ₹4 crore idle and can lend ₹96 crore. 3.2 Economics 101: scarcity

Economics is the study of how people, businesses and governments deal with scarcity: human wants exceed what can be produced (Robinson). Lionel Robbins' refinement: economics is "the study of the use of scarce resources which have alternative uses", so it is really about the decisions made and their consequences. Financial advice is economics in miniature: the advisor helps clients meet life goals with scarce resources that have alternative uses. The textbook's list of planning questions:

  • Which goals are achievable, and in what order?
  • What resources can be applied to each goal?
  • If some goals are unachievable, which is most important?
  • How much of a resource should go to a given goal or need?
  • Can one resource serve more than one goal?
  • Can resources be increased or liabilities reduced?

Three cautions the textbook stresses:

  • Data gets revised. Governments release projections before all data is compiled; statisticians later adjust. So never base big decisions on one report; look at a series.
  • Some sources are unreliable, sometimes for political reasons. Multiple sources help, but bad data is bad regardless of how much research you do.
  • An indicator's value depends on the cycle. In a recession nobody cares about inflation data (people have less money, businesses can't raise prices); in a boom inflation is on everyone's mind. Recession plus inflation can occur when resources become scarce.

Example

Advance orders for durable goods are a forward-looking indicator. If factories stop receiving orders, sellers are sitting on inventory or seeing no demand, which points to a slowdown. A sharp rise in orders points to a robust sales environment. In India, the closest everyday read is the manufacturing PMI's "new orders" component.

3.4 The business cycle

Core idea

The business cycle is a recurring short-term pattern: growth, then weakness and downturn (recession), then recovery and renewed growth. It is measured by output (real GDP) and unemployment (the share of the labour force without jobs). In the long run these have normal levels, called potential output and the natural rate of unemployment; the cycle is the short-term wobble around them.

Phase 1 — Expansion

GDP has four spending areas (Ip): consumer spending and housing (consumption; the largest component in wealthy territories), business investment (factories, equipment, other businesses; long-term), government spending (varies hugely by policy; Keynesian stimulus is hard to switch off) and exports (really net exports: exports minus imports; a trade deficit means living beyond one's means). In an expansion all four grow, largely because confidence grows: consumers and firms spend, tax receipts rise, governments spend more, exports may rise. Gradual, sustainable expansion is healthy; too-fast expansion is a boom (rapid growth, higher GDP, lower unemployment, rising asset prices, and eventually inflation because demand outruns supply).

Bubbles

A boom can become a bubble: confidence skyrockets and people pay ever-higher prices assuming the market will keep rising (it never does; markets always rise and fall). Bubbles occur most often in investments, housing being the classic case. When they pop, prices drop precipitously; in the mid-2000s US housing bubble many areas fell about 30% from peak to trough.

Example from the text: Dutch tulip mania (1600s)

One tulip variety, Admiral Liefken, was valued at 4,400 florins. For scale: four fat oxen were worth 480 florins, 1,000 pounds of cheese 120 florins, a suit of clothes 80 florins. So one bulb was worth about nine teams of oxen or 55 suits. In 1636 the Dutch exchange opened a tulip division (it spread to London and Paris); gambling became rampant, the bubble burst and speculators lost everything. Two lessons: bubbles are hard to identify while you are in one, and hard to bring under control once identified.

India anchor

The 1992 Harshad Mehta episode and the 2007–08 run-up (the Sensex fell by more than half from its January 2008 peak to March 2009) are the Indian versions of the same story: prices detached from fundamentals, then a precipitous fall. Use them when a client says "it can only go up".

Phase 2 — Downturn and contraction

Every expansion is eventually followed by contraction. The mechanism, step by step:

  1. Expansion brings inflation; suppliers raise prices because they can. Unemployment is low, so employees demand and get higher wages; employers recover this through still-higher prices.
  2. Prices reach a level consumers decide is unjustified; they delay or cancel purchases.
  3. Inventories build; businesses cut orders; factories cut production.
  4. Layoffs begin; the pool of workers grows; employers cut wages and benefits.
  5. Lower wages mean less discretionary spending; fewer people buy fewer goods.
  6. Sellers cut prices; profits fall; some firms close; lower corporate values hit the stock market, souring sentiment further.

Government can also cause a slowdown, through two interrelated factors: (1) a decrease in government spending (a debt-loaded government must eventually cut back; if the economy was propped up by that spending, it contracts) and (2) central bank intervention to adjust money supply and rates.

India anchor

India's FY2020-21 pandemic year is a clean example of a two-consecutive-quarter contraction (April–June and July–September 2020 both showed negative real GDP growth), followed by a sharp recovery; full-year real GDP fell in the region of 6%. Phase 4 — Recovery

Demand for goods and services increases; people become optimistic and make delayed purchases. Prices are still low but not for long, because supplies are low after a recession. Production rises slowly, then hiring, then wages (starting low). More money means more demand, so prices rise until supply catches up; consumers accept this because they are working and optimistic. Stock market returns usually grow in this phase. Recovery leads seamlessly into expansion and the cycle repeats.

3.6 Economics overview: two economic systems and price controls

The key idea about prices: scarcity determines price, not the other way round. Beachfront homes are expensive because there are few of them and many want them; computers are cheap despite huge demand because supply is enormous. Prices guide both consumers and producers.

Price controls sabotage this. Diamonds (supply restricted by mining and distribution firms keeps prices high), agricultural price supports (produce stored rather than distributed because full distribution would lower prices), and government policies paying farmers not to grow crops are the textbook examples. Controls often create artificial shortages, public frustration and black markets.

The Phillips Curve, worked through

The 1960s explanation of why a fiscal stimulus raises inflation (learn the sequence):

  1. Government spending generates growth, so demand for labour rises.
  2. The pool of unemployed shrinks.
  3. Firms compete for fewer workers by raising nominal wages.
  4. Workers gain bargaining power and seek further wage rises.
  5. Wage costs rise.
  6. Firms pass the higher costs on as higher prices.

Policymakers in the 1960s–70s believed they could exploit this trade-off, picking a target inflation rate and contracting the economy to reach it. The example: inflation at 6% with unemployment at 3%; the government cuts money supply to bring inflation to the 2% target; unemployment rises to 5% (point A). Reality later showed the trade-off is not stable, which is why every school has been revised.

What causes recessions (modern view)

Beyond supply shocks and government action, a fall in aggregate demand can come from: higher interest rates (less borrowing and investment); falling real wages; falling consumer confidence; a credit crunch (banks lend less, so less investment); deflation (people delay spending; the real value of debt rises, so debtors are worse off); currency appreciation (exports become expensive, demand for them falls). In a recession prices fall as businesses discount to protect profits (a leftward shift of the demand curve lowers equilibrium price and quantity), but essentials like bread fall far less; the price cut depends on how far each product's demand curve shifts.

Exam traps for Chapter 3

  • To increase money supply the central bank buys bonds, cuts the discount rate and lowers reserve requirements. If you can remember "buy = give cash", the other two follow.
  • Discretionary vs automatic stabilisation are the two approaches to fiscal policy; expansionary vs contractionary are labels for direction. Fiscal drag = progressive tax; fiscal boost = lower tax.
  • Four business-cycle stages (review Q4): expansion, downturn, recession/depression, recovery. Not "peak" and "trough" (those are turning points on the figure).
  • Say's Law belongs to a classical economist (Say), and the text says history disproved it. Friedman is monetarism, between the camps, not Keynesian.
  • Depression thresholds (Ip): 10% contraction or three years (or more than two).

Chapter 3 review questions

1. How do monetary and fiscal policy differ? What are the primary tools or approaches of each?Review Q1

Monetary policy is run by the central bank; fiscal policy by the government through its budget. Monetary tools: open market operations (buying government bonds injects money; selling withdraws it), the discount rate (raise to tighten, cut to ease) and reserve requirements (raise to cut money supply, lower to expand it). Fiscal policy has two approaches: discretionary (a one-off policy change) and automatic stabilisation (fiscal drag from progressive taxation, or fiscal boost from lower tax rates), plus government spending decisions.

2. How would you describe the two major types of economic systems?Review Q2

Government-controlled and market-controlled. The economic difference is who controls production and consumption. In a government-controlled system the state decides what is produced, in what quantity and with what availability, usually without good demand and cost data, so shortages and surpluses recur. In a market-controlled system households and firms decide, guided by prices that reflect supply and demand.

3. Your territory's economy has become weaker than normal. What might be causing it, and which indicators should you research?Review Q3

Research the key indicator families: employment, consumer spending and sentiment, GDP (production and inventories), housing, central bank actions, foreign trade, and wages and prices; add the leading indicators (new orders, permits, initial jobless claims, the yield-curve spread) to see what is coming. Likely causes: the economy is in the downward part of the business cycle (goods not being made, sold or bought), or government intervention (spending cuts, central bank tightening) is temporarily holding back development. Other demand-side causes: higher rates, falling real wages or confidence, a credit crunch, deflation, or currency appreciation hurting exports.

4. What are the primary stages of the business cycle?Review Q4

Expansion; downturn (contraction); recession or depression; recovery. Recovery flows into the next expansion.

Social and Political Environments

How government sentiment, regional blocs, global trade, welfare, tax and retirement policy shape a client's plan

Governments change, treaties change, benefits change. A plan that assumes the current political and economic environment will simply continue is a plan with a hidden risk in it.

4.3 Global economic environment: trade

"When one territory sneezes, another catches cold." Imports bring goods in and send money out; exports do the reverse. A trade deficit (imports exceed exports) means more money flows out than in. Short-term deficits are not necessarily bad (people enjoy cheaper goods), but extended or growing deficits cause job losses, rising national debt and a large stock of the currency in foreign hands, so trading partners try to keep some balance.

Worked example: comparative advantage

Territory X can make 10 phones or 5 computers with the same resources; Territory Y can make 4 phones or 4 computers. X is better at both (absolute advantage in both). But X's opportunity cost of one computer is 2 phones, while Y's is 1 phone. Y gives up less to make computers, so Y should make computers and X should make phones, then trade. Both end up with more than if each made both. That is the whole logic of comparative advantage in one line: specialise where your opportunity cost is lowest.

Text example: currency risk wipes out a gain (with a flag)

The textbook says: you live in territory A, invest in territory B, the investment rises 10%, but B's currency "appreciates against your territory's currency by 10%" and you realise no gain. Taken literally that is backwards: if B's currency strengthens, an investor from A gets more home currency back. The point the textbook is making is correct, the direction is mis-stated: a 10% currency move against you cancels a 10% gain in the asset. Concretely: ₹1,00,000 converted at ₹80/$ buys $1,250; a US stock rises 10% to $1,375; if the rupee has meanwhile strengthened to about ₹72.7/$, converting back gives ₹1,00,000, so zero gain despite the stock rising. What hurts a rupee investor abroad is the rupee strengthening; what hurts a foreign investor in India is the rupee weakening. If an exam question repeats the textbook's wording, answer with the textbook's conclusion (no gain), not the mechanics.

India anchor

The rupee is a managed float: market-determined, but RBI intervenes to smooth volatility. Clients investing abroad under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) take on currency risk; historically the rupee has depreciated against the dollar over long periods, which has added to rupee returns from US assets, but that is a historical pattern, not a guarantee.

4.5 Social welfare policy

Core idea

Social welfare is organised public or private assistance for disadvantaged groups, and more broadly the quality of life (drug abuse, environmental quality, crime, availability of essential services). People disagree about whether it is welcome, but it undeniably costs money and generally raises taxes. For the advisor, what matters is that welfare programmes affect clients' financial well-being, so you should know the range of benefits available and keep a directory of organisations that provide services.

Text case: Diego and Maria — how a comfortable family ends up needing the safety netChapter 4 example

Diego and Maria are professionals with good incomes, married 20 years, three children. Their oldest, Tomas, develops serious behavioural difficulties. A chain of events follows: their health insurance does not cover the mental health provider they need; therapy and counselling are not covered (medication is); finding help consumes so much of Maria's time that she gives up her career; residential care is recommended, costing half of Diego's income and again not covered; they borrow, then run out of lenders; Maria's own health collapses. They are close to losing their home.

In some societies that is the end of the story. Where a social welfare safety net exists, benefits for income, food, clothing and Tomas's care become a bridge back to stability. The teaching point: if their financial advisor had known about the available programmes, she could have guided them much earlier. Knowing the welfare landscape is part of the job.

India anchor

An Indian advisor's "directory" would include Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY (hospitalisation cover for eligible families), state health schemes, EPF/EPS benefits, Atal Pension Yojana, PM-KISAN for farming households, disability pensions, and the tax deduction for dependants with disability under Section 80DD. Most middle-class clients have never mapped these against their own contingencies.

Worked example: tax wedge

An employer's total cost for a worker is ₹12,00,000 a year. The employer's PF/social contributions are ₹1,00,000, the employee's own contributions ₹1,00,000, and income tax ₹1,20,000. Total taxes and contributions = ₹3,20,000. Tax wedge = 3,20,000 / 12,00,000 = 26.7%. The worker takes home ₹8,80,000 while the employer pays ₹12,00,000; the 26.7% gap is the wedge. The primary negative (review Q3): the higher the wedge, the fewer jobs employers create and the fewer people find it worthwhile to work.

Compliance and Implications

What compliance is for, what must be disclosed, how to handle conflicts of interest, and why due diligence is your best defence

Compliance feels like paperwork, but it is the mechanism by which the public's trust in financial services is rebuilt. Disclose fully, avoid conflicts where you can, document everything.

5.1 What compliance is

Core idea

Compliance activities arise from regulators' mission to protect the public. They ensure policies, procedures and controls exist throughout the financial system to support its integrity. Advisors, as financial intermediaries (the "middlemen" between product manufacturers and purchasers), should treat compliance as a professional obligation and as part of restoring public trust, not as a distraction from advice.

Example

A bank relationship manager is required to meet targets on the bank's own ULIP. A client wants a long-term equity allocation. Step 1: identify the conflict (the target). Step 2: avoid if possible (in a bank-employee role this cannot be fully avoided). Step 3: disclose plainly: "I am employed by the bank and my recommendations are limited to its products; a lower-cost index fund elsewhere may serve your goal better." The client can then accept or walk away. Burying that sentence on page 14 of a brochure would be disclosure that obscures, which the textbook says is not enough.

5.4 Due diligence

Core idea

Due diligence is the "measure of prudence, responsibility and diligence expected from, and ordinarily exercised by, a reasonable and prudent person under the circumstances". For advisors it is the checking process after advice is given: systematically verify the accuracy of the client's information; ensure relevant legislation has been complied with; ensure full disclosure was made; and keep a full record of conversations and the justification for decisions in the client file.

Clients are becoming more litigious as they become better educated about finance and their rights. Two court cases in the text make the point:

CaseLesson
Swansson (plaintiff) v Harrison (advisor) and othersThe losing advisor's own conclusion: keep meticulous notes; document all discussions with clients and prospects; get everything signed off; keep a paper trail for everything
Commonwealth Financial Planning Ltd v Couper (NSWCA 444, 2013)The advisor's defence was severely compromised by poor record keeping and ignorance of his obligations under the territory's insurance law. The judge observed that many advisors haven't known their obligations, and cases like this are making them aware

India anchor

SEBI IA Regulations require you to maintain records (KYC, risk profile, suitability assessment, rationale for each recommendation, client agreement, and physical or electronic records of client communications) for at least five years, and to have an annual compliance audit. That is due diligence made mandatory. For your own practice, a written rationale attached to every recommendation is the cheapest litigation insurance you can buy.

Exam traps for Chapter 5

  • Compensation disclosure is about the source of pay, not the advisor's total income.
  • Conflicts: avoid first, disclose second. Disclosure that obscures is not compliance.
  • Principle 2 = Integrity; Principle 4 = Fairness (which contains "disclose and manage conflicts"). Principle 1 (client first) was Chapter 2.
  • Due diligence is a post-advice checking process in the textbook's framing, and its centrepiece is the client file.

Chapter 5 review questions

1. What is a financial intermediary and how does being one relate to compliance?Review Q1

An intermediary is a firm or individual serving as the liaison ("middleman") between financial product manufacturers and purchasers. Compliance is intrinsic to intermediaries because they must have systems and processes in place to comply with all applicable laws, codes of conduct and standards of good practice, in order to protect investors and to reduce their own risk of legal or regulatory sanction, financial loss or reputational damage.

2. In general, what should the advisor disclose, and why?Review Q2

Name, organisation and contact details; services provided and by whom, with qualifications; methods of analysis, strategies and risk of loss; disciplinary actions and licences; fees and expenses; compensation (source, not amount) and third-party payments; agency relationships and affiliations including brokerage agreements; other business arrangements; code of ethics; supervision; conflicts of interest; anything else relevant. Why: the client needs essential information to make an informed decision; disclosures must not be misleading, deceptive or confusing; the principle is to clarify rather than obscure.

3. How does the advisor's relationship with an employer potentially cause a conflict of interest?Review Q3

When the advisor is employed by a product provider, the employer requires the advisor to use its products; the advisor has limited or no discretion over the employer's policies. The employer's products may be the best available, but products from other firms may be better for the client. The advisor must disclose this limitation so the client can accept it or choose another advisor.

Anti-Money Laundering

What laundering is, the global rulebook (FATF, World Bank, Basel), how to detect it, and how an advisor complies through customer due diligence

Money laundering is making dirty money look clean. Advisors are one of the doors it comes through, so the law makes you responsible for knowing who your client is, who really owns the money, and where it came from.

Learning objectives 6-1 to 6-6: understand money laundering, its purpose and practice; identify global AML laws; avoid laundering personally and by clients; comply with AML regulation; assess risks to the financial system; assess case histories.

6.2 What money laundering is

Core idea

Money laundering is the practice of making money gained through illegal activity appear legal. FATF's version: the processing of criminal proceeds to disguise their illegal origin and legitimise the ill-gotten gains of crime. Usable description for the exam: illegal activities produce income, which is disguised to conceal its origin; the laundered money is then used in the mainstream economy for all purposes, including funding terrorism. Criminals profit; society is harmed.

The UN conventions define the offence in three limbs; they are broad on purpose so that almost any handling of dirty money is caught:

LimbConductEveryday example
1. Conversion or transferMoving or changing property known to be from an offence, to conceal its origin or help someone evade the consequencesWiring drug cash to an overseas account
2. Concealment or disguiseHiding the true nature, source, location, disposition, movement, rights over or ownership of such propertyHolding assets in a shell company with a nominee director
3. Acquisition, possession or useReceiving or using property, knowing at the time of receipt that it came from an offenceAccepting a "gift" of a flat bought with bribe money

Terrorism has no universally accepted definition (political and religious sensitivities), and most regulations don't define it either. The working consensus: acts intended to cause death or serious bodily injury to people not taking part in armed conflict, designed to intimidate a population or compel a government. Funds used for such purposes fall within money laundering's criteria. Scale: FATF estimates roughly 2.7% of world GDP is laundered; the true figure is unknowable but "exceptionally large".

6.3 The three stages of laundering

Worked example

A smuggler holds ₹3 crore in cash. Placement: over three months, associates deposit ₹9 lakh at a time across 35 accounts in different banks, each below the ₹10 lakh cash-transaction reporting threshold. Layering: the deposits buy units in liquid funds, which are redeemed and wired to a trading company in Dubai as "consultancy fees", then on to a Singapore account. Integration: the Singapore company "invests" in a Hyderabad real estate project, and the smuggler's family receives a legitimate-looking flat. Every stage used a financial intermediary; any one of them asking "where did this come from?" could have broken the chain.

Why it matters beyond the crime itself: financial services run on public trust and a perception of high legal, professional and ethical standards. A bank or advisor through whom money is laundered risks becoming part of the criminal enterprise, or being seen that way, which damages its reputation and can undermine a territory's whole economic environment.

Knew, should have known, chose not to know. All three can be liable. "I didn't ask" is not a defence.

6.6 Detecting money laundering

Laundering cannot happen without financial institutions, certain businesses and certain persons, so the international community regulates entry into the industry:

  • Licensing and authorisation to engage in business.
  • Fit-and-proper evaluation of directors and senior managers (integrity, expertise, experience).
  • Prohibition on directors and managers with criminal records or adverse regulatory findings.
  • Prohibition on ownership or control by those with criminal records.

Basel: Know Your Customer

The Basel Committee (supervisors and central banks of Belgium, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Sweden, Switzerland, UK, US) distilled the CDD guidelines into a single rule: Know Your Customer. Supervisory principles that flow from its Core Principles:

  • Supervisors can review and reject significant ownership changes and set minimum capital.
  • Inter-organisational lending and transactions must be at arm's length.
  • Banks must have a comprehensive risk management process.
  • Strict KYC rules that promote high ethical and professional standards.
  • Independent validation of supervisory information (on-site exams or external auditors).
  • Global consolidated supervision.
  • Local operations of foreign banks held to the same standards as domestic institutions.

Supervision therefore extends to cross-border operations, and advisors must protect and oversee any cross-border relationships they have. Who counts as a customer (Basel, provision 66):

  1. A person or entity who maintains an account, or on whose behalf one is maintained (beneficial owners).
  2. Beneficiaries of transactions conducted by professional intermediaries (agents, accountants, lawyers).
  3. A person or entity connected with a financial transaction who can pose a significant risk to the bank.

6.7 Complying: standard and enhanced CDD

Core idea

The crucial question is whether the customer is acting on their own behalf or whether there is a beneficial owner not visible in the documents. When in doubt, do due diligence. This is hardest with tiered ownership (one corporation owning or controlling others): keep asking "who is the real parent owner?"

Advisors need clear customer acceptance and identification procedures. Independent advisors must build these themselves; employed advisors must comply with the institution's. Not every client needs deep investigation, but firms should have specific guidance for high-risk profiles, using standard risk indicators: personal background, territory of origin, high-profile positions, linked accounts, nature of business activities.

SituationExtra precautions
Cross-border servicesLearn the respondent bank's policies and procedures; have it accurately identify the customer
Non-face-to-face customers (internet, phone)Especially hard to identify to a risk-mitigating degree; no simple solution; senior management should be involved in deciding whether to enter the arrangement
Independent advisorMust determine and comply with the AML law of the home territory and every territory in which they do business

Text example 2: what basic information should an advisor get and keep from a client to support AML?

Reasonable verification of identity and of the nature of transactions, recorded in the AML portion of the client file. For an organisation: name and legal form, address, names of directors, principal owners or beneficiaries, provisions regulating the power to bind it, agents acting for it, account number. For insurance: financial assessment, need analysis, payment method, benefit description, copies of ID documents, post-sale records, claim settlements. For a higher-risk client, add the enhanced items: background, country of origin, source of wealth, links to high-profile persons, linked accounts, purpose of business.

Caveats to the risk-based approach

The purpose of AML rules is to mitigate the risk of laundering and terrorist financing; the purpose of CDD is to help you judge risk, not to add a layer of paperwork. Most clients are not launderers. Policies must not exclude the socially disadvantaged or restrict the public's access to financial services, and everyone must respect individual rights including privacy. Money laundering is a fact of life; every advisor has an individual responsibility to take all reasonable measures, and prudent procedures help even if the problem is never fully solved.

Chapter 6 case studies

Case Study I — The life insurance purchase (Mrs T, Mr B, Company C)Case

Facts. Mrs T, a teacher, buys a life policy with a small initial premium through Mr B, an agent of Insurer C and her cousin. Two days later Company C pays an additional premium of over $540,000 on her behalf. A month later she cancels and the refund goes to three accounts: Mr MD (managing director of Company C) $240,000; Ms N (his niece) $150,000; Mr U $150,000. All three move the money onward to other banks.

1. Concerns? Almost everything: a premium wildly out of line with a teacher's income and with the tiny initial premium; the premium paid by the insurer itself on her behalf; early cancellation (a classic use of insurance products as a laundering vehicle: pay in, cancel, receive a "clean" refund cheque); refund split to third parties unrelated to the policyholder, one of whom is the insurer's own managing director; immediate onward transfers (layering); a family relationship between agent and client that weakens independence.

2. What the agent should have done. Standard CDD at inception: verify identity, financial assessment and need analysis (why does a teacher need cover funded by $540,000?), source of funds, purpose of the policy. The mismatch alone should have triggered an internal escalation or suspicious-transaction report, and a refusal to process the additional premium without explanation.

3. Customer identification needed. Identity documents; financial assessment; need analysis; payment method details; benefit description; source of funds for the large premium; identity and relationship of any third party paying or receiving money; post-sale records including the cancellation and refund instructions.

4. Liability? Yes, potentially. On the "should have known" standard a reasonable agent would have recognised the red flags; on the "wilfully ignorant" standard a cousin who asked no questions is exposed. Afterwards: the money was linked to fuel smuggling; accounts were blocked and the case sent for prosecution.

Case Study II — The intermediary broker ($250,000 in three cash instalments)Case

Facts. A person invests $250,000 by handing a broker three cash instalments. The broker does not report the cash, deposits it into his own bank account (the bank is not suspicious because the broker is known to be connected to a local financial firm), then pays the investment company with three cheques from his own account, so the company sees nothing unusual.

1. Red flags. Large cash; split into three instalments (structuring, i.e. placement); use of the broker's personal account as a pass-through to break the audit trail (layering); no reporting; the investment company receives cheques from the broker, not the investor, so it cannot do CDD on the real customer.

2. Should the bank have acted differently? Yes. Three sizeable cash deposits into a broker's personal account warranted CDD: purpose of the deposits, source of funds, whether the broker was acting for a third party (beneficial owner). Familiarity with the depositor is not a substitute for scrutiny; the textbook's point is that ongoing due diligence means checking transactions against what you know of the customer's business.

3. Is the broker culpable? Yes. He accepted unexplained cash, failed to report it, and deliberately disguised its source by routing it through his own account: conversion/transfer and concealment, the first two limbs of the definition, with at least "should have known" intent.

4. Bank or firm liable? The bank is exposed for failing CDD and reporting duties on the cash deposits; the investment firm less so, since it was deceived, but it should still have asked who the beneficial owner was when a broker paid on someone else's behalf. Afterwards: the depositor was arrested for drug trafficking.

Case Study III — The offshore investment scheme (Mr A, Mr B, Mr C; $140 million)Case

Facts. Mr A runs, through an offshore company, a scheme promising high returns from government-backed securities and raises about $140 million, mostly from small investors. Some funds are paid out through accounts of another offshore company headed by Mr B and Mr C. Mr B helps Mr C reallocate investor funds inappropriately. Mr B has suspicions about the funds' origin but decides not to investigate. The scheme closes; investors recover nothing.

1. Illegal activities? Fraud (the scheme itself, a predicate offence on the FATF list), misappropriation of investor funds, and money laundering through the second offshore company's accounts (conversion/transfer and concealment).

2. Who is guilty? Mr A (operated the fraud) and Mr C (misappropriated funds) were convicted of fraud and imprisoned. Mr B was found civilly liable: the court held he should have known, as a reasonable person, that funds were being misappropriated and should have alerted the authorities; he was ordered to repay investors.

3. Should Mr B have acted differently? Why? Yes. Having suspicions and choosing not to investigate is the "wilfully ignorant" state of mind. The correct actions: conduct enhanced due diligence on the source of funds and the purpose of the transfers, refuse to process the reallocations, and report the suspicion to the relevant authority. Suspicion creates a duty to act; deliberate blindness is treated as culpability.

1. What is a usable description of money laundering?Review Q1

2. What are the activity categories that comprise money laundering?Review Q2

3. According to FATF, what are the four broad measures that come under areas for supervision?Review Q3

(1) Identify the customer and verify identity using reliable, independent sources. (2) Identify the beneficial owner and take reasonable measures to verify identity, including understanding the ownership and control structure of legal persons and arrangements. Self-test

Fifteen exam-style questions. Each answer explains why the wrong options are wrong.

1. Which of the following is not one of the IMF's three-way classification of financial regulation?Ch 1

A. Economic

B. Safety

C. Conduct

D. Information

Answer: C

The IMF (White, 1999) framework is Economic, Safety and Information. "Conduct" is a real regulatory theme (MiFID II is largely conduct-of-business regulation) but it is not one of the three classes; in this framework, conduct rules sit within Safety (corporate governance and securities regulation, including fiduciary duties on intermediaries) and Information.

2. Under prudential regulation, when a financial institution's capital falls below the regulatory minimum, the appropriate response is:Ch 1

A. Reduce reserve requirements so it can lend more

B. Increase scrutiny and tighten restrictions; if insolvent, dispose of it promptly

C. Wait for the next annual audit

D. Guarantee all its liabilities

Answer: B

This is prompt corrective action. A confuses a monetary policy tool with prudential regulation (and would make a weak bank riskier). C is the opposite of "prompt". D describes an improvised bailout, which the textbook argues against in favour of explicit, risk-priced deposit insurance and removal of owners and managers.

3. IOSCO's Objectives and Principles of Securities Regulation have been endorsed by:Ch 1

A. The IAIS and EIOPA

B. The G20 and the Financial Stability Board

C. The IMF and the World Bank

D. The United Nations

Answer: B

Endorsement comes from the G20 and the FSB. C is a near-miss: the IMF and World Bank use the principles as the basis for FSAP assessments, which is not the same as endorsing them. A lists other standard setters; D is not mentioned in the text.

4. Under the ESMA Guidelines, a staff member who lacks the appropriate qualification or experience:Ch 1

A. May not interact with clients at all

B. May provide services under supervision for up to four years, with the supervisor responsible and signing off suitability reports

C. May provide information but not advice indefinitely

D. Must pass the CFP examination within one year

Answer: B

V.IV allows supervised provision of services for a period not exceeding four years (or shorter if the Competent Authority requires). A is too strict: supervised client contact is permitted. C misreads the V.II/V.III distinction; information staff also need qualification and experience. D invents a requirement; ESMA speaks of "an appropriate qualification", not any specific credential.

5. Two investments are equally suitable for a client and deliver the same benefits; one carries a lower cost. Under which standard must the advisor recommend the lower-cost option?Ch 2

A. Suitability standard only

B. Fiduciary standard only

C. Both standards

D. Neither; cost is irrelevant to standards of care

Answer: B

The textbook is explicit: suitability does not require choosing the lower-cost option; a fiduciary, all else being equal, must. C over-reads suitability; A reverses the standards; D is contradicted by the ERISA attorney's list, which includes whether fees are reasonable.

6. The three basic requirements of being a fiduciary are:Ch 2

A. Fair, transparent, efficient

B. Trust, loyalty, disclosure

C. Integrity, fairness, competence

D. Identify, avoid, disclose

Answer: B

A is (three of) MiFID's four descriptors of advice (fair, transparent, efficient, integrated). C mixes FPSB Code principles. D is the three-step approach to conflicts of interest from Chapter 5. Only B is the fiduciary triad.

7. A central bank wants to reduce inflation. Which combination is consistent?Ch 3

A. Buy government bonds, cut the discount rate, lower reserve requirements

B. Sell government bonds, raise the discount rate, raise reserve requirements

C. Sell government bonds, cut the discount rate, lower reserve requirements

D. Buy government bonds, raise the discount rate, raise reserve requirements

Answer: B

Reducing inflation means tightening: drain money (sell bonds), make borrowing dearer (raise the rate), make banks hold more back (raise reserves). A is the full easing set. C and D mix directions, which a central bank would not do deliberately.

8. Fiscal drag is best described as:Ch 3

A. A one-off cut in government spending

B. A progressive tax system automatically taking a larger share as incomes rise

C. The central bank raising reserve requirements

D. Lowering tax rates to increase disposable income

Answer: B

Fiscal drag is one of the two automatic stabilisers. A is discretionary fiscal policy. C is monetary policy. D is the other stabiliser, fiscal boost.

9. Which of the following is a lagging economic indicator?Ch 3

A. Building permits for new private homes

B. Interest rate spread between 10-year bonds and the central bank rate

C. Average duration of unemployment

D. Manufacturers' new orders for consumer goods

Answer: C

Duration of unemployment lengthens only after a downturn is well established, so it confirms rather than predicts. A, B and D are all leading indicators: permits predict building, the yield spread predicts growth or contraction, new orders predict production.

10. Which economist argued that central banks should focus on controlling the rate of growth of the money supply?Ch 3

A. John Maynard Keynes

B. Adam Smith

C. Milton Friedman

D. A. W. Phillips

Answer: C

This is monetarism, Friedman's contribution, standing apart from both classical and Keynesian camps. Keynes favoured government investment to lift aggregate demand; Smith favoured leaving markets alone (invisible hand); Phillips mapped the inflation–unemployment trade-off.

11. Territory X produces both phones and computers more efficiently than Territory Y, yet imports computers from Y. The economic reason is:Ch 4

A. Absolute advantage

B. Comparative advantage (opportunity cost)

C. Economies of scale

D. A trade deficit

Answer: B

X is better at both, so absolute advantage (A) cannot explain importing. The explanation is opportunity cost: making computers would divert resources from phones, where X's edge is largest. C would apply if Y had already made the large fixed investment and X had not. D is a result of trade, not a reason for it.

12. The tax wedge's primary potential negative is that it:Ch 4

A. Increases inflation

B. Discourages employment by widening the gap between labour cost and take-home pay

C. Reduces the government's tax revenue

D. Lowers the gross replacement rate in retirement

Answer: B

The wedge measures how much income taxes and social contributions discourage employment; the textbook's answer is fewer jobs and fewer people seeking them. A is not the mechanism. C is backwards (the wedge is revenue). D is a retirement-policy concept unrelated to the wedge definition.

13. Regarding advisor compensation, disclosure rules typically require the advisor to disclose:Ch 5

A. Total personal income

B. The source of compensation and what the relationship will cost the client

C. Only fees charged directly by the advisor, not product-provider fees

D. Nothing, if the advisor is fee-only

Answer: B

The client has a right to know how the advisor is paid and what the relationship will cost, including product-provider fees; the advisor keeps a right to privacy over total income. A goes too far; C omits provider fees, which must be disclosed; D ignores that fee-only advisors still disclose fees and their basis.

14. A launderer deposits drug cash in amounts just under the reporting threshold across many bank accounts. This is which stage?Ch 6

A. Placement

B. Layering

C. Integration

D. Structuring is not part of the three-stage model

Answer: A

Breaking large cash into inconspicuous deposits is the textbook's own example of placement. Layering (B) is the subsequent movement through accounts and instruments to distance funds from their source. Integration (C) is the re-entry into the legitimate economy via real estate or businesses. D is wrong: structuring is the classic placement technique.

15. Which body has specified due diligence requirements for its sector that mirror the FATF 40 Recommendations, with extra life-insurance guidance?Ch 6

A. IOSCO

B. IAIS

C. ESMA

D. The World Bank

Answer: B

The textbook states that IOSCO has not specified due diligence requirements, while the IAIS has, and its recommendations mirror the 40 Recommendations with additional life-insurance guidance. ESMA's guidelines concern knowledge and competence, not AML due diligence. The World Bank publishes the eight legal-system requirements and the AML reference guide, not sector due diligence.

Wrap-up

The course moves from the outside in: what regulation is for and who writes it (Chapter 1); the standard of care it expects, ideally fiduciary whether or not the law says so (Chapter 2); the economic weather every plan lives in (Chapter 3); the social and political climate that changes benefits, taxes and pensions (Chapter 4); the compliance mechanics of disclosure, conflicts and due diligence (Chapter 5); and the one area where an advisor can become a criminal by not asking questions (Chapter 6).
